# Professor O — A Reading of Walk This Way (Draft 04)

A developmental reading for Timothy J. Smith, prepared June 7, 2026. This is a close editorial reading for craft growth, not a publication assessment. I read the whole manuscript in sequence before I let myself say a word about any part of it. I am sitting in the Ondaatje chair, but I have asked Lehane, Dickey, King, and Robinson to lean in wherever their ear is sharper than mine. No scores. No flattery. Where I praise, treat it as diagnosis that happened to come out favorable.

Throughout I use your own vocabulary — Architecture, Brick, Scalpel, Hose — and I try to write the notes the way the book wants to be written: by image, by the gap, by the thing left unnamed.

---

## The whole book first

Here is the spine, and it is not the one the surface plot advertises. The surface says: two gifted boys, divided by class and town and adults, fail to become the band they should have been. True enough. But that is the plot, not the spine. The spine is this: *every adult in this book has already buried the thing they loved most, and the burying is what they hand down.* Dan Johnson plays guitar in the dark after Grace sleeps and seals it in a toolbox. Dr. Marsh closed a practice-room Steinway in 1960 and never reopened it for himself. Eileen Marsh turned nine canvases to the wall and laid her palette knife down in a Cambridge kitchen and never picked it up. Bob Wentworth folded a Jordan Hall program into a drawer and blew one sagging F into an empty room. Maxanne traded her ear for a blazer. The boys are not the first to renounce. They are the latest in a chain of loving, frightened people who each decided the thing they loved was a luxury they could not afford — and then called the renunciation *care*. "It's not giving up," Nick says. "It's choosing the people who already chose us." That sentence is the book's true subject: inheritance is the renunciation, not the gift. That is honest, and it is moving, and it is the deepest thing you have built here. Robinson would recognize it instantly — the theology of a sacrifice nobody in the family will name, present-action (a closing radio station) opening a backward chamber (a closing piano lid). Trust it more than you trust the Battle.

Now the four levels of Loss of Innocence, judged honestly, layer by layer.

The personal level — Nick and Jackie — is earned, and it is the book's strongest brick. The friendship is shown, not asserted: the milk cartons in the cafeteria, the headphones split between two ears, the fifth bar where the riff and the chord lock, Patty's number torn in half so they have to talk to each other. The erosion is shown too, and beautifully: "forty feet of linoleum," the bridge, the doors hinged at different walls. When this layer fails it is only because the frame keeps explaining what the bricks already proved.

The institutional level — WBCN, Maxanne, the sellout — is the most *told* and the least *shown*. The Kowalski meeting is a good scene strangled by its own thesis statements ("the day WBCN officially became a business instead of a movement"). The tie-clause is a strong piece of architecture — a buried event with a long denouement, pure Lehane — but Maxanne narrates its meaning so relentlessly that the reader never gets to discover the betrayal; she is handed the verdict pre-chewed. This layer is real but it is performed at the lectern.

The cultural level — rock versus disco, authenticity, the death of discovery — lives almost entirely in the frame, and it is where the book most often confuses *information* for *innocence lost*. The "Kung Fu Fighting" cafeteria, the Brickyard, "HI MOM" — these work as image. But the frame's editorializing ("we chose noise over harmony," "America designs its suburbs") states the culture-thesis instead of letting the Cavicchio pigs and the Dump and the four quarters carry it, which they already do.

The societal/class level — Hop Brook, the four quarters, the town's prejudice — is, after the friendship, your second-strongest layer, and it is almost all Brick, almost all shown. Chapter 4 and Chapter 14 are the load-bearing walls of the whole novel. Grace at the PTA, Dr. Taub's decaf, the $500 that reads "a Friday shift on one side, a receipt on the other" — this is class rendered as physical fact, not lecture. Dickey would stand up for these chapters. They do the work with their hands.

So: the layers do build, and three of the four are earned on the page. The fragile one is the institutional/cultural layer, and it is fragile for one reason — the show-versus-tell paradox at the center of your design.

You named the paradox yourself, and it is the right one: Maxanne is a DJ. Her literal job is to tell the story aloud. The frame *must* tell. But here is the distinction the draft has not yet made — there is a difference between a narrator who *tells the story* and a narrator who *tells you what the story means.* The great radio voice compresses, gestures, withholds, and trusts the gap. Yours currently interprets. Again and again the frame chapter narrates the theme of the brick chapter that follows or precedes it: "I sold tickets to watch innocence die. I turned tragedy into entertainment. And then I called it opportunity." That is the second sentence after the line — the explanation you are always tempted to add and should cut. My recommendation, and it is the single highest-leverage move in the book: let Maxanne earn her telling through voice, fragment, and object, and forbid her from glossing the meaning of scenes the reader is about to witness or just witnessed. She can hold the cassette. She can say its label. She can say "Stay with me." She must not tell you it weighs like a bird's hollow bones — wait, she can, that image is hers and it is good — but she must not then tell you "it's the weight of something that should have flown." Trust the bird. Cut the gloss. The frame should point at the dark and go quiet, the way she says radio silence used to terrify program directors. She knows the lesson. The prose hasn't taken it yet.

The "best band that never was" thesis is the book's title-level promise, and right now the ending breaks the promise to keep it. More on that below. But know that the phrase only lands if the book has the nerve to stay quiet — the Neighborhoods mirror works precisely because it is a fact stated once and left alone (the YouTube comment does it perfectly: "The Neighborhoods opened for the Cars at the Rat in '79 and these guys never even made the card. This is criminal."). That is found-language doing in one line what three frame monologues strain to do.

Strongest structural moves: the mirror-object architecture (Dan's closed toolbox / Maxanne's carried reel — a private repository sealed by grief and a private repository sealed by guilt, both finally opened in Act III); the hand motif running under everything (Marsh's octave-reaching pinky, Eileen's brush hand, Dan's and Nick's calluses) — this is your not-naming discipline at its best, a whole theme carried by fingers and never announced; the closed-lid rhyme (toolbox, fallboard, casket). Most fragile moves: the omniscient brick chapters inside a first-person frame (Maxanne cannot know Eileen alone in the painting room), which the book only legitimizes on the very last page; and the triumphal viral ending, which trades elegy for vindication.

## The 41 chapters

### Act I — Toys in the Attic (The Frequency)

### Chapter 1 — "Wish You Were Here" (Act I)

The overture. It seats us in the 2009 booth, names the four-hour clock, and promises the story. The voice has real warmth — "the same Sony MDR-7506s I've worn since 1971, held together now with electrical tape and pure stubbornness" is a perfect object, character and era and grief in one prop. The problem is that the chapter does the thing the whole frame does, only first and worst: it tells you the meaning before you have any story to attach it to. "Tonight is about the night I watched two potential stars die," "got caught in the crossfire of America's class war," "we chose division over music" — these are theses, and they pre-spoil the ache the bricks are built to deliver.

The move — cut every interpretive sentence and let the objects carry the freight. Keep the cassette, the bourbon, the Tyler note. Lose "but of course it was deeper than rock, much deeper. It always is." Ondaatje's rule: replace one theme sentence with one object and one motion. You already have the object; trust it and stop narrating its significance.

### Chapter 2 — "Draw the Line" (Act I)

First brick, and a strong one. Maxanne to the garage; the sound felt "through the soles of Maxanne's boots and into her chest" before it's heard. The pegboard with "an empty slot where the 9/16ths used to live" plants the toolbox and the dead father in a single withheld detail — this is the not-naming working exactly right. Dickey would love that the music arrives as a physical blow before it arrives as information.

The move — watch the late-paragraph explainers ("Four boys with nothing to lose"; "the only difference between him and you is somebody opened a door"). The garage already said it. Let Maxanne's last gesture be the image of the boys "holding instruments the way drowning people hold wreckage" and stop there; the sentence after dilutes the one before.

### Chapter 3 — "Killer Queen" (Act I)

Jackie's gilded cage, and the Bohemian-Rhapsody recital detonation. The room "where you could hear pearls clutching themselves" is Lehane-funny in the best way — comedy carrying class. And the father's hand: "pinky and ring finger curling inward once against his thumb — an unconscious reach." That hand is the book's whole thesis in one tendon, and you never name it. Keep doing exactly that.

The move — "That was rock and roll... That was your son showing you who he really is" is Maxanne telling the room (and us) the meaning of what we just watched. Cut it or shrink it; the silence and the father's curling hand already said it louder. Let her stand up because someone has to, and sit back down.

### Chapter 4 — "More Than a Feeling" (Act I)

The town as character — water and waste, four quarters, the stone walls, the Dump, the Cavicchio pigs, the diamond crossing. This is the architectural keystone of the class layer and some of the best sustained prose in the book. "nobody used official names for places that smelled like truth." The geography *is* the theme; you never have to argue class because you have built it out of methane and fieldstone and mini-bike exhaust.

The move — it is long and it is almost all exposition with no human in it. Consider splitting (Skill 9): let half of this geography arrive *through* Nick's and Jackie's bodies in later chapters rather than front-loaded as a gazetteer. At minimum, plant the southeast-corner girl (Patty) earlier and more sharply so the chapter ends on a person, not a survey.

### Chapter 5 — "School's Out" (Act I)

The origin of the friendship, 1974, the cafeteria, the belt buckles (QUEEN / AEROSMITH). Pure Brick and almost flawless — the argument-as-courtship is shown entirely through dialogue and the comedy is doing the emotional work. "Decent. That's like saying the Grand Canyon is a pretty nice hole." King would point at this chapter as proof you can render working-class and professional-class boyhood in the same room without tipping the scale.

The move — almost none needed. The last lines ("He could. He would.") are a small telling flourish on an otherwise shown chapter; you could end on Jackie planning which album to bring and lose nothing. Restraint is the only note.

### Chapter 6 — "With a Little Help From My Friends" (Act I)

The friendship deepens: cassettes, the conservation land, the first locked jam ("Nick's guitar was the engine; Jackie's was the wingspan"), Grace recognizing Jackie's manners, Dan's "the kid can play." The engine/wingspan image is so good you reuse it — careful, that is becoming a signature phrase (see note on tics below). Doug Malone gives the friendship a cost, which earns it.

The move — the engine/wingspan metaphor appears here, in Ch. 9, and Ch. 10. Pick one occurrence — this one, where it's born — and vary or cut the others, or it becomes a tic that announces itself.

### Chapter 7 — "Bohemian Rhapsody" (Act I)

The Marsh house at sixty-eight degrees. The breakfast table measured with a ruler, the father's octave-hand on the cloth, the attic sheet music, Elizabeth. "You practice Queen like you're hiding a body." This is your excavation chamber for Jackie, and it is largely earned through objects and temperature. The sixty-eight-degrees motif is a real piece of architecture.

The move — "Classical earned his father's approval. Rock earned his breath. He couldn't have both." This is a hose line, and it is good — but you ring it twice in the book (again in Ch. 26). Stop at the hose, and ring it once. Robinson's patience says: a truth stated once and left alone outlasts a truth repeated.

### Chapter 8 — "Mama Kin" (Act I)

The Johnson house and Dan's secret guitar through the floor. The crown molding under four coats of landlord white — Grace's buried gentility — mirrors the Marsh house's sealed piano, and you never connect them aloud. That restraint is the book at its best. "Some things you heard through walls were like prayers — not meant for you."

The move — this chapter and Ch. 15 both carry the toolbox/secret-music reveal. Make sure Ch. 8 only *hears the music through the wall* and withholds the toolbox contents entirely, so Ch. 15's discovery is not pre-spent. Right now the architecture is sound; just guard the withheld fact like the asset it is.

### Chapter 9 — "Just What I Needed" (Act I)

Frame chapter: Maxanne narrates the decision to enter the 1975 talent show. The "nobody knows it except us" hinge is a genuine insight. But this is the clearest case of the frame previewing a brick: Maxanne tells the whole talent-show outcome here, and then Chapter 10 shows it. Two chapters, one event, the first draining suspense from the second.

The move — Lehane would stop you here: you are telling the game's final score in the pre-game show. Either compress this to a few lines of voice (the decision, the "nobody knows it except us," then cut to the show) or cut the chapter and fold its one true insight into Ch. 11. Do not let the frame spoil its own brick.

### Chapter 10 — "Dream On" (Act I)

The talent show, shown. This is a set piece and it mostly earns itself — the mashup rendered bar by bar, the three-second silence, Dr. Marsh's hand opening "once in his lap... and then it had closed again." When you stay in the room and on the hands, you are unbeatable.

The move — the chapter occasionally narrates the crowd's meaning ("every parent in that gymnasium knew what they had seen") in the same breath as showing it. Trust the woman who stops fanning herself with her program; she is the whole crowd. Cut the summary sentences and keep the single bodies.

### Chapter 11 — "Radio, Radio" (Act I)

Maxanne's 1973 origin — the Brickyard, Aerosmith, the "HI MOM" Springsteen interview. The best frame chapter, because here she is *telling her own story*, which a DJ is authorized to do, rather than glossing the boys'. "the sound that came off that stage was alive the way a downed power line is alive." That is your narrator at full strength.

The move — the closing turn ("In 1973... my first instinct was to share them. In 1979... my first instinct was to figure out how to use them") is strong but over-explained across several paragraphs. Compress the self-indictment to its sharpest two sentences. The insight is real; the repetition softens it.

### Chapter 12 — "Summer Breeze" (Act I)

Camp Elbanobscot, Patty arrives, the a cappella triad "with a physical click, like a door latch falling home." The dock, the whip-poor-will, the tractor-snore boy — sensory specificity at its richest. The nighttime bunk confession (sheet music in the attic / guitar in the toolbox) is one of the truest two-hander scenes in the book.

The move — Patty's introduction leans on the pig-farm smell as her defining tag, and the book will lean on it many more times. Plant it once here, hard, and then trust it; you do not need to re-establish "she smelled faintly of pig, she never apologized" every chapter she appears in. Watch that repetition starting now.

### Chapter 13 — "Breakdown" (Act I)

The forty feet of cafeteria linoleum — the friendship's quiet fracture. Architecturally this may be the most important Brick in Act I and it is rendered with great control: "so thin you step over it without looking down... until years later, when the crack has become a canyon." The "scratch on a record, the needle jumping over the part it couldn't play" image (echoed in Ch. 28) is exactly right.

The move — the final paragraph about leaves changing is lovely but it explains the metaphor it just made ("changing so slowly that if you watched them every day you'd swear nothing was different"). One image of the leaves; cut the gloss on what the leaves mean. The canyon line already carried it.

### Chapter 14 — "Baba O'Riley" (Act I)

The four-quarters day-in-the-life mosaic — auto shop, glass corridor, the Rampers' platform, Grace's shift at Friendly's. This is a triumph of the witnessed detail; Lenny Henderson hiding Goodman's Hill Road from the Rats is a whole novel in a paragraph. The chapter proves class without a syllable of argument. This is the book you are actually writing.

The move — it is doing two or three architectural jobs at full altitude (Nick's world, the Rat subculture, Grace's labor) and is very long. Skill 9 applies: consider whether the Friendly's section wants to live closer to Ch. 23, where Grace's diner world pays off, so this chapter can stay the school's social map. Splitting would let each room breathe.

### Chapter 15 — "Landslide" (Act I)

Dan's death and the toolbox discovery — the engine of the whole book. The ordinary morning ("how ordinary it was... nothing in it to grab onto and say here, here is where I should have known") is devastating and true. The handwritten "Sawdust hands and a borrowed name / The house remembers what I became" is found-language done right.

The move — after Grace's "He gave it to you instead. Everything he couldn't let himself have," the chapter keeps explaining the inheritance for several more paragraphs ("Music. The one thing Dan Johnson had that was entirely his own. And now it was Nick's."). That is the hose flooding past the line. Stop at the closed lid: "She couldn't open it again." End the chapter there. King would cut the last three paragraphs without blinking.

### Act II — A Night at the Opera (The Static)

### Chapter 16 — "Running on Empty" (Act II)

Frame chapter anchoring the timeline to Dan's death and delivering "I don't need your pity" — "Four words. That's how friendships end." The voice is strong and the Globe's "paragraph and a misspelled last name" is a perfect class-rage detail. The chapter also states its own thesis repeatedly ("the machinery that lifts kids up is the same machinery that grinds them down").

The move — the John Lennon / "God help Bruce Springsteen" passage is good but it is a borrowed prophecy doing your narrator's interpretive work for her, and it recurs in Ch. 40. Use it once. Here, let the chapter end on "I'll tell you in a minute what walked back down" — that withholding is the right note; the Lennon gloss undercuts it.

### Chapter 17 — "Bridge Over Troubled Water" (Act II)

The bridge — "these boys" — the diamond-plate ringing under the tires, Dr. Marsh reading Nick's callused hand "the way he moved over an X-ray." This is the book's pivot rendered almost entirely in the body: the almost-smile that "some muscle at the corner of his mouth that the body had kept ready for Nick for five years gave up its readiness." Dickey and Ondaatje both live in this chapter. It is one of your best.

The move — protect it by cutting the one explanatory beat: "He didn't decide to step aside. His legs did it before he did." is excellent — but then it explains itself ("The body kept a small book of these measurements"). Keep the legs; trim the book of measurements to a single clause. The action already taught us.

### Chapter 18 — "Rock 'n' Roll Fantasy" (Act II)

The Battle poster goes up; Wentworth's drawer with the photograph and the Jordan Hall program and the dead metronome. Wentworth is the third buried-artist mirror and you reveal him through objects — "A metronome that did not work" — which is the not-naming discipline at its finest. The Xerox-poster paragraph is a small masterclass in physical specificity.

The move — the chapter occasionally over-renders the poster (we get its toner smell, its canted crest, its push-pin holes across several beats). That is the right *instinct* overused; one or two of those details is indelible, five becomes inventory. Scalpel the poster description by half and the survivors get sharper.

### Chapter 19 — "Born to Run" (Act II)

The cafeteria singing duel and the principal's office. "Sit down. Both of you. Principal's office. Now." The competition-as-courtship reads true, and Sargent's KEEFE TECH COUGARS mug is a perfect minor-character object. Comedy and tension balanced well.

The move — the dueling-songs sequence tells us how the room feels ("The silence after was not scattered. It was one silence") right after showing it. You have a real gift for the single-body detail (the lunch lady frozen with tongs); lean on those and cut the choral summaries of crowd emotion. Let the tongs be the room.

### Chapter 20 — "Hotel California" (Act II)

The Marsh dinner and — the chapter's real treasure — Eileen alone in the painting room with nine canvases turned to the wall. This is the fourth buried-artist mirror and the most quietly shattering: "she looked at all of it the way a widow looks at her dead husband's coat closet." Robinson would claim this chapter as her own — a woman written from outside her own walls, theology of renunciation without a church.

The move — two things. First, this is a POV problem in miniature: Maxanne cannot possibly know this scene, and it is the most private interior in the book. It is too good to cut, so the fix is at the frame level (see closing note on licensing the omniscience). Second, the long single paragraph cataloguing Eileen's invisible labor for Richard is powerful but tips toward telling; trust the palette knife "where she had put it down... in the fall of 1959" and let the hand on the easel bar do the rest.

### Chapter 21 — "Another Brick in the Wall" (Act II)

Grace at the emergency PTA meeting. "Fall is when Jackie goes to conservatory. Fall is when my son is fixing cars." The whole class layer pays off in one woman gripping the back of an oak chair in a fryer-smelling uniform. The Star Market, the betting pool at the barber shop, "forty-two to eleven" — class rendered as community fact. Among the strongest chapters in the book.

The move — almost none. The chapter trusts itself. Guard against the one place it nudges ("She did not make a speech of it" — then she rather does). Let her three sentences land and walk her out; the restraint is the power.

### Chapter 22 — "Won't Get Fooled Again" (Act II)

Frame: the Kowalski meeting and the writing of the tie-clause. Architecturally essential — this is the buried event with the long denouement. And the cage imagery is strong. But this is the most over-told chapter in the book: Maxanne narrates her own corruption in thesis after thesis ("That was the moment I became what I'd fought"; "the lock clicks so quiet you don't hear it until you're already inside").

The move — Lehane would gut this to half its length. Show the meeting; let Kowalski's "Make it pay — he said it the way you'd tell a vending machine to deliver the soda" do the indictment (it already does, perfectly). Then let Maxanne write Article 7, Section 3 in near-silence. The reader is smart enough to feel the trap close without being told it's a trap, a loaded gun, an ambush, and a cage in the same chapter. The withheld interpretation is the suspense.

### Chapter 23 — "Working Class Hero" (Act II)

Grace at Friendly's, Dr. Taub and the decaf, "My son has been playing guitar since he was eight." This chapter is Lehane's booth — the diner where working people carry their lives in language they'd never call literary — and it is gorgeous. The Maxwell House jar that becomes, for half a breath, the jar in a study across Hop Brook is your encrypted-grief architecture working without a seam.

The move — the chapter twice reaches for Grace's buried-gentility backstory (the chrome handle "the light off a different door handle... a long time ago") in language that gets misty and abstract just when the diner is most concrete. Keep the concrete (the brown stain on Taub's cardigan she notices) and let the buried past stay a half-beat glimpse, not a paragraph of soft focus.

### Chapter 24 — "Light My Fire" (Act II)

Wentworth's "Take Five," Gould's "Lincoln-Douglas debates," Nick's explosion, and Wentworth's one sagging trumpet F into the empty room. The Brubeck improvisation building and then being killed by an adult's careless metaphor is the institutional/cultural cruelty made flesh — and Wentworth playing the dead F afterward closes his arc without a word of explanation. Superb.

The move — Nick's outburst is a touch on-the-nose ("Between the kids who matter and the kids who don't"; "die quick like him too. That American enough?"). The grief is real but it states the theme aloud. Let him say less and break more — the set-down pick "because if he had not set it down carefully he would have thrown it" is the truer register. Trust the body over the speech.

### Chapter 25 — "Night Moves" (Act II)

Nick's band, the night before — Stephen's "You're the reason any of us started playing," the gear rendered with loving specificity, Grace's hand warming the chrome storm-door handle she can't open. The bandmates' loyalty earns the stakes, and the unopened toolbox is held with discipline.

The move — "She was afraid of what he would do if he lost. She was afraid of what he would do if he won. Both fears were the same fear" is a fine line slightly over-built; the hand on the warming handle already shows the fear. Choose the gesture or the sentence, not both.

### Chapter 26 — "Father and Son" (Act II)

Jackie's band, the night before — Dr. Marsh's "You remind me of Glenn Gould... You've taken his chaos and made it civilized." "Civilized" as the cruelest possible praise is exactly right, and Elizabeth's "he doesn't hear music, Jack. He counts notes" is a hose line. The father's thumb touching middle C without sounding it — the hand motif again, unnamed.

The move — you ring "Classical earned his father's approval. Rock earned his breath. He couldn't have both" here a second time (first in Ch. 7). Cut it here. Let it live once, in Ch. 7, and let Elizabeth's "he counts notes" be this chapter's truth. Two hose lines in one chapter dilute each other.

### Chapter 27 — "Turn the Page" (Act II)

Frame: Maxanne returns to the 1979 Battle as Elektra, confessing the rigged rubric. "I sold tickets to watch innocence die. I turned tragedy into entertainment. And then I called it opportunity." This is the thesis of the entire institutional layer, stated four different ways in one chapter.

The move — this is the single most important place to apply the show-don't-tell discipline to the frame. The confession is more powerful if Maxanne states the *facts* (I wrote the clause; I scored them close; I knew what I was doing) and refuses to interpret them for us. "I didn't kill their friendship. But I gave class warfare a microphone" is your best line in the chapter — keep that one and cut the three that paraphrase it. The reader should be left to render the verdict the narrator keeps rendering for them. That gap is where the guilt actually lives.

### Act III — In Through the Out Door (The Signal)

### Chapter 28 — "Rhiannon" (Act III)

Patty's interiority opens — the pig farm at five-fifteen, "Bacon pays our mortgage," the Elbanobscot flashback, watching the boys erase each other "like a scratch on a record." Patty is the book's witness-conscience, the one margin-dweller who sees the whole board, and she is well-drawn. The decision to enter "because somebody has to play music that isn't a weapon" gives Act III its moral center.

The move — this chapter re-tells the Elbanobscot harmony scene we already lived in Ch. 12, and re-establishes the cafeteria geography we know from Ch. 13/14. Trust the reader's memory: compress the flashback to a sentence of sense-memory and keep Patty in the present. Robinson's patience again — you do not need to re-pour a foundation the reader is already standing on.

### Chapter 29 — "Rock and Roll All Nite" (Act III)

Patty's band at St. Michael's; the hallway where her involuntary hum of "Go Your Own Way" snags Nick mid-stride. The setlist-as-argument ("'Dreams' said: You don't own me, but I remember you") is a clever piece of structure. The hum-as-reflex is a lovely recurring device.

The move — the setlist glosses ("'Landslide' said: Time passes") spell out the emotional plan you are about to dramatize. Lehane would cut the annotations and let the songs do their own talking on the night. Keep Patty's intention private; if she explains her own subtext, the reader stops listening for it.

### Chapter 30 — "Silver Springs" (Act III)

Patty alone in the church with the seven letters — four from Nick, three from Jackie — Nick's cramped hand that crossed out "my dad" three times, Jackie's that "went straight across the page like a prayer... underlined almost once, careful, with a ruler." The two handwritings as two class-selves is your hand motif extended to ink. Quietly excellent.

The move — this chapter and Ch. 29 and Ch. 31 are three consecutive "Patty alone, preparing, humming, not-fixing-them" beats. That is one beat played three times. Consider consolidating: this letters scene is the keeper; much of 29 and 31 could fold into it. As written, Act III's pre-Battle stretch sags because the same emotional note is struck four times before the Battle arrives.

### Chapter 31 — "Thunder Road" (Act III)

Three houses, same hour, the night before — Nick / Jackie / Patty, each lamp out at ten-thirty. The triptych structure is deliberate and the "lamp went out at ten-thirty" refrain across all three is a real Ondaatje move, the gap between scenes doing the rhyming.

The move — the synchronized "ten-thirty" and the repeated unopened-toolbox / unopened-envelope / unwritten-letter beats are powerful once but you have now shown Nick-not-opening-the-toolbox in Ch. 25 and again here, Jackie-not-opening-Juilliard before and here. The triptych works; the *contents* are reruns. Give each room one new fact rather than re-staging the same withholding. The form is right; refresh the bricks inside it.

### Chapter 32 — "Barracuda" (Act III)

Battle night opens — the 1960 auditorium, the eight bands, Chuck Morrison's emcee patter, the crowd self-sorting by class without anyone moving a chair. The room is built with total authority; the comedic band-name roll call ("The Jesters. Detroit Rock City... God help us") is Lehane-funny and lands. Grace and Dr. Marsh planted in their seats, the empty chair beside Grace.

The move — the backstage Nick and Jackie sections each end on the hand-on-the-curtain / hand-on-the-fallboard gesture, which is good, but the chapter tells us they don't look at each other and then tells us again. One clean miss of eye contact through the curtain slit is enough. Otherwise this is a strong Act-opener; mainly guard the repetitions.

### Chapter 33 — "Somebody to Love" (Act III)

Jackie's set. The Yamaha's "key-dip... two millimeters deeper than the Steinway upstairs at home" steadying him with a fact — beautiful, the body finding ground in a flaw. The voice cracking on "find me somebody to love" and his left hand not adjusting is the chapter's truth: the imperfection is the soul. His father unreadable in the dark, "That would have to be all right."

The move — very little. The chapter occasionally explains the music's effect ("It is the reason people cover this song and the reason most people covering it should not") in a slightly essayistic register that steps outside Jackie's POV. Keep us in his hands and his cracking throat; the meta-commentary on the song belongs to a critic, not to the boy playing it.

### Chapter 34 — "Train Kept A-Rollin'" (Act III)

Nick's set. Wentworth's loaned butterscotch Tele and the wordless "Back Friday." Nick carrying the vocal lines on the guitar because "there was no Steven Tyler on the Lincoln-Sudbury stage" — a brilliant constraint, the absence becoming the art. Grace letting go of her purse clasp, her hands going flat in her lap, is the whole chapter's emotional report delivered through one woman's hands.

The move — the secret tremor in his left pinky ("the one he had not told anybody about") is a fine detail but it is told to us rather than felt; let us see the grip tighten and the copper taste before we're told it's a tremor he's been hiding. Dickey's rule: the sentence should do the physical work, and then the reader names the fear, not the narrator.

### Chapter 35 — "Go Your Own Way" (Act III)

Patty's set, barefoot, the full song finally released after seven months of humming. The payoff of the hum-motif is genuinely moving, and her singing "Silver Springs" not for the boys but for "the four women at St. Michael's and the two women in her kitchen" is the inheritance-theme widened to women's lineage — a quiet, true enlargement.

The move — "She saw Nick at the back of the stage-left wing for one beat. She saw Jackie at the back of the stage-right wing for one beat... They were both looking at her." This is the right image, but the chapter then tells us she "did not hold either look," and the symmetry gets a little schematic (stage-left/stage-right, one beat/one beat). Let the staging be slightly less perfectly mirrored so it reads as life, not diagram.

### Chapter 36 — "Stairway to Heaven" (Act III)

The judges' room and the tie. "How do you choose between authenticity and excellence?" / "Maybe you don't." Carter's "That's not judging. That's engineering." / "Yes. It is." The denouement of the buried event — the clause closes its trap in real time — and the dialogue is taut. The signing at the single microphone, "two tides converging," is a strong stage image.

The move — Maxanne explains her own motive aloud to the panel ("I wrote the tie clause because I thought there was a chance... they'd grow up and both be too good to choose between"). This is the confession the frame has already made three times. Let her be more guarded in the room — let the panel (and the reader) infer the rigging from the contract she produces — so the scene dramatizes the manipulation instead of having her narrate it yet again.

### Chapter 37 — "The Boys Are Back in Town" (Act III)

The garage reunion. They try "Walk This Way" angry and "Bohemian Rhapsody" too-careful and fail — "We sound like strangers." / "We *are* strangers." — and then Grace's "Your father would've liked that" turns the silence "from a wall" into "a window," and the D-minor ninth opens the locked room. The tools trembling on the pegboard, the toolbox not moving. This is the emotional reunion and it largely earns its eleven minutes.

The move — the prose tells us the meaning of the silence ("The silence after was different from the silences before. Those had been walls. This one was a window") — a lovely line, but then the playing demonstrates exactly that, so the line pre-empts its own scene. Trust the music. Also: the tools trembling / toolbox-not-moving is your best unspoken image here; resist the urge in Ch. 38 to repeat it.

### Chapter 38 — "Sweet Emotion" (Act III)

Studio C, 3 a.m., the one-take recording, and the double renunciation: "I can't" / "Me either." This is the keystone of the spine — both boys choose the people who already chose them, repeating their fathers' renunciations in real time, and the chapter knows it. "It's not giving up. It's choosing the people who already chose us." Grace idling in the lot, unmentioned and total. The recording at last lets the reader *hear* the band.

The move — the chapter occasionally narrates the universal weight ("a question that had been asked in a hundred thousand rooms where young musicians sat across from the choice"). Cut the cosmic framing; the specific room — Grace in a 21-hour uniform in a wagon with a slipping transmission — is a thousand times stronger than "a hundred thousand rooms." Robinson and Lehane agree on this: the universal arrives only through the absolutely particular. Keep Grace; lose the choir of imagined rooms.

### Chapter 39 — "Free Bird" (Act III)

The split denouement — Nick at Fort Benning, Jackie at Juilliard, both playing in secret, both renouncing in the family inheritance. Nick's guitar unplugged in the footlocker "structure only, no voice," Jackie saying "classical" to his roommate "the way you close a door." The two boys now living their fathers' lives. Structurally this is the long Lehane denouement, and it is earned.

The move — strong chapter; the main risk is that it states the bargain it has dramatized ("The American working-class bargain: three years of service for tuition"). You showed it in the footlocker and the unplugged strings; you don't need the sociological caption. Let "Joined the Army" — Nick's answer to "what happened?" — be the thesis, because it already is.

### Chapter 40 — "Comfortably Numb" (Act III)

2009: Maxanne walks the dying station, the boxed-up archive, the "41 years. Thank you" in blue ballpoint, the Pink Floyd cart, the Syd Barrett meditation. The dead hum of the building ("a heartbeat you could lean your hand on. That hum is gone") is one of the book's best sustained elegiac passages, and the Syd-as-mirror for Nick is a real intellectual move.

The move — this chapter does the Syd Barrett exegesis at length and then Chapter 41 does much of it again on-air. Decide which chapter carries Syd. I would let 40 be the silent walk-through (objects, the dead hum, the cart) and move all the Syd talk to the broadcast in 41, so 40 stays atmosphere and 41 carries the argument. As is, the Lennon prophecy and the Syd material both appear twice across these two chapters.

### Chapter 41 — "Walk This Way" (Act III)

The finale: Maxanne chooses the demo over the sanctioned Pink Floyd elegy, plays the tape full-volume on the last broadcast, signs off, and the recording goes viral. "Nick Johnson has four minutes and forty-three seconds in a drawer in my house" and "the best band that never was" — the title's contract, paid at last. The closing imagined scene of Jackie at a Berklee Steinway, the D-minor chord, the hand that "will lift the way a hand lifts from a table when someone calls you from the next room," is gorgeous and also the moment the book finally licenses its own omniscience ("I am making him up... because I need to").

The move — see the ending section below. In brief: the viral explosion ("It is BLOWING UP," a million views by 1:14 a.m., NPR and Pitchfork calling) is the sports-movie volume problem — it converts an elegy into a vindication and quietly betrays "the best band that never was," because a band a million people discover at 1 a.m. is no longer the band that never was. The playing of the tape is the right climax. The internet's verdict is the wrong coda.

## The ending — toward downbeat-but-resonant and "the best band that never was"

You already know the diagnosis, because you named the intent: the current ending lands feel-good. The tape goes viral, the comments pour in, the phone stacks up like planes over Logan, a million views by 1:14 a.m. The problem is structural, not tonal-decoration: virality is *rescue*. It answers the wound. It tells us the world has, after thirty years, finally seen Nick and Jackie — which means they are no longer "the best band that *never* was." They became, at the end, the band that was discovered. That is the disallowed-goal lesson from your own past work (Qitat): the goal goes in, the flag goes up, and you must let the offside be called. Here the goal goes in and the whole stadium roars. The thesis you most want to land is the one the coda erases.

Three options, each keeping Nick, Jackie, and the 2009 Maxanne frame. The constant in all three: she still plays the tape. The playing is the act of love and defiance and it must stay. What changes is what the world does with it afterward — and the discipline is to deny the reader the rescue.

1. The silence option (most Ondaatje, most aligned with your instincts). She plays the tape. She signs off. The ON AIR light dies. JOY FM comes up mid-hymn. She drives home with the radio off. End there — or nearly. No phone, no YouTube, no Sophie. The tape went out on fifty thousand watts to a city that was already asleep or already tuned away, and we never learn if anyone heard it. The "best band that never was" stays never. The book ends on Maxanne's posture toward the dark, not the dark's answer to her — exactly the ending-as-posture principle you landed in both prior novels (Percy's spatula, the disallowed goal). The imagined-Jackie-at-the-Steinway scene can stay, *because she names it as something she invents in the car "because I need to"* — that is the truest version of hope: unverified, self-administered, possibly false. Keep that. Cut the view counter.

2. The one-listener option (downbeat with a single warm ember, no rescue). Strip the virality to exactly one response. Not a million views — one. A single voicemail, or one car that does not pull out of a CVS lot in Quincy. Or: the imagined middle-aged man (Jackie, unnamed, "I am making him up") is the *only* receiver the book grants. One person, maybe real, maybe invented, heard four minutes and forty-three seconds. That honors the mirror — the Neighborhoods were excellent and local and never national — while denying the triumphal scale. The band stays unfamous; the music stays real; one person carries it. That is elegy, not vindication.

3. The Neighborhoods-mirror option (lean hardest into your stated source). End on the explicit doubling you set up but currently underuse. Let Maxanne, after the tape, speak the mirror plainly once and then stop: the Neighborhoods won the Rumble at the Rat in '79 and became the best band that never was — excellent, beloved in Boston, never national — and Nick and Jackie "never even made the card." Right now that truth arrives as a YouTube comment from a stranger, which is clever but secondhand. Give it to Maxanne as her last real sentence before "Goodnight, Boston," and then let the signal die. The found-language comment can stay as texture, but the thesis should be hers, said once, unglossed, and then surrendered to static.

My recommendation, holding your constraints: option 1, with the imagined-Jackie coda retained and the view-counter cut entirely. It is the most downbeat, it is the most Ondaatje, it is the most consistent with the two endings you have already earned in this study, and it is the only one that lets "the best band that never was" remain literally true on the last page. Lower the volume on the outcome (Skill 7): she still plays it — the win goes in — but the flag never goes up, because no one tells her, or us, that it landed.

One more thing for the ending, and it touches the whole book. The omniscience problem (Maxanne narrating Eileen's painting room, Dr. Marsh's interior, Patty's farm dawn — scenes she cannot know) is resolved beautifully and *only* on the final page: "I am making him up in a Volvo in an empty parking lot... because I need to. The imagining is what I have." That single confession retroactively licenses the entire book's omniscience — it tells us the brick chapters are Maxanne's *imaginative reconstruction*, a grieving woman building the interiors she was never given. That is a real and moving solution. But it arrives too late to do its work. Seed it earlier — once, lightly, in an early frame chapter (Ch. 1 or Ch. 2): a line where Maxanne admits she is imagining the rooms she never stood in, that she has spent thirty years building these people in her head. Plant it once at the top and the whole novel becomes legitimately, knowingly hers — and the show-versus-tell paradox half-dissolves, because a woman openly *imagining* the past is allowed to compress, withhold, and even to tell, in a way a woman *reporting* it is not.

## Closing — the first four moves

For an honest start, smallest first. Do these in order; each is a scalpel pass, not a rewrite.

1. Forbid the frame from glossing the bricks. Go through the eight frame chapters (1, 9, 11, 16, 22, 27, 40, 41) and cut every sentence where Maxanne tells you what the story *means* rather than what happened. Keep her objects, her voice, her "stay with me." This is the single highest-leverage change in the manuscript and it directly serves your core craft goal.

2. Fix the ending — option 1. Cut the viral coda; keep the played tape, the dead ON AIR light, the drive home with the radio off, and the invented Jackie at the Steinway. Let "the best band that never was" stay true.

3. Seed the imagining once, early. Add one line in Chapter 1 or 2 where Maxanne admits she is reconstructing rooms she never entered. This legitimizes the omniscient bricks and earns Eileen's painting room, Marsh's interior, and Patty's dawn.

4. Hunt the tics. Three constructions have become signatures: the negative triple ("She did not X. She did not Y. She Z'd."), the "door closing on its soft seal," and the repeated hose lines ("Classical earned his father's approval. Rock earned his breath"; engine/wingspan). Ring each once, where it is strongest, and cut the rest. A move earns its place twice; the fourth use is a tic.

And the honest encouragement, which is diagnosis: the bricks of this book — the garage, the bridge, the four quarters, the toolbox, Grace at the PTA, Eileen's turned canvases, the hands that reach for octaves nobody asked them to span — are the work of a writer who already trusts the architecture to carry the meaning. The thing keeping Walk This Way from being as good as it is built to be is not a deficit of craft. It is an excess of care: you have written a frame narrator who loves these people so much she keeps explaining them, the way a parent narrates a child's recital from the third row. Trust the silence the way Maxanne says she learned to trust dead air. The not-naming is the discipline, and you have already proven, chapter after chapter, that you know how.
