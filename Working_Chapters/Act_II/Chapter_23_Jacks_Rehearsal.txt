# CHAPTER 23: JACKIE'S REHEARSAL - THE WEIGHT OF EXCELLENCE

---

May 9th, 1979. The Marsh basement had been fixed up over the years—foam panels on the walls, a couple of good mics on stands, drum kit off in the corner. Jackie’s band, Crown Jewels, rehearsed there most days after school, surrounded by equipment that was definitely nicer than what most kids had, but not quite professional. They worked hard, running through their set over and over, stopping to redo harmonies that didn't quite lock in, tightening up the rhythm whenever someone got off. Jackie cared a lot about getting it right, and with his bandmates just as focused, they sometimes managed to pull off a sound that felt sharp and impressive—at least by high school standards—even if they knew it was never perfect.

But Jackie had heard about Grace Johnson's speech too—everyone in Sudbury had heard about it—and the knowledge sat in his stomach like a stone, weighing down every song, making every perfectly executed passage sound like evidence of the very inequality his mother's outburst had challenged.

Wednesday after school, forty-eight hours before the Battle of the Bands, Jackie found himself alone in the basement, playing "Bohemian Rhapsody" on the upright piano his parents kept against the far wall, trying to find in Mercury's operatic complexity some answer to questions he didn't know how to ask.

## **DR. MARSH'S PRESSURE**

To Dr. MArsh the Battle of the Bands had become his son's audition for a future that had already been planned, a demonstration that proper preparation and family investment produced superior results.

But Jackie was seventeen, not a thesis statement. And sitting alone in that perfect basement studio, playing songs that had once felt like rebellion, he couldn't shake the feeling that he was preparing to win an argument that missed the point entirely, to prove something that didn't need proving to people who wouldn't be convinced anyway.

The upright piano at the far wall caught the glow from the expensive lighting, its wood gleaming beneath Jackie's hands. His fingers moved across the keys with the mechanical precision of someone who'd been practicing since before memory, but the music that emerged felt hollow—technically perfect, but emotionally vacant.

## **THE PERFECT BASEMENT**

The basement studio was the mirror opposite of Nick's garage. Where Nick made do with Dan's battered tools and patched-together equipment, Jackie played surrounded by professional-grade instruments and carefully sculpted acoustics. Nick had to keep an ear out for neighbors if the music got too loud; Jackie had thick soundproofing that turned their sessions into a private world. Where Nick's band powered through with raw energy and scraped-up gear, Jackie's band rehearsed with the focus and discipline of a chamber ensemble, every take measured and exact.

Jackie's bandmates were more than just names—they were prodigies in their own right, each with a reputation that stretched beyond the walls of the Marsh basement. Elizabeth Marsh, Jackie's thirteen-year-old sister, sat at the violin. She was already well-known in Sudbury for her technical brilliance, having studied since she could hold a bow; every note she played was shaped by years of classical training and the unspoken pressure of family expectation. On drums was Arthur "Ace" Callahan, the freshman soccer star whose discipline on the field translated into his playing—every fill was crisp, every rhythm executed with athletic precision that set the foundation for the band's relentless momentum. On bass, Max Wesslen anchored the rhythm section. Tall and reserved, Max had been taking lessons since elementary school, and handled his instrument with easy confidence—helped, no doubt, by owning gear that made other local musicians jealous. 

They took their marks: Elizabeth’s bow hovered, ready; Ace settled behind the kit, sticks poised; Max tuned his bass with quick, practiced flicks. When they played, each part was exact and unyielding—no note out of place, no beat behind the metronome, no room for mistakes.

Together, they were Crown Jewels, and they sounded like it. Every harmony was perfectly arranged, every chord progression mathematically precise, every song executed with the kind of technical excellence that made other bands sound amateur by comparison.

But something was missing. Something that Jackie couldn't quite identify, something that made their perfect performances feel hollow, technically impressive but emotionally empty.

He tried "We Will Rock You," working on the bridge section, trying to find a way to make Mercury's anthem sound earned rather than assumed. But every note felt like evidence of the very privilege that Grace Johnson had challenged in her speech at Friendly's.


## **THE WEIGHT OF PRIVILEGE**

Jackie sat alone in that perfect basement studio, surrounded by equipment that cost more than most families' monthly grocery budgets, and understood that Friday night would determine more than which band won a high school competition. It would decide whether two former best friends could find a way back to each other, or whether America's talent for division was stronger than their shared history of harmony.
The upright piano stood out against the far wall, catching the spill of expensive lighting, its worn keys moving easily beneath Jackie’s practiced fingers. The sound was clear, perfectly balanced, a testament to years of careful maintenance and quiet investment. But Jackie couldn't shake the feeling that something was missing, something that had nothing to do with technique or equipment or the kind of advantages that money could buy.

He tried "Somebody to Love," working on the vocal harmonies, trying to find the emotional core that made Mercury's operatic complexity feel earned rather than assumed. But every note felt like evidence of the very privilege that Grace Johnson had challenged in her speech at Friendly's.

The weight of privilege sat on his shoulders like a lead apron. Every note he played carried the burden of proving that excellence wasn't just about having the best equipment and the best training. It was about having something to say, something that couldn't be bought or taught or inherited.

But what did he have to say? What was his story? What was he trying to prove?

## **THE REHEARSAL WITH CROWN JEWELS**

Elizabeth arrived at 7:00 PM, followed by Ace and Max, and suddenly the basement was full of the kind of energy that came from musicians who knew they were good and weren't afraid to show it. They ran through their set list with the precision of a military operation, every song executed with technical perfection that would have made most professional bands jealous.

"We Will Rock You" was their signature song, and they played it like they owned it. The opening stomp-stomp-clap was perfectly synchronized, the guitar riff crisp and clean, the vocals soaring with the kind of confidence that came from knowing you had the best equipment and the best training money could buy.

But Jackie couldn't shake the feeling that something was missing. Something that had nothing to do with technique or equipment or the kind of advantages that money could buy.

"Perfect," Dr. Marsh said, appearing in the doorway with the kind of satisfaction that came from watching a carefully planned demonstration unfold exactly as intended. "That's exactly what we need for Friday night. Technical excellence, emotional authenticity, proof that proper preparation produces superior results."

Jackie felt a particular loneliness in being chosen to prove things he wasn't even sure he believed himself. It was isolating to realize that, for all his preparation and talent, he was expected to embody ideals that didn't entirely fit his own doubts and insecurities.

He was thinking about the weight of carrying other people's dreams, about the impossible mathematics of representing entire communities through three-minute songs, about the particular burden of proving that excellence wasn't just about having the best equipment and the best training.

## **THE FINAL COUNTDOWN**

The Battle of the Bands was twenty-four hours away, and Jackie Marsh was discovering that the hardest part of competition wasn't playing better than your opponent. It was playing well enough to justify the faith that other people had invested in your success, while trying to remember why you'd started playing music in the first place.

He sat alone in that perfect basement studio, surrounded by equipment that cost more than most families' monthly grocery budgets, and wondered what he was trying to prove. Was he trying to prove that technical excellence could overcome emotional authenticity? Was he trying to prove that privilege could be earned rather than inherited? Was he trying to prove that two former best friends could find a way back to each other through music?

The upright piano stood against the far wall, catching the warm spill of expensive light across its aging wood. Every key responded faithfully to Jackie’s touch, the sound rich and balanced from years of careful maintenance. But no matter how technically perfect the music, he could feel an absence in every chord—a missing piece that had nothing to do with instruments, or practice, or privilege.

The Battle of the Bands wasn't just about music anymore. It was about everything that had gone wrong between him and Nick, everything that had gone wrong in Sudbury, everything that had gone wrong in America. And Jackie was about to find out if he was strong enough to carry it all.
