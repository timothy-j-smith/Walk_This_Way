Literary Comparisons
This novel sits in the tradition of Richard Russo's "Empire Falls" and Russell Banks's "The Sweet Hereafter"—examining how class divisions destroy American communities through a single crystallizing event. The radio station framework and musical focus echo Nick Hornby's "High Fidelity" but with deeper social critique. The dual timeline structure and meditation on lost possibility resembles Michael Chabon's "The Amazing Adventures of Kavalier & Clay" or Jennifer Egan's "A Visit from the Goon Squad".
The specific use of real historical detail within fiction follows E.L. Doctorow's "Ragtime" tradition, while the focus on teenage possibility destroyed by adult systems echoes Donna Tartt's "The Secret History". The novel's examination of how art fails to transcend class barriers despite our myths about its democratizing power connects to Colson Whitehead's "Sag Harbor" and Tom Perrotta's "The Leftovers”.
Act Synopses (with expansions)
ACT ONE: THE FREQUENCY
In 2024, legendary WBCN DJ Maxanne Sartori begins her final broadcast before the station dies at midnight, choosing to tell the story of two boys whose musical partnership was destroyed by class divisions in 1979 Sudbury, Massachusetts. Through flashbacks, we witness Nick Johnson (working-class, father dead from industrial accident) and Jackie Marsh (upper-middle-class, surgeon's son) as former friends now rivals, separated by Hop Brook and the town's four quadrants divided by both nature and the town dump. Their shared history at a 1974 music camp, where they harmonized with Patty Lally before the world taught them they shouldn't, haunts their present rivalry.
As the Battle of the Bands approaches, both boys struggle with their assigned roles—Nick's authentic garage rock versus Jackie's classical training forced into rock performance. The competition transforms from a simple school event into a class war, with WBCN's coverage elevating it to regional significance. Through Maxanne's visits to both boys, we see how their musical preferences (Aerosmith vs. Queen) represent deeper divisions about authenticity versus sophistication, with adults like Bob Wentworth trying to bridge gaps while others like Dr. Marsh reinforce them.
ACT TWO: THE STATIC
The Battle of the Bands unfolds in real-time, with eight bands representing different facets of Sudbury's divided community—from the working-class Jesters to the all-girl Cherry Bomb to the rich kids' Establishment. Each full performance reveals not just musical style but class position, with the audience initially segregated by geography gradually beginning to mix as music temporarily dissolves boundaries. Jackie's Crown Jewels deliver technical perfection until he breaks script with Elton John's "Your Song," revealing the emotion his parents tried to train out of him.
The competition's climax comes when Patty Lally's Rumours performs original songs about their shared past, explicitly naming the 1974 camp where "three kids found a frequency," followed by Nick's Walking the Line, whose original "Hop Brook Blues" devastates the audience with its raw truth about division. When Walking the Line wins, Nick's attempt to share the victory with Jackie fails as Dr. Marsh immediately reasserts control, forcing Jackie to choose between momentary unity and his predetermined future. The parking lot aftermath shows all divisions immediately reinstating themselves, with Maxanne observing they witnessed something "both revolutionary and completely futile."
ACT THREE: THE SILENCE
Jumping between 1979's immediate aftermath and 2024's final broadcast, we witness the slow dissolution of all possibility as Nick, Jackie, and Patty are pulled into their predetermined paths. The expanded chapters follow Nick's final summer before joining the Army for the GI Bill, Jackie's isolation at Tanglewood then Juilliard, and Patty's evolution into a Cambridge folk singer and Roxbury music teacher. The geography of Sudbury transforms—the dump becomes Cavicchio's greenhouse empire, railroad lines become bike paths, Hop Brook gets a microbrewery—but the divisions simply take new forms.
As midnight approaches, Maxanne reveals what became of each through "digital breadcrumbs": Nick owns a construction company restoring the history he couldn't afford to inhabit; Jackie teaches music theory at Berklee, academically analyzing what he once lived; Patty still seeks the frequency in Roxbury classrooms and empty Cambridge clubs. Playing Patty's never-aired 1980 recording "After the Battle," Maxanne counts down WBCN's final seconds, ending with the revelation that "somewhere in Sudbury, three kids are still harmonizing in the garage of memory," but only if we remember how to listen past the static we've chosen over songs.
