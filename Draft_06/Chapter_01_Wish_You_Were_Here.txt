ACT I: TOYS IN THE ATTIC

---

CHAPTER 1: "WISH YOU WERE HERE"

BOSTON.  August 11, 2009

Four hours. That's what I've got left.

Four hours before WBCN becomes just another frequency in the graveyard of American radio. Four hours before the suits flip the switch and turn us into automated Christian contemporary—because apparently Jesus rocks harder than Freddie Mercury ever did. Four hours before forty-one years of breaking bands, of late-night conversations with musicians who poured their souls into three-minute songs, of watching Boston transform from a city of steel and sweat into whatever the hell it's become now—four hours before all of that gets erased like a bad demo tape.

I'm Maxanne Sartori. Fifty-nine years old, sitting in Studio B with a paper cup of Heaven Hill at my elbow and the ON AIR sign blinking red like a dying heartbeat. Can you believe it? The station called and brought me back. This one last time, and I'm in the same studio where I watched Steven Tyler hear his own voice on the radio for the first time back in '73—the look on his face when he realized that raw, untamed sound was actually going out to thousands of people, that it was real, that it mattered. The same turntables that spun the first singles from bands who went on to fill stadiums. The same microphone that's carried my voice through the 70's, the years of chaos, rebellion, and music that made parents wary of what was coming.

"Maxanne?" The program director's voice crackles through the intercom like a bad phone connection. The Kid's twenty-eight, wears sneakers that cost more than my first apartment—a studio in the Back Bay I rented with my first WBCN paycheck—and thinks rock history started with Nirvana. "You've got four hours left. Make them count."

Make them count.

Make them count. As if four hours could contain what WBCN meant to a city that lived and breathed rock and roll. As if I could summarize forty-one years of rock radio in some tidy narrative arc with a beginning, middle, and end. As if the story I'm about to tell could be wrapped up with a happy ending and a lesson learned. It doesn't work that way, I'm afraid.

But here's the thing about endings—they make liars of us all. You promise to go out with dignity, say something profound, make those final moments matter. And then you find yourself sitting in a radio booth with that cup warming your palm and a note Steven Tyler had sent around the time he heard the station was closing. "For the first lady who ever gave a damn," the note said. Even after all these years, even after becoming one of the biggest rock stars in the world, he still remembers. I take a sip.

I adjust the headphones—the same Sony MDR-7506s I've worn since 1971, held together now with electrical tape and pure stubbornness. The console lights pulse expectantly. Outside the booth windows, Boston glitters in the August darkness—all glass towers and gentrification where dive bars used to breed bands, where the Rat used to host shows that would make your grandmother blush.

"This is Maxanne Sartori, and for the next four hours, you're listening to the final broadcast of WBCN, 104.1 on your FM dial. The Rock of Boston. The station that launched Aerosmith when they were just kids from New Hampshire with more attitude than money. The station that gave J. Geils his first shot, that broke The Cars when they were still just a garage band, that made Boston believe it could rock as hard as anywhere else."

"But tonight isn't about the bands that made it. Tonight is about two kids from Sudbury I could never get out of my head—and the only tape that proves they ever breathed into the same microphone."

The silence goes out over the city—dead air on every radio still tuned to us. In the engineering booth, the kid they've assigned to babysit my final show looks up from his phone. Maybe something in my voice warns him this won't be the nostalgia trip he expected.

Spring of '79. Disco was finally running out of breath—you could hear it in the way the Bee Gees had stopped being dangerous and started being wallpaper. Three years of *Saturday Night Fever*; bell bottoms and mirror balls in every bar that used to host a four-piece. The labels wanted safe. The dance floor wanted falsetto. But in the garages and the cafeterias, rock was pushing back—treating disco like something you caught at the roller rink and couldn't shake. Punk was leaking in from the clubs. MTV was still three years off, when image would start eating sound. WBCN had spent a decade betting on the kids who thought volume was a virtue. That spring we put our name on a Battle of the Bands at Lincoln-Sudbury. I judged it. We told ourselves we were scouting talent. That was half the truth.

I spin my chair to face the wall of albums. The vinyl spines catch the booth's amber light, each one a ghost. My fingers find the right cart without looking—muscle memory, my hands knowing these grooves better than my own face in the mirror.

Through the booth window, the billboard across Boylston Street advertises luxury condos where The Rat used to host bands too raw for radio. Twenty-four hundred square feet starting at 2.2 million—you could buy the entire block of Kenmore Square for that in '77. The Ramones played that club back when Joey was so tall he had to duck under the pipes backstage, and the sound system was one blown fuse away from silence.

The kid in engineering is scrolling again. He has no idea what a garage means to rock and roll. He grew up with GarageBand, thinking music could be manufactured in a bedroom with the right plugins. He never felt four walls, concrete floor, and kids who had nothing to lose.

I reach for the cassette on my desk. The plastic case is warm from my grip, the label slightly faded: "Demo - Nick/Jackie - un-Authorized - May 1979" in block letters. My letters. The only proof they ever played together. The rest of the story? That's what the next four hours are for. So pour yourself something strong, turn off your iPhone, and let me tell you how the Battle of the Bands became a war."

The ON AIR sign pulses. Four hours left to tell the truth.

I thumb the channel fader down a hair—old habit, making room for my own voice—and lean into the microphone.

"But first, we need to talk about a garage on Dutton Road—and a pegboard with an empty slot where the nine-sixteenths used to live."

I close my eyes and let the memory take me back. March 1979. Mud season—the snow gone gray at the edges of the yards, the brook running high and brown with melt, the maples still bare, the sky the color of old steel. That's when I first drove out to Sudbury, following rumors about a kid who played guitar like he was trying to resurrect the dead.
