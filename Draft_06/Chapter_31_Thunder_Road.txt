CHAPTER 31: "THUNDER ROAD"


---

May 16, 1979. Battle day.

The heat had come in overnight — eighty-nine by noon if the bank sign on Route 20 could be trusted, the air thick enough to chew. By seven in the morning the sky over Sudbury was the color of a bruise healing wrong: yellow at the horizon, iron-gray above the pines, and somewhere west of Worcester the first distant roll of thunder that wasn't thunder yet, just pressure moving through the atmosphere the way a chord moves through a room before anybody plays it.

Nick Johnson woke to the house ticking.

Refrigerator. Pipe in the wall. Grace already up — coffee pot burbling in the kitchen, the smell of Maxwell House through the closed bedroom door. He lay on his back with the sheet at his waist and listened to the thunder again, farther off, a low note under the morning.

He got up.

---

Grace had left a plate on the counter — two eggs scrambled dry the way he liked them, toast cut diagonal, glass of milk. She was in the good navy dress already, the one with the buttons, winter coat folded over the back of the chair though it was May. Friendly's had given her the night off. Tom Andrews had pinned a WBCN button crooked over her purse clasp without being asked.

"You eat," she said.

He ate standing up.

The Marshall sat by the side door in its case. The pawn-shop guitar Dan had bought him — crack in the upper bout packed with wood glue, strap dark where his thumb rode — leaned against the pegboard with the picks in his pocket. Stephen's Nova would be at the curb at eight. David was walking from the quarter mile down. Mark had the Rhodes in his brother's van.

Grace watched him check the latch on the guitar case twice.

"Don't forget to breathe," she said.

"I know."

"Nick."

"I know, Mom."

She put her hand on his shoulder once, the way she put her hand on things she couldn't fix — flat, brief, warm — and took it away before it could become a speech.

---

Two miles north on Maple Street, the Marsh house faced clipped hedges and a sky that looked the same from every window.

Jackie had been awake since five.

He sat at the kitchen table in jeans and a white shirt with the top button open, coffee he didn't drink cooling in front of him. Eileen moved through the room in her morning way — pearls, orange juice poured into a glass with the pulp screened out, toast triangles on a plate nobody had asked for. Dr. Richard Marsh read the *Globe* business section without turning the page.

"The forecast calls for storms after six," Eileen said.

"Mm," Richard said.

On the sideboard, the Juilliard envelope sat unopened where it had sat for three weeks. In Jackie's room upstairs, face down in a drugstore frame, the Polaroid from Camp Elbanobscot — three kids on a dock — had not been turned over since March.

Stephen Marsh would drive him at eight-fifteen. Ace and Max and Elizabeth would meet at the loading dock behind the auditorium with the Baldwin parts and the Ampeg and the violin case that cost more than Nick's entire garage.

Jackie finished the coffee because finishing was what you did. He went upstairs to tie his shoes.

---

Patty Lally fed the sows at five-fifteen the way she always fed them. Thunder rolled closer while she hosed the near pen — not close, not yet, just the sky clearing its throat.

By seven-thirty she was in the Plymouth with her Martin in back and Steve's textless plan already made: meet at the school at four, sound check at five, Rumours seventh on the card if Chuck Morrison read the lineup the same way Wentworth had posted it. She had the set in her head. Rhiannon. Dreams. Silver Springs. Go Your Own Way. Not a weapon. A reminder.

She drove past the Cavicchio fields with the windows down. The smell came in and she did not apologize for it.

---

Route 20 was already wrong by eight o'clock.

Cars lined the shoulder near the Lincoln-Sudbury front walk — station wagons from the northwest waxed and quiet, pickups from the northeast with rust at the wheel wells, a Volvo with a college sticker and a Nova with a dent in the rear quarter panel and Steve Bankuti's Chevette with the hinge that whined. Kids in denim and kids in blazers crossed the parking lot carrying cases the way soldiers carry rifles — not looking where they were going, looking at who was looking.

The booster club had set up a table by the lobby doors. Popcorn machine not running yet. Programs stacked in a cardboard box. WBCN banner tacked to the brick with duct tape.

Nick came up from the shop-corridor side with Stephen and David and Mark behind him, Rhodes on a dolly rattling over the expansion joints. The Marshall case banged Nick's knee through the leather. Stephen said something about the set list order and Nick didn't answer because his throat had gone dry the way it went dry before every room that mattered.

Jackie came from the east entrance with his father walking ten feet behind — not escorting, accompanying, the way Richard Marsh accompanied everything — and Ace carrying the kick drum and Elizabeth with her violin case and Max silent with the Precision on his back.

They crossed the same asphalt without crossing the same path.

Nick's eyes swept the lot and skipped Jackie's blazer the way a needle skips a scratch. Jackie's gaze moved through the space where Nick stood as if that patch of pavement held nothing but empty oil stains.

Forty feet. Linoleum's outdoor cousin.

Patty watched from the loading ramp with her case on her hip and Robin tuning bass behind her and did not wave at either of them.

---

The auditorium doors were propped open with a wedge of two-by-four.

Inside, the air smelled of floor wax and cast iron and four hundred and forty-three folding seats waiting to groan. The painted maple backdrop from every school play since 1960 hung behind an empty stage — red gone brown at the edges. Fluorescent cove lights threw the room into grocery-store color. Somewhere backstage a custodian tested a microphone — tap, tap, feedback squeal, apology.

Nick stood at the stage-right gap in the velvet curtain where the hem didn't reach the floor and looked at the house through the slit. Third row left was where Grace would sit. He made himself look away.

Jackie stood stage left at the Yamaha they'd rolled out for the night — not the Steinway, the school grand, scarred but in tune — and ran a scale without sound, fingers on the keys, breath held.

Patty stood in the wings with her guitar strap over her shoulder and felt the thunder move closer — a real roll this time, east to west, the building's old bones carrying it through the concrete rake.

Not hope. Pressure.

The sky outside the lobby doors had gone green at the edges.

Chuck Morrison's voice would be hot on the mic by seven. Maxanne Sartori was already somewhere in the building with a clipboard and a pencil and a styrofoam cup of WBCN coffee. Eight bands. Five hundred dollars. A town that had turned music into sides.

Nick adjusted the strap. Jackie rolled his sleeves. Patty tuned the B string once because the B string was always the one.

The doors kept opening. The heat kept rising. The thunder kept not breaking — held, loaded, waiting for the downbeat nobody in Sudbury had agreed to share.
