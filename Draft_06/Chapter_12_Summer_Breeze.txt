CHAPTER 12: "SUMMER BREEZE"

Camp Elbanobscot sat on forty acres of Sudbury conservation land between Route 20 and the reservoir — an outdoor camp that specialized, of all things, in music. Not archery, not sailing, not the kind of place where rich kids learned tennis and poor kids learned they couldn't afford to learn tennis. Music. Guitars and keyboards and voices carrying through white pines that had been standing since before the Revolution.

The wilderness part was real. Pine needles under your boots on the path to the lake. Pine cones scattered along the conservation floor, dry and resinous, crushed when you didn't watch where you stepped. Humidity that stuck your shirt to your back by ten in the morning. Hiking boots by the cabin door. Fallen trees left where they'd dropped — nobody on this land cut firewood; the blowdowns rotted slow and fed the moss. The lake had a dock, a rowboat with chipped green paint, kids fishing for sunfish with worms kept in a coffee can. Blue sky in July that went white at the edges by afternoon.

The music part was cared for. The converted barn had a piano that stayed almost in tune and acoustics that made everything sound better than it was. Instruments lived inside — cases latched, keyboards under tarps until someone needed them. You wiped your hands before you touched the keys. Counselors meant it. This was the strange thing about Elbanobscot: fifteen minutes from anybody's house in town, and nobody asked what your father did.

---

Sunday, late July.

Grace's wood-paneled wagon came up the gravel access road with the timing a half-beat off in third and didn't stop until the tires were on the camp-side grass. Nick sat in the back with his acoustic across his knees and Jackie's case beside him — no seatbelt, window down, pine resin on the air mixing with the hot vinyl smell of the seat. Grace put the wagon in park and turned around.

"Two weeks," she said. "You earned it."

She had her wallet because she was Grace Johnson and she did not leave the house without it. She had a windbreaker over her sweatshirt though it was eighty-two degrees. She didn't get out.

"Go on," she said.

Nick climbed out with both guitars. Grace watched him cross to the registration table by the flagpole, then pulled the wagon back onto Route 20 toward Dutton Road and the mortgage and whatever Dan was building that Sunday.

Jackie arrived twelve minutes later in the back seat of the Marshes' Volvo wagon — college sticker on the rear window, upholstery still smelling like the chamois Dr. Marsh used on Saturdays. Dr. Marsh cut the engine and let Jackie out with his guitar and a duffel that had been packed by someone who believed in folding. His father didn't wave. Jackie lifted a hand to the closed window anyway, adjusted his strap, and walked up the same gravel Nick had walked.

They claimed bunks on the same wall of Cabin 7 that afternoon — screen walls, mosquitoes with open invitations, guitars propped against pine planks inside the cabin where the dew couldn't reach them. By dinnertime they'd mapped the place. The lake was a ten-minute walk through birch trees, path worn to bare dirt. The practice meadow sat on a slope above the water; if you played there at sunset the sound carried down across the lake and came back a half-second later, softened.

That first night, lying in their bunks while eight other boys settled into camp fatigue, Nick said, "This is different."

Jackie didn't ask what he meant. He knew. At Elbanobscot, nobody's last name meant anything. Nobody knew that Nick's house had a mortgage three months behind or that Jackie's father had opinions about rock and roll that could freeze a room. Here the only currency was whether you could play, and both of them were rich.

"Yeah," Jackie said. "It is."

---

The next morning they met Patty Lally.

She walked into the dining hall during breakfast and the room shifted. Not dramatically — nobody dropped a fork or stopped talking. But the air moved. She was thirteen, tall and lanky, with straight wheat-colored hair that hung past her shoulders. She was barefoot. Her feet were brown with dirt and she didn't seem to notice or care, and she carried a canvas bag slung across one shoulder with a songbook sticking out of it, pages flagged with torn strips of newspaper.

She smelled like something. Not perfume — the opposite of perfume. Something faintly animal and earthy that the other kids couldn't quite place, a smell that lived in her jacket and her hair and the leather strap of her bag. Southeast Sudbury, the wind off the Cavicchio fields, a smell that got into curtains and school notebooks and didn't wash out all the way. Patty had grown up breathing it. She never apologized for it because she'd never been taught that the place you came from was something to apologize for.

She slid her tray onto the table across from Nick and Jackie and sat down like she'd been sitting there all summer.

"You two are in the intensive, right?" Not a question, really. A confirmation.

Nick nodded. "Guitar. I'm Nick. This is Jackie."

"Piano and guitar," Jackie added.

"I know who you are." She bit into an apple, chewed, kept talking. Her hands on the apple were small and strong, nails cut straight across, a faint permanent stain at the base of her thumb and a white scar across one knuckle; Nick noticed and didn't ask. "Talent show. 'Walk This Way' folded into 'Bohemian Rhapsody.' You made Karen Bowers cry in the bathroom afterward. That was the best thing I've ever seen at Curtis."

"You go to Curtis?" Nick said, and felt immediately stupid, because of course she did. She'd been in the hallways, in the cafeteria, at assemblies, a presence he'd walked past a hundred times without registering.

"Same school since kindergarten," Patty said. She looked at Nick with a level gaze that had no accusation in it, just amusement. "You never turned around once."

Nick felt heat climb his neck. Jackie was very quiet beside him, holding his milk carton with both hands.

"I sing," Patty said. "And play guitar. And I hum constantly, which drives everyone insane." As if to prove it, she started humming something — a melody that moved in a way Nick couldn't quite track, rising and turning back on itself, restless and certain at the same time. He didn't recognize it. It sounded like it belonged to her.

She stopped humming and looked at both of them. "So what are we working on?"

We. As if the three of them had already agreed on something.

---

Over the next five days they became the thing the other campers talked about. Not because they were the best musicians at Elbanobscot — there was a cellist from Newton who could make you cry and a drummer from Framingham who played like he had six arms — but because something happened when the three of them played together.

They found it on the third afternoon, in the practice meadow above the lake.

Ninety-two degrees at noon. Thunderheads over the reservoir by three, a hard twenty-minute rain that knocked the pollen down and dropped the air ten degrees. By four-thirty the sky was washed blue and the grass was steaming and everything smelled like wet pine and electricity.

Nick was noodling on his guitar, working through a progression he'd stolen from Joe Perry and bent into something his own fingers preferred. Jackie sat at the portable keyboard the camp had carried up from the barn under a tarp, adding chords underneath — not copying, not competing, building a floor for Nick's line to stand on, the way he always did.

Then Patty started singing.

She didn't ask what key they were in. She didn't wait for a verse or a chorus. She opened her mouth and a voice came out that was lower than you'd expect from a girl that thin, with a grain in it like fine sandpaper, a texture that caught on the melody and held it. She sang without words at first — vowel sounds weaving between Nick's guitar and Jackie's piano like a third rope braiding into a cord. Her voice found the spaces the instruments left open and filled them without crowding, and when she finally landed on a harmony it locked into place with a physical click, like a door latch falling home.

Nick stopped playing. Jackie stopped playing. The silence that followed had a different shape than the silence before.

"Don't stop," Patty said. "Play it again."

They played it again. Before the instruments came back in, she took a breath and nodded at them both, and what they found on the second pass — all three of them opening their mouths on the same held breath — was a plain C-major triad a cappella for one beat: Nick's root underneath, Jackie's G above, Patty's E clean and unshowy on top, and she laughed.

The laugh was short and surprised and belonged to no one. It belonged to the chord.

Then the guitar and the keyboard came back under it and all three of them leaned into the song — Nick driving the rhythm harder, Jackie pushing the chords into unexpected territory, Patty's voice climbing above both of them and then diving underneath, finding notes that shouldn't have worked but did. The sound carried down the slope and across the lake, and the lake sent it back, and for a few minutes the meadow held all three of them inside a single resonance that felt less like performing and more like breathing.

The sun was going down. The lake had turned the color of copper. The light caught Patty's hair and turned it gold.

Nick looked at Jackie. Jackie was watching Patty.

Not the way you watch a musician. The way you watch a person who has just changed the terms of your life without asking permission. Jackie's hands were still on the keys but he wasn't playing. He was just looking at her, and his face held an expression Nick had never seen on him before — open, unguarded, the careful Marsh composure cracked just enough to let something raw show through.

Nick looked away. He looked at the lake, at the sky, at his own hands on the guitar strings.

"Again," Patty said, and grinned.

They played until the mosquitoes drove them inside. They played in the music building after dinner — doors shut against the damp, instruments wiped down on the benches — working through songs they all knew. Patty leading them into Fleetwood Mac, Nick pulling them toward Aerosmith, Jackie insisting on Queen, the arguments becoming their own kind of music.

---

The night they found the chord, Nick couldn't sleep.

Cabin 7 had gone quiet by eleven — eight other boys in their bunks, breath settling into the loose unison of camp fatigue, pine-board walls ticking as the day's heat let go of the metal roof. Somewhere out past the reservoir a whip-poor-will called its own name on repeat. A bullfrog answered from the reedy end of the lake. Someone three cabins over was working through chord changes on an unplugged electric, the notes drifting through the screens with the lake damp and the peepers.

The boy two bunks down from Nick snored like a tractor — the low hitch and diesel catch of an engine trying to turn over in cold weather, once, twice, then the long roll.

"You awake?" Jackie's voice came down from the bunk across the aisle, pitched so it wouldn't carry.

"Yeah."

A pause. Then Jackie said, "There's sheet music in our attic."

Nick rolled onto his side. "Yours?"

"My father's. Boxes of it. Piano." The word landed with the weight of a thing Jackie had not said out loud before. "I found it looking for skis. He doesn't know I saw."

"He played?"

"He played." Jackie was quiet a minute. "He doesn't anymore. The Baldwin in the den hasn't been tuned since before I was born."

Nick thought about Dr. Marsh coming home from the hospital at seven and reading a journal in the wing chair and not looking at the piano in the same room with him.

"My dad plays," Nick said. "Guitar. In the garage, after he thinks I'm asleep. It comes up through the floor of my room."

"You ever tell him you hear it?"

"No."

The tractor-boy snored. The whip-poor-will kept its count.

"I don't know what I'm going to do after high school," Nick said. It came out before he'd decided to say it. "I don't know if I can do anything."

"You can play."

"Yeah."

"That's something."

"Is it?"

Jackie didn't answer right away. The kid three cabins over had moved on to something Nick didn't know — a slow descending line, almost a lullaby, the low E string buzzing a little where it needed a new set.

"It's what we've got," Jackie said finally.

Somewhere out beyond Cabin 7 the lake did whatever lakes do at midnight. The screen door of the counselors' cabin banged once and didn't bang again. Nick lay on his back and listened to eight other boys breathing and the whip-poor-will counting and his best friend not sleeping.

He thought about Patty's E on top of the triad. He thought about her hands.

He was asleep before he could think about anything else.

---

The first week of August they turned fourteen — a week apart on paper, a shared birthday on the camp schedule because the counselor running the intensive didn't care about paperwork and the boys had asked to split the difference. Sheet cake in the dining hall. Candles. The Newton cellist played something classical while everybody clapped. Patty sat between them at the table and stole frosting off Nick's plate with her finger and didn't apologize.

---

They played on the last night of camp, sitting on the dock with their feet in the black water, Patty humming that same restless melody while Nick picked a quiet line on his acoustic and Jackie added soft chords on a guitar he'd signed out from the barn because the keyboard wouldn't survive the dew.

Three voices. Three instruments. The sound moved out across the water in the dark, mixing with the loon calls and the lap of small waves against the dock pilings, and if you were lying in your bunk in Cabin 4 or Cabin 9, half-asleep, you would have heard it and thought it was one voice, one instrument, one song.

---

The last morning, gravel parking by the flagpole.

Patty wrote her phone number on the back of a camp schedule and tore it in half. She gave one half to Nick and one half to Jackie.

"You'll have to talk to each other to get the whole number," she said.

Nick laughed. Jackie laughed.

A pickup truck idled at the edge of the lot — farm blue gone chalky on the hood, hay in the bed, an older brother behind the wheel who didn't get out. Patty climbed into the back with her canvas bag, bare feet on the warm metal, and settled against the wheel well like she'd been doing it her whole life. The truck pulled onto Route 20 without a wave.

Grace's wagon was already there when Nick looked for it. Grace in the driver's seat, window down, same windbreaker. Nick loaded his guitar into the back and climbed in front. No seatbelt. She didn't ask how it was. She put the wagon in gear.

Jackie's Volvo was last. Dr. Marsh had the engine running, windows up, air conditioning on. Jackie got in the back with his duffel and his case and lifted two fingers to the rear glass as the wagon pulled away. Nick saw it in the side mirror. Jackie saw the wagon go. Neither of them mentioned Patty for the ride home.

Nick watched the camp access road shrink in the passenger window — pines, gravel, the practice meadow gone behind the trees — and smelled pine resin on his hands from the dock last night, not from sap on the strings, and felt the ghost of those two weeks rise up through his chest like a chord he'd forgotten he knew.
