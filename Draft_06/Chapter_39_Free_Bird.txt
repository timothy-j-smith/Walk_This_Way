CHAPTER 39: "FREE BIRD"

---

The town decided the tie meant nobody won.

Friendly's replayed the Battle in fragments — *Did you hear the Marsh kid?* / *The Johnson boy was loud* / *That girl from the farms* — and the register kid on Route 217 rang up coffee while two men argued about whether a tie was a cop-out or the only honest ending. The Sudbury Town Crier ran a photo of the contract signing: two boys on stage, two pens, faces turned slightly away from each other. The caption said *Shared Victory* in a font that made sharing sound like a refund.

Nick and Jackie did not explain Studio C.

They did not explain the garage. They did not explain why they passed in the east hallway with their eyes on the lockers and their shoulders angled toward opposite walls. Three weeks left of senior year felt like three weeks of held breath — teachers handing out review packets, yearbook signatures in the commons, the class of '79 counting down to something nobody could name without lying.

Patty signed yearbooks with *Stay wild* and meant it. She saw Nick once at the bike rack and Jackie once at the west doors and did not try to stand between them. She had sung *Go Your Own Way* on a stage that belonged to her for four minutes. She knew what leaving sounded like before the leaving happened.

---

Graduation was the second Friday in June.

The field behind Lincoln-Sudbury smelled like cut grass and sunscreen and the faint diesel from the buses idling on Route 117. Folding chairs in rows on the turf. Families in the bleachers with cameras and folding fans. The sky was the color of a clean channel — no static, no promise.

Grace Johnson sat mid-bleachers in a dress she'd bought at Filene's Basement in April — not the navy Friendly's dress from the Battle, never that dress again. She held a tissue she didn't use. When Nick's name was called she stood before the rest of the row did, one sharp motion, and sat back down when he crossed the stage.

Dr. Richard Marsh sat three sections over with Eileen and Elizabeth. His suit was gray. His hands were still. When Jackie took the diploma he did not stand — a restraint that cost him something visible only in the set of his jaw.

Nick and Jackie crossed the same stage twelve names apart.

Nick in a borrowed sport coat from Stephen's father, tie loose, mortarboard straight. Jackie in what his mother called appropriate — blazer, crease, the uniform of a life already packed in boxes marked JUILLIARD.

They did not shake hands in the line. They did not look at each other on the grass after. Forty feet of parents and balloons and other people's joy between them — the same forty feet they'd been carrying since November, since the funeral, since before the funeral if you counted honestly.

Patty stood at the edge of the parking lot with her Martin in its case and her boots unlaced. She watched Nick load his gown into the wagon's back seat. She watched Jackie's mother steer him toward the Volvo. She hummed nothing. The pig-farm quarter air moved through the pines toward the dump and carried the smell of turned earth and something sweeter underneath — cut grass, exhaust, the end of something.

From a Trans Am pulling out of the lot, two bars of Lynyrd Skynyrd climbed through an open window before the driver rolled it up against the wind.

*If I leave here tomorrow…*

Then the parking lot swallowed it.

---

Nick cashed the two hundred and fifty dollars the Monday after graduation.

Grace protested at the kitchen table — Maxwell House between them, the check flat on the Formica. *That's your money. Your prize.*

He left it beside the salt shaker. "Transmission."

She cashed it without ceremony. The mechanic on Route 20 replaced the belt and told her the timing would need work by fall. She wrote the estimate on the back of a deposit slip and stuck it to the refrigerator with a magnet shaped like a lobster.

Nick mowed three lawns on Dutton Road. Fixed Mrs. Connolly's screen door without being asked. Slept with the pawn-shop guitar on the floor beside his bed — crack in the upper bout, handle re-riveted, Dan's purchase still holding.

In July he signed the papers the recruiter had left in an envelope thick enough to mean business. Three years. Education benefit. Fort Benning in September. Grace read the signature line twice and poured more coffee. She did not say *don't*. She did not say *your father*. She said, "Eat something before you go," which was the same sentence she'd used the morning of the Battle and the morning of Studio C and every morning that mattered without announcing that it mattered.

The last night in Sudbury he opened the garage bay door and played unplugged with the door up and the stars over the conservation pines. No Marshall. No audience. The notes barely left the strings — structure only, the way blueprints hold a house before the walls go up. He played until his fingertips hurt and the hurt felt like proof and then he closed the door and latched it and did not look at the red toolbox under the calendar.

---

Jackie left for New York the third week of August.

Two suitcases. A garment bag for concert black. His mother cried at the door with her hand over her mouth — quiet, disciplined grief. Dr. Marsh drove him to Logan with the radio off and both hands at ten and two.

The Juilliard dorm room was nine feet by eleven. Jackie measured it with his shoes heel to toe because measuring was what you did when the world gave you a box and asked you to become yourself inside it. Twin bed. Desk. Dresser. Window on West 66th where taxis ran yellow all night and the sound never stopped.

His bedroom in Sudbury had been fourteen by sixteen with an oak tree outside the window his mother had planted the year he was born.

The practice-room Steinway on the fourth floor was better. Seven-foot Model B. The keys responded to intention the way a blade responds to pressure — immediately, without forgiveness.

He practiced six hours a day. Chopin etudes. Bach inventions. Debussy preludes. His left hand was called exemplary. His sight-reading was called exceptional.

Paul Nakamura from Portland shared the suite — cello case by the door, turntable in the corner, Monk and Miles in rotation.

"What kind of music do you listen to?" Paul asked their second night.

"Classical," Jackie said.

He did not mention Queen. He did not mention the Crown Jewels or the tie or the Wurlitzer with the cracked middle C or the garage on Dutton Road where lemonade had come in Mason jars. He said *classical* the way you close a door — firmly, with your back against it.

---

September — Fort Benning, Georgia.

The barracks smelled like floor wax and sweat and Pine-Sol, and underneath all of it, red clay. Georgia clay got into boot seams and nostrils and the spaces between teeth. By the second week Nick had stopped tasting it. By the fourth week he'd stopped noticing most things.

The body adapted. Run. March. Crawl. Repeat. His legs learned distances his brain refused to calculate. His hands learned the M16 the way they'd learned the pawn-shop guitar — repetition so thorough it became reflex.

The guitar lived in his footlocker. Bedsheet wrapped around the body because the latches were loose and the lock was broken and the instrument was the one thing he owned that could not be replaced at a surplus store. Combination set to his mother's birthday. Thirty-eight men in the barracks. He trusted none of them with this.

After lights out, when the breathing settled into the rhythm of collective sleep, Nick opened the footlocker. He didn't plug in. No amp. No room for an amp. He played so quiet the notes existed only between his fingers and the wire — audible to himself and maybe to Specialist Haywood two bunks down, who listened a month before he spoke.

"You any good?" Haywood whispered one night.

"I was."

"What happened?"

Nick fretted a D minor chord soft as paper tearing. "Joined the Army."

Grace wrote every week. Envelopes from Woolworth's — white, standard, handwriting tight as inventory forms. She wrote about Friendly's. The new assistant manager and the register. The wagon holding. Leaves turning on Dutton Road. Mrs. Abernathy's dog and the Hendersons' tomatoes.

She never asked about the guitar. She never asked if he was happy. She asked if he was eating and if he'd gotten the oatmeal cookies and if he needed socks.

Nick wrote back on government lined paper. Obstacle course. Rifle range. Ten-mile march. Blister the size of a quarter. Haywood from Detroit who sang Motown in the shower with a voice that belonged somewhere else.

He did not mention the studio. He did not mention Jackie Marsh. He did not mention the nights he lay awake after playing and heard eleven minutes of music in a room the size of his mother's living room — music that worked, music that wasn't enough, music that lived somewhere now without his name on the door.

The bargain was the bargain. Three years for tuition. Room. Board. A shot at becoming something Dan had never gotten the shot to become. Dan had traded his body for a mortgage and a garage full of tools and a red box he never opened again.

The bargain killed him.

Nick closed the footlocker. Set the combination. Stared at the ceiling until staring became sleep.

---

November. Jackie took the train to Boston.

Thanksgiving break. WBCN's hallway looked smaller in daylight — scuffed linoleum, coffee stains the color of old pennies. Maxanne was in Studio B setting up for the evening show, leather jacket over the back of her chair, cassette on the console with the leader pulled out a quarter inch.

"I wanted to say goodbye," Jackie said.

"You came from New York to say goodbye?"

"I was in town."

"How's Juilliard?"

"Fine. Different." He paused. "We don't talk."

Maxanne's eyes narrowed. "We?"

"Nick and me. Different worlds."

Silence that tasted like tape oxide.

"You heard from him?" she asked.

"No."

She looked at him a long moment. Looked at the cassette. Looked back.

"The music you made together—"

"Was a long time ago," Jackie said.

Eight months since the tie. He said it the way people say *a long time ago* when they mean *a distance I've put between myself and something I can't afford to look at directly*.

His eyes moved to the metal shelving behind the console — seven-inch reels spine-out, dates in black marker. He looked a second longer than a glance. Looked away.

"I've got a train to catch," he said.

He rode back to New York. Practiced Debussy. Did not play anything in D minor. Slept in nine by eleven and dreamed in dimensions he couldn't measure.

---

Sudbury kept going.

The Battle poster came down in Wentworth's music room and was replaced by the fall concert schedule. Hop Brook ran clear through October. Store 24 on Route 217 sold cigarettes and bait and did not stock a single record with both Aerosmith and Queen on the label.

Grace worked doubles. Dr. Marsh drove to Boston for department meetings. Patty rode the blue Schwinn on the conservation trails and sang to the pines when nobody was listening.

Nick and Jackie did not share a stage again.

They did not share a garage. They did not share a studio. They shared a town until one of them left and then they shared a silence that covered everything — the tie, the contract, the eleven minutes Maxanne labeled in block letters and filed where boys who chose their people would never think to look.

If you leave here tomorrow, the song had asked from a Trans Am in a graduation parking lot.

They left.

Not together. Not on the same road. Not with the thing they'd built still burning in the same room.

The town called it a tie and moved on. The boys called it choosing and did not call it anything at all where anyone could hear.
