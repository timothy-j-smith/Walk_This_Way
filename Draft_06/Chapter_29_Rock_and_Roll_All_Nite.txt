CHAPTER 29: "ROCK AND ROLL ALL NITE"


---

Tuesday, May 15, 1979. One day to the Battle.

Lincoln-Sudbury had a fever.

Not the kind the nurse measured with a thermometer under the tongue — the kind that lived in the hallways, in the way kids walked faster between bells, in the volume of nothing important turned up to maximum. Posters for May sixteenth had multiplied overnight. One on the music-room corkboard. One above the milk coolers. One in the glass corridor where the Rats smoked between classes and pretended not to read anything official. The Xerox toner still smelled faintly of ozone, as if the paper itself were warm.

Mr. Wentworth's door stayed open. Inside, his clipboard had become a battle map — eight band names, eight time slots, pencil marks crossed out and rewritten until the graphite shone. He sat at his desk with the metronome that didn't work and the Jordan Hall program in the drawer and the expression of a man who had traded symphony halls for this and was not sure which had been the sacrifice.

Patty Lally came through the east hallway with her biology textbook and her canvas bag and the melody that lived in her throat whether she wanted it or not. She had not slept much. Rumours had run the set at Memorial Congregational until Reverend Goff's nine o'clock curfew — Rhiannon twice, then Dreams, then a stumble through Silver Springs that Robin made right on the fourth pass. Her fingers still felt the steel strings in her pockets when she wasn't playing.

The cafeteria at lunch was geography turned vertical.

Nick Johnson's table near the windows — Stephen Murphy with his chocolate milk, David Cassel drumming the formica, Mark Pianka arguing about a Rhodes buzz on the B below middle C. Nick sat with his tray untouched and his jaw set, guitar case propped against the radiator like a rifle he didn't plan to put down. He had lost weight. The boy from Elbanobscot was still in there somewhere, cheekbones and all, buried under two years and a garage that smelled like Dan.

Jackie Marsh's table on the platform — Crown Jewels in blazer and jeans, Elizabeth Chen running a passage on her violin without the bow touching the strings, just fingerings in the air. Jackie sat with the posture of a conductor waiting for a downbeat that wouldn't come until tomorrow night. His eyes did not cross the forty feet of linoleum. Neither did Nick's.

Patty sat with Kristy and Robin by the milk coolers and ate her apple and watched the room divide itself along lines that had nothing to do with Hop Brook and everything to do with a poster Maxanne Sartori had pitched in the winter of '78.

"Whole school's gone stupid," Steve Bankuti said, sliding onto the bench with a bag of chips and a grin that didn't reach his eyes. "My sister asked me who I was voting for. Like it's an election."

"It's not an election," Kristy said.

"Tell them that."

---

The between-class crush at eleven-fifteen moved like cattle through a chute — four hundred kids in six minutes, shoulder to shoulder, sneakers squeaking, the air thick with body spray and bubble gum and voices saying nothing important at maximum volume.

Patty pressed against the lockers to let a knot of Rampers pass. Her combination stuck since February; she had the textbook in her left arm and the canvas bag on her right hip.

Nick Johnson came from the other direction.

Walking fast. Guitar case slung over his shoulder like a rifleman carrying his weapon, head down, the set of his jaw saying *don't talk to me* and the tension in his shoulders saying *I dare you to try*. He passed within arm's reach.

Patty hummed.

Not deliberately — the way other people cracked knuckles. Five notes from the opening of "Go Your Own Way," a reflex, not a message. The melody left her throat before she could swallow it.

Nick stopped.

One step past her. Frozen. His shoulders tightened, loosened, tightened again — micro-movements of a body fighting a memory it didn't want to acknowledge. He turned his head, not all the way, just enough to show his profile against the fluorescent light. Something in the phrase had snagged him — a lake, pine trees, a girl's voice finding the space between his and Jackie's, a week when music hadn't been a war.

He looked at Patty. She looked back.

For one second the hallway noise fell away and they stood in the gap between past and present — two people who knew what the other sounded like at thirteen, what a voice did when it wasn't trying to prove anything. Recognition faint as a shape through clouded glass.

Then Nick blinked. Shook his head, half a motion, like shaking off a chill. He walked on. Guitar case bouncing against his hip. Gone around the corner before Patty could exhale.

She watched him go. Lockers slammed. Somebody laughed too loud about something that wasn't funny.

Patty leaned against the cool metal and let the melody die. Five notes. Unplanned. But they'd landed — somewhere behind Nick's eyes, in the part of his brain that remembered what his body had decided to forget.

Whether it mattered was another question.

She hefted the biology book and walked to class.

---

Third period, the parking lot.

Somebody's Trans Am had the windows down and the radio up — KISS, "Rock and Roll All Nite," the chorus bleeding through the open windows of the auto-shop bay where Mr. Pacenka pretended not to hear it. Two juniors by the vending machines mouthed the words without getting the irony right. Patty walked past on her way to the side door and heard the hook one more time before the car pulled out toward Route 117 — *party every day* — and the sound thinned to tire hiss on asphalt.

The town was tuning itself. Some kids for volume. Some for precision. Rumours for something else entirely.

---

Last period. 2:45 PM. Wentworth's music room.

Patty had come back for the guitar picks she'd left in the instrument cupboard. The hallway was empty — after-school clubs migrating toward the gym, where custodians were already stacking chairs in the auditorium and testing the microphone with a tap that echoed through the whole east wing.

She opened the door and heard the piano.

Jackie Marsh sat at the upright in the corner — not the Steinway in the band room, the scarred practice piano Wentworth kept for students who needed to work through something the Steinway was too pristine to hold. Jackie's blazer was draped over the bench beside him. His sleeves were rolled to his elbows. His hands moved with the precision ten thousand dollars in lessons bought — but something else was happening in the music. Something the lessons hadn't taught.

"Somebody to Love."

Not performing it. Not rehearsing it for tomorrow. Playing it the way a person writes in a journal — private, unpolished, honest. The runs weren't clean. He let notes ring too long or cut them too short, feeling his way through instead of executing. His left hand found the bass line by instinct, not chart, and the chord voicings were his own — darker than the original, stacked with dissonance that resolved into something aching and true.

He sang it quiet. Barely above the piano's volume. Not Freddie Mercury's operatic howl — Jackie's voice, stripped of the performance polish he wore like armor, raw and reaching.

Patty stood in the doorway. She didn't move. Didn't breathe loud enough to break the room's agreement with what Jackie was doing — the old walls and the fluorescents and the instrument lockers all holding still, offering the boy what the rest of his life refused: a space where he didn't have to be perfect.

The song ended. The last chord hung three seconds, then two, then one, then gone.

"That was real," Patty said.

Jackie spun on the bench. His hands came off the keys like they'd been burned. Eyes wide — not fear, exactly, but the wild exposure of someone caught without their costume.

"How long have you been standing there?"

"Long enough."

He stared at her. Mouth opened, closed, opened again. She watched the armor rebuild in real time — shoulders squaring, chin lifting, the careful construction of a boy taught that vulnerability was weakness and musicianship was measured in precision, not feeling.

Underneath it, in the second before the walls went up, she saw something she recognized. Elbanobscot. The music cabin. Queen when he thought nobody was listening. Shoulders dropping three inches. The same breath.

Freedom.

Patty turned to leave.

"Patty."

She stopped. Looked back over her shoulder.

Jackie sat on the piano bench with his hands in his lap. The armor was up, but it had a crack in it — a thin line from his throat to his chest where the song still lived.

"What are you doing here?"

"Getting my picks." She crossed to the cupboard, found the tortoiseshell Fenders worn smooth at the tips, put them in her jacket pocket.

At the door she stopped again. Not for drama. Because something in her wouldn't let her leave without saying it.

"You don't play like that when anyone's watching."

Jackie said nothing.

"You should."

The door closed behind her with a soft click. Her boots on the linoleum sounded like a slow heartbeat in the empty hall.

---

Five o'clock. Memorial Congregational Church, Sudbury Center.

The parking lot held three cars and a Chevy van with a rumble strip on the rear bumper. Steve was already inside, setting up on the chancel rug. Robin coiled cables from the last run. Kristy tuned by ear on the communion rail.

Patty shouldered her Martin through the side door — the D-28 her father had bought used in Framingham when she was fourteen, spruce top darkened with age, neck worn smooth where her thumb rode the back during chord changes.

"One more," she said. "Full run. Like tomorrow."

Steve counted in on the hi-hat. They ran Rhiannon into Dreams into the bridge of Silver Springs they kept botching and then, because Steve was seventeen and nervous and couldn't help himself, the opening riff of "Rock and Roll All Nite" — four bars on the snare rim, a joke, a pressure valve.

Kristy laughed into her hand. Robin shook his head but his foot kept time.

"Not on the setlist," Patty said.

"Thank God," Robin said.

They packed up at seven. Reverend Goff looked in once, nodded, said *I'll lock up after you*, and disappeared toward the parish office. Steve loaded his kick pedal into the Chevette with the hinge that whined past halfway. Kristy hugged Patty a little too long at the door — the way you hug somebody the night before something you can't take back.

"See you at the school," Kristy said.

"See you at the school."

Patty stood in the parking lot after the taillights went. The steeple caught the last sun. Sudbury Center was quiet — white clapboard, honor-roll glass, the town's ordinary Sunday face turned toward tomorrow's war.

She did not hum on the ride home. She listened to the wind off the Cavicchio fields instead, and to the engine of her mother's Plymouth, and to the silence that wasn't empty — the silence of a girl who had put her fingers on the fretboard and knew what she was for.

Tonight the town still held its breath.

Tomorrow the fever would break into noise.
