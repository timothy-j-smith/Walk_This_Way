ACT I: TOYS IN THE ATTIC

---

CHAPTER 10: "DREAM ON"

SUDBURY. May 15, 1975

Ephraim Curtis Middle School gymnasium. Seven-thirty.

Mr. Petrocelli had waxed the court that afternoon with the same lemon-industrial compound he used every Thursday. The room smelled like floor polish and the sourness of two hundred people under fluorescent lights on a warm spring evening. The lights hummed — a sixty-cycle drone you felt behind the eyes more than heard — flattening every color to institutional yellow-green. Programs still tacky from the Xerox. Parents fanning themselves. Fire doors propped open on May air that smelled like cut grass and warm asphalt and did nothing to cool the room.

Parents in their good clothes. Kids fidgeting. Teachers in the aisles shushing sixth-graders who were already throwing wadded programs.

Backstage: storage room, rubber kickballs, chalk dust. Karen Bowers in the corner climbing chromatic scales while her mother adjusted the collar of her navy dress for the third time. She would close with "Tomorrow" from Annie — the slot reserved for the voice that always won.

Nick and Jackie sat on a folding table near the emergency exit, acoustics across their thighs. Nick's pawn-shop Fender, crack in the pickguard. Jackie's birthday Gibson, frets worn smooth in the first position. Hands still. Waiting.

"You ready?" Nick asked.

Jackie pressed his thumb across the low E and let it go. A quiet thud. "Yeah."

In the audience, Dan Johnson still in work boots, concrete dust in the creases of his jeans — straight from the job site because Grace had told him he was coming. Grace beside him in her blue church dress, hands folded.

Six rows back, Dr. Richard Marsh in a gray suit, no tie. He'd come because Jackie asked, unusual enough that Eileen had noted it without comment. Beside them, Elizabeth read the program and had already found three spelling errors.

First acts: "Für Elise" on a piano tuned a quarter-tone flat. A boy whose card tricks kept slipping. Three girls whose harmony drifted like a distant station. A kid reaching for Jimmy Page and landing at a campfire in the Berkshires. Polite clapping after each.

Then Mr. Hennessey, the vice principal, read from an index card. "Next up — Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh."

They walked out carrying the guitars by the necks. No stands. No sheet music. Jeans and t-shirts — Nick in a faded Aerosmith shirt with a hole near the collar, Jackie in a plain white tee his mother had ironed that afternoon. Two chairs at center stage. Nick tilted his back on two legs the way he always did, then let it drop forward.

They looked at each other. Nick's eyebrows a fraction. Jackie nodded.

Nick's right hand came down.

The opening of "Walk This Way" hit the gymnasium like a sentence the room didn't know it spoke. Not the album — you couldn't get that from one acoustic. Joe Perry's riff stripped to rhythmic bone: muted downstrokes, hammer-ons, strings slapping the frets with a percussive crack that bounced off cinderblock and waxed floor and came back harder. Palm half-damping the bridge — short, punchy, mean — guitar turned into a drum that happened to have pitch.

Four bars in, Jackie entered.

Not with "Walk This Way." The descending chords from "Bohemian Rhapsody," transposed, fingerpicked — each note set the way you'd set glasses on a table. The chords dropped through the gaps in Nick's riff. Attack and percussion underneath; sustain and air above. For a beat — maybe two — the audience didn't know what it was hearing.

Then the patterns locked.

Fifth bar. Nick's riff on the downbeat, Jackie's chord on the upbeat — syncopation clicking like a latch into its strike plate. Forward drive and upward open at once. Two acoustics, one sound bigger and stranger than either song alone.

The woman three rows back stopped fanning her program and didn't notice she'd stopped.

Nick sang first — "Walk This Way" verses in a rough untrained voice that cracked on the high notes and didn't care, a kid who'd learned by singing along to records in a garage with the door closed. Into the mic too loud, the way Aerosmith was supposed to be too loud.

Jackie answered — not the next verse. "Is this the real life," clear and placed, diaphragm under each note the way his teacher had drilled, a choirboy's instrument carrying Freddie's question. A bell through traffic. Clean. High. Impossible to ignore.

Call and response they'd built in Dan's garage: Nick threw a line raw, Jackie sent it back polished, Nick roughed it up again.

Middle section — where the rehearsal showed. Jackie into the arpeggiated bridge of "Bohemian Rhapsody," ascending figures and dramatic pauses; Nick underneath, "Walk This Way" palm-muted to a whisper, pulse kept alive. Their voices on a harmony they'd invented — not Freddie's, not Tyler's — a major third splitting precision and abandon.

Then the crash. Both guitars open-strummed. Both voices on the final chorus — rasp and clarity fused — rattling the folding chairs, buzzing in the fluorescent casings, hitting the back wall and going on through the fire doors into the May evening.

They ended together. One chord, struck hard, left to ring.

Three seconds of silence. Nick glanced at Jackie. Jackie glanced at Nick.

Grace pressed both hands to her mouth. Eyes wet.

Then the gymnasium broke open — not applause, noise. Parents standing. Kids yelling. Mr. Hennessey clapping over his head, which no one had seen him do. Mrs. Stephens — the librarian who'd shushed them a thousand times — on her feet with her fist in the air.

Six rows back, Dr. Richard Marsh did not stand. He sat very still. His left hand opened once in his lap — thumb and pinky spanning an octave the hand had not been asked to hold in a very long time — and closed again. Elizabeth stood on her chair, clapping, program on the floor.

Karen Bowers sang "Tomorrow." Every note on time. Technically irreproachable. The audience clapped — polite, appreciative.

The judges were out eleven minutes.

"First place — Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh."

They walked back on grinning, guitars in hand, and the applause found them again. Dan on his feet, work boots on the waxed floor, clapping the slow hard way of a man who built things with those hands and knew solid work when he heard it. Grace beside him, mascara ruined, laughing and crying at once.

Someone had a Polaroid.

Two thirteen-year-old boys, arms around each other's shoulders, guitars at their sides. Nick's grin enormous, crooked, Aerosmith shirt riding up. Jackie's smile quieter but total — the whole face. Behind them the banner in hand-painted letters on butcher paper: EPHRAIM CURTIS MIDDLE SCHOOL ANNUAL TALENT SHOW 1975. Folding chairs askew. Programs on the floor.

Dan drove the Johnsons home. Windows down. Lilac from someone's yard on Dutton Road. Nick in the back with his guitar case and the first-place ribbon, talking — the riff, the lock, Karen Bowers' face. Grace reached back and squeezed his knee once. Dan watched the road and said nothing; in the rearview his eyes were bright.

At the Marsh house Jackie came through the door still holding the ribbon. Elizabeth said, "You were the best thing that's ever happened in that gym, including basketball," and went upstairs.

Dr. Marsh was in his study. Jackie stood in the doorway.

"Your harmonics were good," his father said quietly. "The voicing in the bridge. That was sophisticated work."

Jackie didn't move. His father had never commented on his rock arrangements. Not once.

"Goodnight, Jackie."

"Goodnight, Dad."

Jackie sat on his bed with the ribbon and the window open to the May night. Down the street, through the trees, the slow tock-tock-tock of someone's sprinkler. He held the fifth bar in his head — the lock — and Nick's face when the silence broke.

On Dutton Road, Nick lay with his hands behind his head, still hearing it. In the kitchen Dan and Grace talked low — her high laugh, his low one.

The Polaroid on Nick's desk, still tacky, colors still deepening. Two boys. Two guitars. Behind them the gymnasium already emptying — chairs stacked, banner coming down.

In the photograph they are still up there.

May 15, 1975. A Thursday.
