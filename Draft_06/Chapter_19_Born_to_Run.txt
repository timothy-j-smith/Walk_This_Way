CHAPTER 19: "BORN TO RUN"

---

The L-S cafeteria smelled like fish sticks and canned tomato sauce and the plastic tang of trays still hot from the dishwasher. Friday menu. Above the steam tables a fluorescent ballast hummed at a frequency Nick's ear sorted, against his will, into a flat B-natural — the note it had hummed at every lunch for four years, the school's own pedal tone. He had his back to the cinder block wall and a tuna sandwich in wax paper Grace had made at five-forty that morning before the pickup left for Friendly's on Route 20.

Stephen Murphy sat across from him with a chocolate milk. Mark Pianka drummed the edge of the formica with two fingers, unaware, the way he drummed everything. Three weeks since the Battle of the Bands poster had gone up, and the cafeteria had sorted itself without anyone moving a chair. Jackie Marsh's table was two rows over on the raised platform — the college-bound kids, the ones who said SAT the way other kids said Budweiser.

Carl Marquis had a Panasonic boom box on the bench beside him, volume low, the tape inside playing "Sweet Emotion" through a speaker the size of a lunch tray. Against policy. Nobody enforced it on Fridays when the lunch monitors were counting fish sticks. The bass line walked under the roar of three hundred voices and Nick's ear followed it the way his ear followed everything that sounded like his side of the argument.

Jackie stood up.

The scrape of his chair on linoleum went through the room like a struck cymbal. Carl's finger came off the pause button without thinking. The Aerosmith tape died mid-riff. A hush traveled outward in rings — first the kids who had been facing the platform, then the tables alongside, then the back benches where the Ramper girls sat, one of them mid-sentence with her hand lifted. Her hand stayed lifted. Stephen Murphy set his chocolate milk down very carefully, the way you set down a glass you've just learned was full of something breakable.

"'Somebody to Love,'" Jackie said.

He sang.

In the music room his voice had a ceiling. Here it didn't. The cinder block threw everything back at harder angles — a slap rather than a bloom — and Jackie rode the slap, Freddie Mercury through a kid trained since he was eight, operatic at the top, and here and there the trained edge frayed because he was seventeen and because you cannot really sing Queen at lunch without the frayed edge showing. David Bellenghi, beside him in a polo shirt, fumbled into the harmony a half-beat late. The fish-stick steam rose past Jackie in the light. A lunch lady came to the kitchen doorway holding a pair of tongs and did not move again.

When Jackie hit the last high one he had to push, but he got there, and the note held against the block walls and came back at him from the other side.

Nick set down his sandwich and stood up.

"'Dream On,'" he said.

No guitar. Nothing in his hands. His throat was dry. He swallowed and his ear found the note from the hum in the ballast above him and he stepped onto it like a man stepping onto a porch. Stephen Murphy came in under him, third below, rough — not good, honest. Mark Pianka flattened his hands on the formica and tapped the opening figure of the piano part, right hand arpeggio against the tabletop, fingers finding keys that were not there.

Carl hit his tray once with the edge of a fork — one sharp ring on the downbeat, then he stopped, embarrassed, and went back to holding the fork like a fork.

Nick sang. His voice was not trained. It went where he had dragged it — out past a closed lid on a red toolbox and back, past a hundred nights in a garage learning what his fingers could be made to do. On the long word — *dream* — the cafeteria went still. He went up for the top of it — *dream until your dreams come true* — and he pushed, and he got there, and the high note was thinner than Jackie's and rawer than Jackie's and nobody in that cafeteria mistook it for anything but what it was.

The lunch lady with the tongs still had not moved.

Then the noise came — not choreographed, not a number, just three hundred teenagers who had been holding their breath and couldn't anymore. Lockers would slam in the hall. Somebody would tell somebody else. That was later.

"Sit down." Mr. Wentworth stood at the end of the shop-corridor tables, hands in the pockets of his corduroy jacket, the music teacher's face set in the expression of a man trying not to grin. "Both of you. Principal's office. Now."

---

Brad Sargent had been principal at L-S for twenty years and vice principal for eight before that. His office had a gray metal desk and a carpet the color of wet oatmeal that smelled like thirty years of industrial solvent laid over thirty years of whatever the solvent was laid over. On the desk sat a framed photograph of two kids in a canoe, a pen cup of Bic Clics, and a white ceramic coffee mug that read KEEFE TECH COUGARS — BASEBALL '63 in green letters faded at the rim where his thumb had gone a thousand times.

Nick sat in one chair. Jackie sat in the other. The chairs were molded plastic and exactly the same height.

"I have reports," Sargent said. He looked at a manila folder on his desk and did not open it. "Kids skipping class to rehearse. An argument in C-wing last Thursday. Mrs. Fiore says she can't get through homeroom announcements because of you." He tipped the mug an inch, set it back. "And now I hear singing in the cafeteria."

"We were —" Jackie began.

"I don't want to know what you were." Sargent leaned back. The chair creaked. A fluorescent tube in the ceiling ticked like a cheap watch. "I got a fire marshal who signed off on the auditorium for May sixteenth. I got a station calling my office about a sponsorship. I got a superintendent who wants to know why his senior class is using the cafeteria as a rehearsal hall." He looked at Nick. He looked at Jackie. "You want me to cancel it?"

"No, sir," they said. Same word. Same moment.

Sargent almost smiled.

"Then off school property. You want to sing at each other, do it somewhere my secretary can't hear it. You have until May sixteenth. After that I don't care what you do." He reached for the mug. "Get out of my office."

---

In the hallway the lockers were pale green and their sneakers did not quite squeak on the waxed floor. They walked side by side because the hallway was narrow. Jackie's shoulder was an inch from Nick's shoulder and did not touch it.

Near the milk coolers, Patty Lally watched them pass with her canvas bag on her hip and her apple half-eaten. She did not wave. She had heard every note from where she sat — the Queen slap off the block walls, the raw *dream* that wasn't thinner to her, just true — and she knew what the hallway was going to do to them before they reached the doors.

"It's on," Nick said.

"Set list coming?"

"Yeah. Yours?"

"Yeah."

They reached the fork in the corridor. Jackie pushed left toward the arts wing. Nick pushed right toward the shop doors. Forty feet of linoleum again, only vertical this time.
