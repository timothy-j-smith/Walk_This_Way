CHAPTER 15: "LANDSLIDE"

Grace was up at five-thirty. She filled the coffee pot and set it on the burner and stood at the counter while the house was still dark around her — November dark, the kind that pressed against the kitchen windows like it wanted in.

She made Dan's lunch the way she made it every morning. Tuna on white bread, the can opened and drained and mixed with mayonnaise and a little celery salt, the way Dan's mother had made it for Dan's father in this same kitchen for forty years before Grace ever stood at this counter. She spread the tuna with a butter knife, her hands moving without thought — knuckles swollen from hot water and fryer grease, the skin across the backs cracked in fine white lines like the glaze on old china. She wrapped the sandwich in wax paper, folded the edges tight, wrapped it again in aluminum foil. An apple. Two oatmeal cookies from Sunday night. Brown paper bag. Thermos of coffee, black.

Dan came down at six-fifteen. Work boots, jeans, flannel shirt. He poured coffee, drank half of it standing at the counter, set the mug in the sink.

"Let him sleep," he said, meaning Nick, who was upstairs after staying up past midnight in the garage working a chord change he couldn't get clean. "He's got trig first period."

Grace nodded. She was wiping the counter where the tuna can had left a ring of oil.

Dan picked up the lunch bag and the thermos. He kissed her — coffee on his mouth, chin rough because he never shaved before work, sawdust in his collar even fresh from the shower, the smell had gotten into his skin years ago the way fryer oil was in hers. "Home by six," he said.

"Drive safe," she said.

The truck's headlights moved down Dutton Road and disappeared where the road bent toward Route 20. She rinsed the tuna can and poured herself coffee and sat at the table while the sky went from black to gray. A Tuesday. Ordinary.

---

Dan had a 9/16ths socket wrench in his back pocket the way other men carried a wallet. Nineteen years in the ironworkers' union, never a lost-time injury, never a citation. He was on a tower job in the financial district — steel going up between the Old State House and the harbor, the crews called it by the street address the way crews always did. He was in the construction trailer below the thirty-first floor, blueprints spread on a plywood desk, the wax paper from Grace's sandwich peeled back, the tuna half-eaten beside his thermos.

At 10:17 AM a crane cable snapped.

The sound reached the men on the ground half a second after the weight already had. Four steel I-beams came down. The roof of the trailer came apart like paper — plywood, blueprints, the wax paper Grace had folded around his sandwich six hours before, all of it gone under thirty tons of steel before anyone had time to run.

---

The call came to Friendly's at 11:47.

Grace was on the lunch line, apron pockets full of Sweet 'N Low and folded green guest checks, pencil behind her ear, name tag reading GRACE in the typeface they'd used since Eisenhower. Tom Andrews came out of the back office and stood at the edge of the dining room with a look on his face that made her hands stop moving.

She followed him to the office. The phone receiver was on the desk. She picked it up. Her hands — the same hands that had wrapped wax paper six hours ago — went white around the plastic.

A man's voice. Boston Construction. An accident. Your husband. I'm sorry.

Tom drove her to the hospital. Grace sat in the passenger seat still in her uniform, name tag crooked over her left breast, and looked out at Route 9 and the strip malls and the trees bare now, every branch visible against the white sky.

---

At Lincoln-Sudbury, Nick was in trigonometry.

Mrs. Delano was at the board, chalk tapping in a rhythm his brain sorted into music anyway because that was what his brain did. The radiator clanked. A pencil rolled off a desk and hit the floor with a sound like a rim shot on a snare.

The classroom door opened.

Mrs. Patterson stood in the doorway — low-heeled shoes the color of oatmeal, gray cardigan buttoned to the neck — and did not look at the class.

"Nick Johnson? Could you come with me, please?"

The hallway was empty and bright. Fluorescent tubes buzzing at sixty cycles. Linoleum reflecting light in a long pale stripe. Mrs. Patterson's shoes clicked. Nick's sneakers made no sound. He followed her past the trophy case, past the gym doors, past the water fountain with its permanent puddle.

Grace was on the brown couch in the principal's office. Still in her Friendly's uniform. Her face was a color Nick had never seen on a living person.

"Mom?"

"Sit down, Nick."

He sat.

"There was an accident at Daddy's job. A cable broke." She was looking at a point somewhere past his left shoulder, outside the building, outside the town. "Some beams fell. He's gone, Nick."

He looked at her hands in her lap. Cracked knuckles. Fryer burns. Wedding ring.

---

Closed casket. The doctor used the word trauma the way you'd use a term in a manual. Grace signed papers. Date of birth. Social security number. Next of kin.

Nick sat in a plastic chair in a hallway that smelled like floor cleaner and something underneath that floor cleaner existed to cover up. He counted the ceiling tiles. Sixty-four. He counted them again. Sixty-four.

Tom Andrews drove them home from the hospital that first day. Grace went into the bedroom and closed the door — slow, precise, the latch settling. Nick stood in the kitchen where six hours ago his father had been alive, drinking coffee, saying let him sleep. The mug was still in the sink. Nick picked it up. It was cold. He washed it and put it on the drying rack and stood there with the water running over his hands until the water went cold too.

---

The funeral was Saturday. St. Mary's. Union men from the site sat in the back pews in their one good suit, hands in their laps.

Grace received every handshake without moving her mouth. Nick stood beside her in Dan's old sport coat, shoulders too wide, sleeves past his wrists, and shook every hand offered.

Jackie came with his parents. He stood at the back after the service. When Nick finally saw him, Jackie didn't say anything. Nick nodded once. Six feet of candle wax and chrysanthemums between them.

---

Three days after the funeral, Nick went into the garage.

The door had swollen with November damp, and Nick had to lean his shoulder into it low and hard, the way Dan used to, and for a second the wood gave under his weight like it remembered his father better than he did.

The garage smelled like sawdust. Dan's tools hung on the pegboard where he had placed them, each one outlined in black marker, the socket set open in its case with an empty slot where the 9/16ths used to live. Nick's guitar sat in its case on the milk crate in the corner. He didn't touch it.

Dan's red metal toolbox sat on the workbench, heavy and scarred. Nick had never opened it.

He opened it.

The top tray held what you'd expect — wrenches, screwdrivers, tape measure, a roll of electrical tape gone stiff with age. Nick lifted the tray and set it on the bench.

Underneath, in the bottom of the box, he found what he did not expect.

Three guitar picks, Fender mediums, tortoiseshell, worn smooth at the edges. A stack of handwritten sheet music on staff paper — Dan's block print, notes drawn with the same precision he used on blueprints. "Wish You Were Here." "Landslide." Chords in pencil, fingering in the margins — capo third fret, hammer-on to the suspended fourth. A pawn ticket, yellow, folded once, the ink gone pale.

Under the music, a spiral notebook, cover soft with handling, wire binding bent from being shoved into the box a hundred times. Nick opened it.

Pages of chord progressions in Dan's hand — not copied, original, moving from major to minor in ways that surprised Nick's ear even reading them off the page. A verse crossed out and rewritten and crossed out again:

    Sawdust hands and a borrowed name
    The house remembers what I became

Nick sat on the concrete floor with the notebook in his lap and the picks in his palm. He could hear Dan in the progressions the way he'd heard him through the bedroom wall at thirteen — patient, unhurried, playing after the house decided everyone was asleep. He could hear him in the garage at six-forty, pressing Nick's ring finger flat against the fret wire, saying *feel that*, saying *there, you had it*.

When Grace found him an hour later, he was holding the notebook open to the chord chart for "Landslide."

"You knew," Nick said.

"He played after I went to sleep." She leaned against the doorframe. Her voice was quiet. "I'd hear it through the floor. He was good, Nick."

"Why didn't he—"

"Because we needed the money." She wiped her eyes with the back of her hand. "He gave it to you instead."

Grace reached over and closed the toolbox lid. The latch clicked shut.

The empty slot in the socket set caught the light from the bare bulb overhead.
