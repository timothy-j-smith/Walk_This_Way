ACT I: TOYS IN THE ATTIC

---

CHAPTER 3: “KILLER QUEEN”

On the other side of Hop Brook, Jackie Marsh woke at 6:47 AM — three minutes before his alarm, the way he always did. The walls were painted a color his mother called Dove Gray. The bookshelves held alphabetized volumes he'd actually read. The Baldwin in the corner waited with its lid shut, sheet music stacked on the bench in performance order — Chopin, Bach, Debussy, Rachmaninoff, Mozart to close.

Everything had its place. The tuxedo hung pressed on the closet door for tonight's recital. His electric guitar — the one his father pretended not to know about — was in its case under the bed, untouched since Christmas.

He sat up and looked at the framed photo on his nightstand: himself at seven, hands on piano keys, his mother behind him with the smile of a woman who'd finally gotten something right. The photo had won a prize in a parenting magazine — *Classical Excellence: Nurturing Your Child's Musical Gift*. Jackie remembered the photographer making him hold the position twenty minutes while they adjusted the lighting.

Through his bedroom window he could see toward Dutton Road — not that he was looking. Not that he was thinking about someone who used to be his friend before high school taught them they belonged in different worlds.

Tonight he would sit for his parents' friends and his father's colleagues and play what he'd been trained to play since he was five. Everyone would applaud with their fingertips. Dr. Marsh would accept the compliments the way a man accepts tributes.

Somewhere under all that, a kid still believed Freddie Mercury understood something Mozart never did.

---

The same week Maxanne Sartori hauled up a stuck garage door on Dutton Road, Dr. Richard Marsh called WBCN personally — rare for a man who usually delegated to a secretary.

"Jackie trained at New England Conservatory prep," he said, each syllable polished free of wherever he'd started. "We're being considered for early admission to Juilliard. I'd like you in the room tonight."

Classical recitals were not her beat. She'd have said no — except something in his voice matched Grace Johnson's *don't fill his head with dreams we can't afford*. Different words. Same fear that music could ruin a child.

---

By seven o'clock the Marsh house had been transformed. Folding chairs in rows. A rented Steinway grand that cost more than Grace Johnson made in a year. Champagne in silver buckets. Prep-school parents and university faculty — people who discussed summer houses on the Cape between selections.

Colonial perfection: black shutters, white clapboard, a lawn that would green up by April. Twelve minutes from Nick Johnson's garage if you walked straight. Hop Brook made it farther.

Jackie stood in the upstairs bathroom, adjusting his bow tie for the third time. His hands were steady. His mind was not.

---

He entered the living room at 7:30 PM in a tuxedo tailored to fit, seventeen years old, carrying himself like someone who'd been preparing since birth. When he sat at the Steinway, something changed.

Chopin's Nocturne in E-flat major. Fingers finding keys with thousands of hours behind them — lessons before memory, parents who considered anything less than excellence a character flaw. The notes flowed, technically perfect, each phrase shaped exactly as instructed.

Maxanne watched his face. Behind the perfection, he was dying in there.

The audience nodded, already composing compliments. Dr. Marsh sat front row, posture radiating satisfaction.

Bach — Goldberg excerpts. Debussy's "Clair de Lune." Rachmaninoff's Prelude in C-sharp minor.

Each piece perfect. Each piece empty.

Between Rachmaninoff and the closing Mozart, Jackie paused. Too long. Dr. Marsh cleared his throat — recital pacing, not encouragement.

Jackie wasn't listening to his father. His fingers found different keys.

The opening of "Bohemian Rhapsody" came out of that Steinway like something that had been locked in the bench for years.

The room froze. You could hear pearls clutching themselves.

"Is this the real life?" he sang, a classically trained voice hitting notes that would have made Freddie Mercury grin. His left hand found a bass line that rattled the frame. His right hand stacked harmonies no solo piano should have been able to hold — eleven years of lessons finally aimed at something he chose.

The champagne glasses on the sideboard vibrated. Dr. Marsh's water glass hummed on its coaster. He played past the record's ending, past what the room could comfortably absorb, until Jackie Marsh was the only person in the house worth listening to.

When he stopped, nobody clapped. Nobody moved.

Dr. Marsh was the first to speak. "That was... unexpected."

Maxanne stood. She didn't trust herself to say more.

Jackie looked at her — really looked — the same thing she'd seen in a garage three days earlier, when a boy flinched at his father's tools and played anyway.

"You're Maxanne Sartori," he said. "You play real rock and roll."

She gave him the same card she'd given Nick. WBCN, 104.1 FM. "Come by the station. Bring whatever you've got."

Jackie took it. His hands were still shaking. He looked at the card, then at his father's face — rearranged into something Jackie had never seen. Not anger. Not pride. Something older.

Dr. Marsh's right hand moved in his lap. Pinky and ring finger curled inward once against his thumb — an unconscious reach, the shape the hand had made on keys decades ago, before the lid on his own music had shut.

"Thank you," Jackie said.

Mrs. Marsh moved through the room with champagne, deflecting questions, doing what she'd been trained to do when the social order cracked.

Maxanne walked to her Karmann Ghia. Behind her, through the lit windows of the Marsh house, the rented Steinway still held the last chord in its wires.
