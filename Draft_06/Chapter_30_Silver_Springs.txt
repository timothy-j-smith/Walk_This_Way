CHAPTER 30: "SILVER SPRINGS"

---

Tuesday night, May 15, 1979. A little after nine.

The last car door in the Memorial Congregational parking lot had been Steve Bankuti's Chevette — the driver-side hinge whining past halfway — and Patty had counted it the way she counted beats, and then she'd been alone.

Rumours had packed up an hour ago. Kristy's amp. Robin's bass in its gig bag. Steve's kick pedal and snare in their padded cases. They'd hugged her at the side door the way you hug somebody the night before surgery — a little too long and too careful — and gone home to their mothers and their own nerves.

The sanctuary was what it always was on a Tuesday night when the town wasn't looking: empty.

Patty had moved the guitar in on purpose. The Martin D-28 her father had bought used in Framingham when she was fourteen — spruce top darkened with age, neck worn smooth where her thumb rode the back during chord changes. She took it out and sat in a front pew with the case open beside her and tuned by ear.

E. Flat. A hair on the tuning key. The string came up under her thumbnail with the small sweet creak of bronze against bone nut. A. Fine. D. Fine. G — sharp; she gave it back. B. B was always the one. She tuned it twice. High E. Fine.

Jeans with barn mud dried on the left knee. Flannel over her Rumours T-shirt. Hair braided over the right shoulder. The back of her left hand had a pink line from barbed wire last Tuesday. On her right forearm, a hoof step going yellow at the edges. She smelled faintly of pig. She knew it. She was not going to apologize for it to a room full of Sudbury names in gold leaf.

She walked to the votive rack by the side wall — red glass jars on a black iron frame, most unlit on a weekday. She struck a long match. The head caught with that small chemical pop. The flame found a wick in the second row. The wax made a tiny suck of air taking the fire, and the red glass around it went alive.

She did not say who it was for. She never said.

Above the chancel, the honor-roll window held the town's dead in lead seams — ordinary dead and war dead together, red and blue light gone dark without the sun behind it. Sunday's incense still hung in the wood. Melted wax. Worn oak. The brass plaques on the wall caught nothing but the votive's flicker.

The hymnal was red. She rolled it back and forth along her thigh, pebbled leatherette on denim, a soft dry sound.

She opened the camp notebook.

The letters were inside the back cover. Seven of them, folded small, paper gone soft at the creases. Four from Nick. Three from Jackie.

She took Nick's top one and opened it across her knee.

The handwriting was cramped and sideways-slanted, like he was running out of room even though the page was half empty. In the third line he had crossed out *my dad* and written *my father* above it and then crossed that out too and put *him*. Three tries on two letters. The pen had pressed so hard on the last one that the paper had dimpled through to the back.

She folded it. She slid it back.

Jackie's top letter was thinner paper, the kind with faint blue lines. The handwriting went straight across the page like a prayer. Every *t* crossed. Every *i* dotted. At the bottom, where he had written *I am learning the C-minor prelude and I can almost make it breathe*, he had underlined *almost* once, careful, with a ruler.

Her thumb rode the crease. She put it back.

Nick's cramped urgent fingers. Jackie's ruler.

She thought about Nick in the hallway today — five notes, a head turn, a shake, gone. She thought about Jackie at the practice piano with his sleeves rolled up, singing *Somebody to Love* to an empty room that wasn't empty.

She let the letters sit. She did not want to sort them anymore. She was not going to fix two boys by reading hard enough.

---

She pulled the guitar back onto her thigh and played a D. Then a G. She slid to a C; her ring finger missed the third fret and buzzed against the second, and she laughed — out loud, in the empty sanctuary, one small startled bark. The honor-roll window did not move. She played the C again and this time it rang.

Then, without meaning to, she was humming.

It came up out of her the way weather came up out of a field — the shape of *Silver Springs*, not the words, the step-down at the end of the line, her right hand finding a quiet bass note underneath because the song wanted it and her hand knew.

Camp Elbanobscot. Four summers ago. Lake going copper at the end of the day. Nick's guitar out of tune and neither of them caring. Jackie finding a suspended chord nobody had asked for, a fourth hanging in the air, and her voice coming in over the top without thinking — lake water on the dock pilings, sap-hot pine, three voices and two guitars and nobody keeping count. They had not been trying. That was why it worked. They had been breathing, and the breathing had happened to rhyme.

She hummed another bar. She was on the turn the song wouldn't give her cleanly — the place between *you* and *isn't* — when a car door went in the parking lot. Short. Metal. Far side.

Her thumb went still on the G string. The car started. The car pulled out. It wasn't for her.

Her hand went into her canvas bag and found the little white Panasonic transistor her brother had given her two Christmases ago, antenna bent from too many glove compartments. She almost turned it on. WBCN would be where she'd left it; it was always where she'd left it. She took her hand out of the bag. Not tonight.

She tuned the B string one more time because the B string was always the one.

She played the opening of the song — the first four bars, for herself — and let it die against the stone. The note went up into the rafters and came back thinner, the church holding it the way the meadow had held the triad, the way the lake had held the dock.

She put the guitar in the case and latched it.

The votive was still going. The red glass was warm when she passed a hand near it, not touching. At the door she dipped two fingers in the font and crossed herself the way her mother had taught her — quick, four points, sternum and shoulders — without closing her eyes.

She pushed the door open with her hip, guitar case in her right hand, notebook in her left. The night air smelled of cut grass and diesel off Route 20 and, faintly from the southeast, home.

Behind her the little orange flower kept burning inside its red glass. The hymnal sat on the pew with the print of her thumb on the cover.

She let the door close on its soft seal.

She walked to her mother's Plymouth at the curb.

Tomorrow she would stand on a stage with eight bands and a town that had turned music into sides. Nick with grief and duct tape. Jackie with precision and a blazer. She could not save them.

She could play *Silver Springs* and mean it.

The Plymouth's engine turned over on the third try. She drove home past the Cavicchio fields with the notebook on the seat beside her and the guitar in back and did not turn the radio on.

The stars were out. The sow pens were quiet.

She wasn't nervous. She wasn't hopeful.

She was ready.
