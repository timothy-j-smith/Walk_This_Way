CHAPTER 38: "SWEET EMOTION"

---

Thursday they passed in the east hallway without speaking. Friday Jackie sat in Dr. Holloway's studio with the Juilliard envelope unopened on the bench and did not play the Battle set. Nick fixed a screen door on Dutton Road for Mrs. Connolly and sanded until his hands went numb.

Saturday was three days after the tie. May nineteenth. Four o'clock.

---

Nick drove the wagon himself.

Route 20 east through Weston, then the Jamaicaway, then Boylston into the city — forty minutes with the timing off in third and the radio scanning static because WBCN was local and he was almost there. He parked in the lot behind the station with twenty minutes to spare and sat with both hands on the wheel and the pawn-shop guitar in its case on the passenger seat.

Jackie was already on the sidewalk when he climbed out.

Peacoat collar up. Hands in pockets. Breath visible in the May air that wasn't cold but felt cold anyway. Stephen's truck was pulling away from the curb — two fingers off the wheel, gone.

They didn't say hello. They walked in together because the contract said together.

---

The hallway smelled like tape oxide and old cigarettes. Maxanne stood under the WBCN sign with her arms crossed and her leather jacket zipped to her throat — less radio legend than a woman who had bet something she couldn't afford to lose.

"You showed," she said. "That's the hard part."

She led them down the hall without looking back. Boot heels on linoleum. Nick carried the guitar case. Jackie carried nothing — the piano was already in the room.

---

Studio C was the size of Grace's living room.

Nick had imagined palatial — walls of equipment, a board the size of a car. Instead: acoustic panels like quilted armor. Two boom mics four feet apart. A window into the control room where a kid in a Red Sox cap sat behind a board that was, in fact, the size of a small car.

"Mike," Maxanne said, knocking the glass. "They're here."

Wire-rims. Enthusiasm like heat off an engine. Thumbs-up. Switches — red, green, amber — the room humming awake.

"Where's the amp?" Nick said.

"DI box." Maxanne pointed at a small metal rectangle by the right mic, cable to the wall. "Plug in. Mike takes the tone off the board."

"Never played through a DI."

"Today you have."

Against the far wall: a Yamaha U3 upright — black lacquer, overheads reflected in long bright streaks. Not the Wurlitzer with its cracked middle C. Jackie lifted the fallboard. Weighted keys. He pressed middle C and the note **bloomed** — clarity that made the garage version sound like it had been played through a blanket.

"Sit." Maxanne pointed Jackie to the bench, Nick to a stool by the DI. "Tune. Mike needs five minutes."

She stepped into the control room. Through the glass Nick watched her lean to Mike's ear. Mike nodded. Hands on the board.

Nick plugged in. Without an amp the guitar sounded thin in the monitors — string without chest — until Mike shaped it, added room, gave it body.

Jackie ran scales. Long strokes. Technique so internalized it had gone invisible. The piano responded like it had been waiting.

Maxanne's voice through the overheads, studio-clear: "One take. No overdubs. No punch-ins. Play what you unlocked in that garage. Mike rolls until you're done or I stop you."

Nick looked at Jackie across four feet of treated air. "Ready?"

Jackie didn't smile. "No."

"Good."

---

The first thirty seconds were wrong.

Nick came in too fast — Battle anger without the Battle. Jackie answered with sheet music hands. The monitors threw it back naked. Nick stopped.

"Again," he said.

Jackie flexed his fingers. "Again."

Second start: honest tempo. D minor — the chord from the garage, but on the Yamaha it didn't open a door. It knocked down a wall. Overtones spiraled and hung.

Nick's hands found the *Sweet Emotion* bass line — low G, F-sharp, E, D — clean, no distortion, the bones without the muscle. The title groove. Heartbeat under everything.

Jackie's left hand doubled the line an octave up. Four bars in unison — guitar and piano, same phrase, same breath. Then Jackie's right hand drifted upward and the Queen melody entered, not sung, **played** — piano carrying the vocal line the way a river carries a leaf.

The songs merged the way tributaries merge — each losing its name, not its current. Aerosmith groove. Queen architecture. Fragments of *Walk This Way* crept in as texture, Tyler's strut on six strings, answering, trading, the conversation they'd started at thirteen when they didn't know enough to be afraid.

Nick's eyes closed. He didn't need the fretboard. The operatic section arrived — Yamaha bass to treble, thunder to glass — and Nick's guitar sang a counter-melody on one line where Brian May had stacked three.

Once they stumbled — Nick's fingers slipped at the twelfth fret, caught, turned the slip into a bend. Jackie matched with a crushed note on the piano. The crack let the light in. Proof this was live and would never happen exactly this way again.

The hard-rock section hit. Nick dug in. Jackie hammered the keys. Thirty seconds loud enough to rattle the panels, monitors feeding back, building until it crested and broke —

And they didn't stop.

They kept going because Mike's light was still red and neither of them wanted the red to go out. The groove widened. The D minor came back. *Is this the real life* over the descending bass figure. A thread of *Dream On* on the high strings — not the whole song, just the ache in the melody Dan had put into Nick's left hand one Sunday when the garage smelled like Quikrete and hope.

Eleven minutes before Nick's right hand finally lifted and the last harmonic rang out and died against the panels.

Silence.

Two beats.

Jackie played the final chord — D minor, sustain pedal down, note fading like the last light of a day neither of them wanted to end.

Nick's hands shook on the strings.

Through the glass Mike sat with his mouth open. Maxanne's palm flat on the glass. The reel still turning.

---

Mike's voice on the talkback, young and steady: "Eleven minutes. Still rolling."

Maxanne came in. She didn't rewind. She looked at both of them the way a woman looks who has been in rooms where music happened for thirty years and knows what it costs.

Jackie pressed his fingers to his eyes. When he dropped his hands, his face was wet.

"This is what we could have been," he said.

Nick looked at him. Studio light on the wetness.

"We could still be this," Nick said.

The monitors hummed. A question nobody in the room could answer out loud.

Nick thought about Grace — home on Dutton Road right now, probably in the Friendly's uniform for the Saturday dinner shift, probably not sitting down. The wagon transmission. The mortgage. The Army recruiter's card in his dresser drawer from April, the path that meant a paycheck and a ticket out that wasn't a dream and wasn't a betrayal if you didn't call it one.

He couldn't walk back into that kitchen and tell her he was throwing it over for eleven minutes on a reel in a room that smelled like tape oxide. Couldn't take more than she'd already given.

"I can't," Nick said.

---

Jackie sat on the bench, hands on his thighs, away from the keys.

His father. Fourteen years of Saturday drives on Route 9. Bach on the tape deck. Juilliard application — three drafts, his father's careful handwriting in the margins. The bankbook in the desk drawer — deposits since Jackie was five, twelve years of numbers in a column that only went one direction.

Dr. Marsh had closed a practice-room lid in 1960 and never opened it for himself. He had poured everything he sacrificed into his son — not the same dream, a translation of it, the safe version of the dangerous thing.

Jackie knew his father was wrong about which version of him was real. He also knew the bridge was real.

He closed his eyes. "Me either."

The words fell like stones into water.

"It's not giving up," Nick said, voice rough. "It's choosing the people who already chose us."

Maxanne turned to the window. Mike watched through the glass with headphones around his neck, the face of a man who knew he was witnessing something he would spend years failing to describe.

Jackie stood. Looked at Nick. Nick looked back.

"If things were different," Jackie said.

"Yeah," Nick said.

Jackie extended his hand. Nick took it — one firm grip, two seconds, released. Not a hug. A hug would have been too much.

Nick went out through the studio door to the parking lot and the wagon with the timing off in third. Jackie went out through the control room and down the hall the other way, toward the street where Stephen had said he'd circle back.

Separate exits. Separate lives waiting the way debts wait — patient, certain, compounding.

---

Maxanne stood alone in Studio C. Empty chairs. Boom mics angled toward nothing.

Mike pressed the talkback. "What do we do with this?"

He held up the reel — seven inches of magnetic oxide, **eleven minutes**, everything.

Maxanne took it in both hands. Light. Lighter than it should have been for what it carried.

"We preserve it," she said. "Somebody needs to remember this was possible."

She slid the reel into a cardboard sleeve. Wrote on the label in block letters she would still recognize in 2009. Turned off the studio lights.

The Boylston lot at five-thirty held two empty spaces where two boys had parked. Afternoon sun on the brick. WBCN sign buzzing overhead.

She tucked the reel under her arm and walked inside to file what the station would pretend never happened.
