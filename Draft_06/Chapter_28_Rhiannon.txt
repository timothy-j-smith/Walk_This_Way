ACT III: IN THROUGH THE OUT DOOR  (The Signal)

CHAPTER 28: "RHIANNON"

---

Saturday, May 12, 1979. Four days to the Battle.

The rooster didn't crow at the Lally place. The pigs handled that — two hundred and sixty Yorkshires grunting their way to consciousness at five-fifteen every morning, a sound like the earth clearing its throat.

Patty Lally pulled on her boots at the back door and stepped into air thick with hog waste and wet hay. The southeast corner of Sudbury didn't wake up pretty. It woke up honest.

Five-gallon buckets. Grain mixed with kitchen scraps. Eight pens while her father checked water lines and her mother tallied receipts at the kitchen table with the radio low — country, turned down to company, not conversation. The pigs pressed against the gates when they saw her coming. Not affection. Hunger wearing a familiar face. Patty didn't mind. There was something clean about a relationship that never pretended to be more than it was.

By seven-forty she'd showered twice, braided her hair still damp, and caught the bus at the corner of Morse and Landham. Soap could mask pig but not erase it. She'd learned that in seventh grade when Cheryl Mancini wrinkled her nose in homeroom and said, loud enough for everyone, "Something smells like bacon."

The class laughed.

Patty looked at Cheryl until the laughter died. "Bacon pays our mortgage."

Nobody brought it up again.

---

Lincoln-Sudbury in May smelled like chalk dust and floor wax and the low-grade panic of seniors counting down to graduation. Patty chained her blue Schwinn to the rack by the gym door and walked the east hallway toward first period, past lockers tagged with band names and AEROSMITH vs QUEEN graffiti the janitor scrubbed off every Friday and that reappeared every Monday, the argument as permanent as the cinder blocks underneath.

On the corkboard outside Mr. Wentworth's music room, the goldenrod flyer was curling at the corners:

BATTLE OF THE BANDS
MAY 16, 1979
LINCOLN-SUDBURY REGIONAL HIGH SCHOOL
GRAND PRIZE: $500 — PROFESSIONAL RECORDING SESSION
SPONSORED BY WBCN — THE ROCK OF BOSTON

Seven names on the sign-up sheet. One line left.

Patty read the date twice. Not the money — five hundred dollars wouldn't change what she already knew about her music. She read the empty line and the war the whole school had been choosing sides for since March.

---

The cafeteria split like a cell dividing.

Nick Johnson's table near the windows — Rollers, shop kids, trays cleared fast, jaws working. Jackie Marsh's table by the doors — Rampers, college talk over sandwiches wrapped in wax paper, every gesture measured before it left his mouth. Between them, forty feet of linoleum nobody crossed.

Patty sat with Kristy Wesslen and Robin Bosshard near the milk coolers — neutral ground, claimed by nobody. From there she could see both boys without turning her head.

The strange thing — the thing only Patty seemed to notice — was the way they never looked at each other. Not avoiding. Avoiding would require acknowledgment. This was worse. They'd erased each other from the room. Nick's eyes swept the cafeteria and skipped Jackie's table like a scratch on a record, the needle jumping over the part it couldn't play. Jackie's gaze moved through the space where Nick sat as if that corner held nothing but empty chairs.

Patty bit her apple and watched.

And for one breath the cafeteria thinned — pine resin, lake water turning copper at the edge of dark, three voices finding the same held note on a dock she'd left four summers ago. Nick's root. Jackie's fifth above. Her E on top, clean and unshowy, the way a bell finds the pitch it's been waiting for. The meadow had sent the sound down to the water and the water had sent it back, and for five days nothing in the world had mattered except what happened when they stopped trying to make it.

Then Donnie said something that made Kathy laugh on the platform, and the image broke, and the linoleum was linoleum again.

---

Memorial Congregational Church at Sudbury Center smelled like old hymnals and lemon oil and whatever they put in the wood polish that made the pews glow amber under the overhead lights — white clapboard and a modest steeple you could see from Route 20 when the leaves were down, the kind of New England church that had been holding the town's ordinary Sundays since before anybody's grandmother was born.

Sunday evening. Five o'clock. Reverend Goff left the key under the mat because he trusted musicians the way he trusted anyone who brought their own instrument to a house of God. By five-fifteen they'd moved hymnals off the front pews, run an extension cord from the sacristy, and turned the chancel rail into a stage line.

Steve Bankuti set up on the chancel rug — snare, hi-hat, kick, a ride cymbal he'd bought at a yard sale in Maynard for three dollars. Robin Bosshard plugged his bass into a small Fender amp wedged between the pulpit and the lectern. Kristy Wesslen perched on the communion rail with her acoustic, tuning by ear.

Patty stood where the minister stood on Sundays. Guitar strap over her shoulder. Steel strings, calluses that had nothing to do with feed buckets and everything to do with the reclaim corner where her father had taught her that quit-on things still had worth.

"Battle of the Bands," she said. "WBCN. May sixteenth. One slot left."

Robin looked up from his bass. "That's the Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh show."

"I know."

Steve's sticks stopped on the pew back — a paradiddle cut in half by silence. "Whole school's chosen sides."

"I'm not choosing sides." Patty looked at the stained glass above the chancel — Sudbury names in gold leaf, the town's war dead and its ordinary dead honored in red and blue light that fell across the floor like bruises on the church's skin. "Somebody has to play music that isn't a weapon."

Kristy exchanged a look with Steve. "You want to walk into their war?"

"I want to play our songs. Not louder. Not angrier. Not more perfect than them. Honest."

Robin set the bass down, then picked it back up — his thinking move. "So we're entering a war to play peace songs?"

Patty almost smiled. "Something like that."

Nobody said yes. Nobody had to.

---

"Rhiannon," she said. "From the top. Count it in, Steve."

Steve touched the hi-hat with his stick — one, two, three — and Robin found the pulse underneath, a walking line that didn't hurry, hooves on wet ground, the song's restlessness before the voice arrives. Kristy came in on the second pass, picking the figure that circled the root without crowding it, the way ivy circled a trellis without choking it.

Patty let two bars go by with the guitar silent. Just the room breathing — stone floor, empty pews, dust in the stained-glass light — and then she opened her mouth.

Not loud. Not yet. The melody came up through her chest the way the brook came up through the conservation land in March: everything to say, nowhere to hide.

She didn't sing the words all the way through — she never did in rehearsal, not until the song had a body — but the vowels were there when they needed to be, the rise on *night*, the turn where the line refused to resolve, where Stevie Nicks had built a song about a woman who wouldn't stay where you put her. Patty understood that song. She had always understood it.

By the second chorus Robin had locked in. His bass hummed through the floor — you felt it in your feet, the building's bones carrying what the air carried. Kristy's harmony wound around the lead without copying it, different temperature, same weather. Steve moved from brushes to sticks on the snare rim, a hiss that said *listen* before it said *move*.

They ran it again.

The third pass was the one. The church stopped being a church and became a resonant box — vaulted ceiling, old beams, brass plaques on the wall and the long honor roll of Sudbury names hearing a prayer they'd never been offered before. Patty's voice climbed and didn't show off the climb. It just went where the song asked. When she hit the last held note she let it die in the rafters instead of forcing it, and the silence after was full, the way the meadow had been full when the mosquitoes finally drove them inside and the lake still held the echo.

Steve set his sticks down. "That's the one."

Robin nodded. "That's the one."

Kristy was crying a little, which she would deny if you asked. "We're going to get swallowed."

"Maybe," Patty said. "I don't care about winning."

"Then what's the point?"

She thought about the dock. The triad. The half-second the lake gave back.

"The point is showing up," she said. "The point is playing music that remembers what music is for."

---

She walked home after rehearsal because the bus didn't run past seven and her mother was buried in feed invoices. Two miles through Southeast Sudbury in the May dark, past the Cavicchio security light throwing long shadows across empty pens, past mini-bike ruts in the conservation trailheads like old wounds.

The smell found her at the property line. Thick, animal, alive. Home.

The house sat dark except for the kitchen light — vinyl siding that needed paint, barn leaning slightly east, chickens having beaten the grass into mud. Her mother sat at the table, calculator and ledger open, reading glasses pushed up on her forehead.

"Rehearsal okay?"

"Yeah. We're entering the Battle of the Bands."

Her mother looked up. "At the high school. The one with the radio station."

"WBCN. Yeah."

Calculator set down. Glasses removed. The look a mother gives when she knows her daughter is about to walk into something complicated and has decided to let her do it anyway.

"That's the Johnson boy and the Marsh boy's thing. The whole town's been talking about it."

"I know."

"You sure you want to get in the middle of that?"

Patty opened the refrigerator, found last night's meatloaf, ate it cold over the sink. The meat was dry. She didn't care.

"I'm not getting in the middle of anything. I'm just playing music."

Her mother watched her eat. The expression on her face said she'd raised a daughter who made her own decisions and wasn't about to start second-guessing now — but she reserved the right to worry.

"Okay," she said. And went back to the ledger.

Mortgage due the twentieth. Feed bill the twenty-second. The numbers didn't care about battles.

---

Monday, May 14, 1979. Last period. 2:45 PM.

The hallway outside Wentworth's room had cleared — buses idling, parking lots emptying, the building settling into the hollow quiet of a Monday afternoon.

Patty walked into the music room alone. Fluorescents humming their one flat note. Wentworth sat behind his desk grading papers with the patience of a man who'd traded symphony halls for steady paychecks twenty years ago and had mostly stopped regretting it.

The sign-up sheet sat at the corner of his desk. Seven names filled the seven lines above the last blank space.

"Mr. Wentworth."

He looked up. Pen still in hand.

"I'd like to register a band."

Something shifted in his face. Not surprise. Recognition. He'd heard Patty sing in music class — had stopped mid-sentence once, the way a counselor at camp had stopped on a dock four years ago, because the voice coming out of this tall girl from the southeast corner wasn't the voice of a girl filling out worksheets about time signatures.

"Band name?"

"Rumours."

He wrote it in careful block letters.

"Members?"

"Robin Bosshard — bass and guitar. Kristy Wesslen, harmonies and acoustic. Steve Bankuti, drums. Me — lead vocal and guitar."

"Genre?"

"Fleetwood Mac."

Wentworth set his pen down. Leaned back. Studied her — damp braid, steel-string calluses, the faintest trace of something soap couldn't conquer.

"You know what you're walking into," he said.

It wasn't a question.

"I know what I'm walking into."

He held her gaze a moment longer, then slid the registration form across the desk. "Eight bands. You're the last slot."

Patty signed her name. The pen scratched against the paper, loud in the empty room.

She took the carbon copy, folded it into her back pocket, and walked out into the corridor. A locker slammed somewhere down the hall — last student, last echo. Four days.

She hummed "Rhiannon" all the way to the bus stop. Not as a statement. Just something the song did when you let it live in your mouth — restless, certain, moving through the May air like it knew where it was going even when the town didn't.
