ACT I: TOYS IN THE ATTIC

---

CHAPTER 2: "DRAW THE LINE"

SUDBURY.  March, 1979

Maxanne Sartori drove up to the Johnson house in her powder-blue Karmann Ghia, the car bottoming out twice on potholes the town never fixed on the working-class side of Hop Brook. Sparks kicked off the undercarriage. She didn't care. Mud season — the yards gray at the edges, the brook running high and brown with melt, the maples still bare. She'd heard rumors about a kid on Dutton Road: a voice that could stop you mid-dial-turn, guitar work that sounded like he was dragging something up from underground.

The Johnson house squatted like it was tired: mustard paint peeling in long strips, gutters sagging, front steps listing to port. Brown grass pushed through dirty snow along the walk like stubble on a dead man's face. The bones of the house were good — wide clapboard, solid frame, the kind of place built by someone who knew what plumb meant.

From the detached garage came something alive before she heard it — distorted power chords traveling through the concrete foundation, through the frozen ground, up through the soles of her boots and into her chest.

Grace Johnson answered the door in her Friendly's uniform, polyester clinging to her frame, name tag reading GRACE in that cheerful corporate font that lies about everything. She smelled like fryer oil and coffee and the particular exhaustion of a woman working her second double this week.

She studied Maxanne — bell-bottoms, oversized sunglasses, standing on the cracked front step like a visitor from a planet where people bought clothes that didn't come from the Jordan Marsh clearance rack.

"You're that lady from WBCN. The one who broke Aerosmith."

"Guilty as charged."

"Nick's in the garage." Grace paused. Something crossed her face — the memory of hope, quickly shelved. "Don't fill his head with dreams we can't afford."

"Mind if I listen?"

Grace held her gaze a beat longer than comfortable. "He's been at it since school let out. Won't stop until dinner."

Maxanne crossed the yard toward the garage. The sound grew with each step — bass thrum in the frozen dirt, a snare crack that echoed off the neighbor's siding. The big door was mostly down, a six-inch gap along the bottom where the seal had rotted out — enough for chords to leak into the yard, not enough to see inside. She gripped the handle and hauled. The door climbed the track with a shriek of springs Dan had meant to fix, then stopped chest-high where the chain had given up. Through the opening: a bare bulb swinging on its cord, a kid bent over a guitar, four concrete walls holding more noise than they were built for.

She ducked under the bottom rail and stepped inside.

Inside, the air was warmer, thicker — sawdust and motor oil and the metallic bite of old concrete. Dan Johnson's tools hung on a pegboard along the back wall, each outline traced in black marker where a wrench or hammer used to live. Claw hammers in descending order. Box wrenches. A socket set open in its case with one empty slot — the nine-sixteenths Dan had carried in his pocket the November morning the cable snapped. Tucked behind a pipe wrench: a flattened tortoiseshell pick, worn thin. Behind a rusted coffee can, barely visible: the spine of a spiral notebook with staff lines on the pages. Under the workbench, a red toolbox, latch shut, paint chipped at the corners. Nick had not opened it. Nobody had.

Nick Johnson stood in the center of the garage, mid-solo, fingers moving on a pawn-shop guitar held together with electrical tape at the input jack and faith everywhere else. Twenty-five dollars at the Goodwill in Framingham. New strings from Store 24 on Route 217 when he could scrape together four bucks mowing lawns. The frets were worn smooth in the first position — a year of the same progressions until his fingertips knew the neck by heart.

Three other kids backed him — Lincoln boys, Maxanne would learn later, from the even-smaller town that shared a high school with Sudbury but not much else. They stopped when they saw her. That teenage terror of being caught caring about something. The music died mid-note like a bird dropping out of flight.

"Don't stop on my account," Maxanne said, settling onto a paint can. "Play it like you mean it."

They traded looks — four boys deciding whether to trust a stranger from actual radio. Nick decided for them. He launched back into "Dream On" with a fury that made the hooks sing.

His voice cracked on the high notes. The garage walls seemed to lean in. A claw hammer vibrated against its nail, buzzing at a frequency only the concrete could hear.

Nick's eyes cut to the tools — half a beat, the way a runner flinches passing the spot where someone fell. Then his fingers found the riff again and drove it home.

When they finished, nobody moved. The last note hung in the air. The bare bulb kept swinging.

"What's your band called?" Maxanne asked.

Nick shrugged. "Don't have a name yet. We're just... playing."

"You got a demo?"

The other boys looked at Nick. He shook his head. "Can't afford studio time."

Maxanne pulled out her card. WBCN, 104.1 FM.

"Come by the station. We'll find some time."

Nick hesitated. His eyes moved between the card and the shut toolbox under the bench.

"Why?" he asked. "Why would you help us?"

Maxanne stood, knees protesting. "Because Steven Tyler played his first gig in a garage not much bigger than this one. Somebody opened a door for him."

She paused at the garage door, looking back at four boys holding instruments the way drowning people hold wreckage. She wanted to say more. She didn't.

Walking back to the Karmann Ghia, she heard the music start again behind her — "Sweet Emotion," that bass line crawling out across the frozen yard like something that refused to stay buried. The sound carried past the potholed asphalt and the bare trees toward Hop Brook, where it died against the far bank.

On the other side of that brook, a boy sat at a Steinway preparing for a recital.
