ACT I: TOYS IN THE ATTIC

---

CHAPTER 11: "RADIO, RADIO"

BOSTON. August 11, 2009 — still

Three hours and nineteen minutes.

The talent show hangs in the booth the way a last chord hangs — you wait for the next thing and the next thing is slow to arrive.

Paper cup at my elbow. Heaven Hill. One swallow. The burn finds the back of my throat and stays there.

"You want to know how I got here?" I say into the mic. "Not the booth. Not the call letters. The woman in the chair. You've got to go back further than Sudbury. Further than those two boys. All the way to a bar in Allston — spilled beer, a low ceiling, a twenty-three-year-old who thought if you heard it true you were obligated to put it on the air."

I spin toward the album wall. Third row, left. Hand finds it without looking — Aerosmith's debut, blue wings on cream, January '73. Nobody else touches this copy.

"1973. First time I found the real thing."

---

January. Cold that made your teeth ache. Breath white in the streetlight. I drove the Dodge Dart down Commonwealth — heater when it felt like it, which was almost never — toward Brighton Ave because Paulie DeLuca had caught my sleeve at the station that afternoon.

"Maxanne. Brickyard tonight. You need to hear them."

"What kind of band?"

"The singer moves like he's trying to shake something off his skin. The guitar player looks like he'd kill you and sleep fine. Stones and Yardbirds had a kid and raised it behind a dumpster."

Paulie had seen ten thousand bands and been impressed by six. So I went.

The Brickyard sat between a laundromat and a sub shop. Narrow room. Ceiling so low the tall ones hunched. Stage the size of a dining table. Sound system that had been current around Eisenhower. Floor sticky under the shoes. Bathroom door that didn't latch. Neon Budweiser in the window buzzing like a fly against glass. Maybe twenty people. Fake-ID kids with nowhere warmer. A woman at the bar inside a paperback. Sal behind the taps, wiping glasses with a rag dirtier than the glass.

Then five of them walked onto that postage stamp, and the air in the room changed density.

The singer first — twenty-four, rail-thin, mouth too wide for the face, hair that had lost a fight with a socket. Hands on the mic stand like it was the only upright thing in the building. No hello. No name of the band.

He looked at the guitar player — dark hair, cheekbones that could open mail — and nodded.

The guitar player hit the opening riff.

Alive the way a downed power line is alive. Sparks. Don't care who steps near.

Steven Tyler sang like he'd swallowed something that burned and was giving it back as music before it finished him. Joe Perry played each note like an argument he meant to win. Bass and drums held the floor so the top could lean out over nothing and not fall.

Forty-five minutes. Four people left — the paperback, a couple who'd been fighting since I walked in, a kid with a curfew. The rest of us forgot to close our mouths.

Panasonic cassette recorder in my bag. I carried one everywhere. The day I talked my way into WBCN I swore I'd never let a sound I believed in go unmarked. Tape quality: garbage. Tinny. Distorted. Coffee-can acoustics. Underneath the noise — signal. Frequency that didn't apologize.

After the set I walked up to the singer. Emergency exit wall. Camel. T-shirt soaked through.

"Maxanne Sartori. WBCN."

He looked at me like I'd said Neptune. "The radio station?"

"The one where I'm going to play your album tomorrow night."

He stared. Then the grin — enormous, disbelieving — dangerous face gone suddenly young. "We have an album. Nobody plays it."

"I'm going to play it."

"You're going to play it."

"On WBCN. Tomorrow. Eleven. If I can find a copy."

He reached into a duffel and handed me the record like it might break. "Dream On," he said. "If you're only going to play one, play that."

Out into the January street. Cold in the lungs. The dial in my head turned a hair — clearer station, same city.

---

Next night, WBCN, ten-fifteen. Charlie Morris still in his office — unusual; Charlie believed the night shift ran itself or didn't deserve to. Feet on the desk. Joint between his fingers. Tolerant suspicion of a man who'd built a station on rules-as-suggestions.

"You've got that look," he said.

"I found something."

"You always find something."

I put the album on his desk. "Track three."

He studied the cover. Studied me. Dropped the needle.

Four and a half minutes. Neither of us breathed. Piano came in — Charlie's eyes shut. Tyler's open-throated scream — eyes open again, looking at me like I'd walked in with a map to buried money.

"Where'd you find this?"

"Allston. Tuesday. Twenty people in the room."

Charlie turned the album over. Pointed with the joint hand; ash on the desk. "This is what we DO, Maxanne. Find the real shit. Play it tonight. Eleven. Full volume."

Eleven o'clock I dropped the needle on "Dream On" — no intro, no context, no program director (gone at six). Tyler's voice into dorm rooms at BU and Northeastern, apartments in Cambridge and Somerville, cars on the Pike — a city that didn't know yet what it was hearing.

Request line lit before the fade. Every lamp on the board. Played it again at midnight. Twice more before two. By Wednesday kids walked into record stores asking by name. By Friday Boston had outsold the rest of the country.

---

Three months later a kid walked into Studio C for his first interview. Twenty-three. Skinny as a parking meter. Leather jacket from a harder decade. Bandana that looked borrowed from a mechanic. Hands shaking on the table — not stage fright exactly. Too much current for the wiring.

"You're Bruce Springsteen," I said.

"That's what they tell me."

"New Jersey."

"Asbury Park. Born to be anywhere else." Grin — wild, unpracticed.

I leaned in. "WBCN, 104.1. Bruce Springsteen — Greetings from Asbury Park, N.J. Welcome to Boston."

And the kid — unknown, unpolished — leaned into the mic and said:

"This is my first time on the radio. My mom lives out in California. HI MOM!"

Control room went. I went. Somewhere on the other coast a kitchen radio was probably loud enough.

That was 1973. Brickyard. Panasonic. A record handed over like a relic. A kid saying hi to his mother on the air.

---

Eyes open. 2009. Studio B. ON AIR still pulsing red.

Album wall: Aerosmith. The Cars. Boston. J. Geils. All found within walking distance of this glass.

I pick up the cassette. Don't read the label. Weight I already know.

"1973 I shared what I heard. By 1979 I'd learned other words — sponsors, demographics, make it pay."

Outside, a bus pulls off Boylston, taillights matching the sign.

Chair creaks. Old leather, still holding.

"After the talent show, summer. Camp. Two boys who still thought the town had room for both of them."

I cue "Dream On" — the album cut. Piano like someone starting a prayer in a room with the lights half on.

Press play. Tyler's voice fills Studio B, fills the dial, fills what's left of a city that once believed a dive-bar frequency could travel that far.

I keep my eyes closed and let him finish the sentence.
