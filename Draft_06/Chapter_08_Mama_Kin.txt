ACT I: TOYS IN THE ATTIC

---

CHAPTER 8: "MAMA KIN"

SUDBURY. March, 1975

The Johnson house on Dutton Road had crown molding in the dining room. Not the pressed-foam kind from the lumber yard — real plaster, hand-carved, acanthus leaves curling where the ceiling met the walls. Grace ran her fingers along the baseboard every time she vacuumed, the way you'd touch a scar without meaning to. Most of it was painted over now, three or four coats of landlord white, but where the living room met the front hall you could still see the original cut underneath — vines and small flowers, work someone had been paid for when this house was the kind of place that paid for that.

---

Nick came through the back door at four-fifteen — thirteen, brown paper bag from Store 24, mud on both knees. Martin Marquis strings, medium gauge, $3.49 — and a Slim Jim already eaten in the parking lot.

Grace was at the kitchen table in her Friendly's uniform, brown polyester with the orange piping, name tag crooked. Bills spread in a row in front of her — electric, mortgage, Sears — a hand of cards she hadn't decided how to play.

"Boots," she said without looking up.

Nick kicked them off. "Hey, Mom."

"How was school?"

"Fine."

"Fine like you went to all your classes, or fine like you went to some of your classes?"

"All of them." He held up the bag. "Got new strings."

Grace glanced at the bag, then at his knees. "You walked to Store 24 in the mud."

"The shortcut's faster."

"The shortcut goes through the Hendersons' yard."

"Mrs. Henderson doesn't care."

"Mrs. Henderson called me last week, Nick." She set down her pen. Eyes the color of strong tea; they didn't miss much. "Your father's working late. Leftover chicken in the fridge. Homework before you touch that guitar."

"Yes ma'am."

He didn't do his homework first. Grace knew he wouldn't. Socks across the kitchen, side door, two concrete steps down to the garage. She picked up her pen and went back to the column of numbers that never came out right.

---

The garage smelled like sawdust and motor oil and the cold mineral breath of concrete that never fully dried. Dan's workbench along the back wall — plywood scarred with saw cuts and half-moon burns from mugs set down hot. Tools on the pegboard above it. Everything in its place.

Nick's corner was the opposite. Milk crate for a chair. Pawn-shop Stratocaster copy, electrical tape at the strap button, shoebox of picks, Mel Bay chord book from the Sudbury library he'd never returned.

He sat on the crate and changed the strings. Old ones off stiff, nickel plating worn through where the pick hand worked. New ones threaded, wound, stretched, retuned until they held. Bright under the single bulb — clean steel against rosewood.

He played for an hour. The same riff. Opening of "Walk This Way" — that nasty syncopated thing Joe Perry played, muted scratches and pull-offs, notes tumbling like they were in a hurry to get somewhere bad. He could hear it perfectly in his head. His fingers were slower than the song. First four bars clean, then the ring finger a fret too high, and the whole thing collapsed into a buzz.

Again. And again.

---

Dan's truck in the driveway at six-forty — the rattle of a '68 F-100 with a loose heat shield and 140,000 miles of opinion. Engine cut. Work boots on gravel.

He came through the garage door sideways, shoulders clearing the frame. Carhartt jacket with drywall dust on the shoulders, jeans gone soft from washing, Red Wings caked at the soles. Hands enormous. Pads of the left fingertips worn flat and glassy — dimes stacked on dimes — in a pattern that didn't come from framing alone, though Nick had never seen his father hold a guitar.

"Hey, bud." Dan set his lunch pail on the workbench. "That 'Walk This Way'?"

"Trying to be."

Dan hung the jacket on the nail by the door. Stood behind Nick and watched his hands. "You're rushing the second bar. Slow down."

"It sounds wrong slow."

"Sounds worse fast if the notes aren't clean." He reached past and pressed Nick's ring finger flat against the fret wire. Warm. Rough. Joint compound. "Here. Not behind the fret — right on top of it. Feel that?"

Nick pressed. The note rang clean.

"Now the pull-off. Don't lift straight up. Pull down toward the floor. You're snapping the string, not just releasing it."

Nick tried. Better. The note had a voice now instead of a click.

"Again," Dan said.

First two bars. Dan's hand near the neck — not touching, hovering. At the transition to the third bar he tapped the body twice with a knuckle, setting tempo.

"There," Dan said. "Hear that? You had it."

Nick played it again. Whole this time — rough at the edges, but the bones were right.

Dan squeezed his shoulder once, hard. "Good. Now play it fifty more times."

Lunch pail. Inside. Through the screen door: "Smells good." Faucet. Nick played the riff again. Fresh strings biting the fingertips; by the twentieth pass the sting had gone dull and useful.

---

Dinner was chicken and rice and canned green beans on plates that didn't match — two blue, one white with a chip that Grace always took. Table pushed against the wall. Dan with his back to the window. Grace closest to the stove. Nick between them, left hand flexing under the table, fingertips dented and sore.

"He's getting better," Dan said to Grace, forking rice.

"I can hear." Grace looked at Nick. "You do your homework?"

"Most of it."

"Nick."

"I'll finish after dinner."

She held his eyes a beat, then let it go. Turned to Dan. "The banister's loose again. Top of the stairs."

"I'll look at it Saturday."

"It's been loose since November."

"Then another week won't kill it." Half a smile. Grace shook her head but didn't push.

She stood to clear the plates. At the dining-room doorway she stopped. Thumb finding the groove where the original molding met the casing — a seam you'd notice only if you knew to look. She'd traced it as a girl in a house with a name, on the way to a dining room with a mahogany table and eight matching chairs. She traced it now in Dan's house, where the plaster was his family's and the memory was hers.

"What?" Dan said.

"Nothing." She pulled her hand away. "The paint's peeling."

Plates to the sink. Water hot.

---

Nick finished homework at nine — close enough. Brushed his teeth, said goodnight, lay in the dark and listened.

Grace in the hallway. Bedroom door. Lamp click. Quiet.

He waited. Twenty minutes. Maybe thirty. Long enough for the house to decide everyone was asleep.

Then the sound came up through the floor — from the garage, muffled by drywall and joists. Acoustic guitar, soft, notes dampened like someone cupping the sound with a palm. Nick couldn't make out the song. Just rhythm, steady and slow, fingerpicking that rose and fell like breathing. Sometimes a pause — the small squeak of dry skin on wound strings — then the music again, patient, unhurried.

He'd heard it for years. Never mentioned it.

The music went on. Nick lay still, sore fingertips against the cool sheet, the sound coming up through the floorboards and the bedframe into his chest.

Eyes closed. "Walk This Way" moving through his fingers against the mattress — pull down, not up, right on the fret, feel that — and somewhere beneath him the guitar kept going, quiet and sure.
