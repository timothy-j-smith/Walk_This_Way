ACT I: TOYS IN THE ATTIC

---

CHAPTER 7: "BOHEMIAN RHAPSODY"

SUDBURY. Fall, 1974

The thermostat in the Marsh dining room was set to sixty-eight degrees. Always sixty-eight. Jackie had checked once with a thermometer from his chemistry set, hoping for a degree of drift — some small proof that nature still had a say. Sixty-eight point zero. His father's house did not permit variation.

The breakfast table was set the way it was set every morning — forks one inch from the plate edge, knives turned inward, cloth napkins folded into identical rectangles. Jackie knew the distances because he'd measured them on a dare from Elizabeth. She'd bet him a dollar their mother used a ruler. She was right. Not a ruler exactly — habit so deep it had become precision, Eileen Marsh's hands finding the spacing the way a pianist finds an octave.

Dr. Richard Marsh sat at the head of the table with the Boston Globe. He held the paper with both hands, the way he held everything — deliberately, taking up exactly the right amount of space. Forty-five. Lean from discipline. Long fingers. A span wider than it needed to be for turning pages.

Jackie sat across from him, thirteen and already trained in the architecture of this room. Under the tablecloth his fingers moved — silent scales on an invisible keyboard, the notes playing only inside his head.

"Juilliard's pre-college program wrote back," Dr. Marsh said without looking up. "They want to schedule your audition."

Jackie's fingers stopped. "When?"

"Next month. We'll discuss repertoire this weekend. Chopin needs another month. The Bach is ready."

"I've been working on other things too."

Dr. Marsh turned a page. The sound was precise as a metronome. "Such as?"

Eileen Marsh came in carrying toast cut into triangles — always triangles; years ago someone had decided that was the shape toast should be in this house, and no one had revisited it. She poured orange juice into crystal and sat with her napkin across her lap and pearls at her throat at seven-thirty in the morning.

"Queen," Jackie said. "The way Mercury layers the vocals — "

"Jackie." Just his name. The weight of a full sentence: We've discussed this.

"Rock music is a phase," Dr. Marsh said, folding the newspaper and setting it beside his plate, aligned with the table edge. "You have a genuine gift. I won't watch you waste it."

Tightness in the jaw — not anger exactly. Something underneath that looked almost like pain. His father's left hand moved to the tablecloth. The long fingers pressed once — thumb and pinky spanning what would have been an octave — then relaxed.

"Richard," Eileen said. Quiet. One word that meant: not at breakfast.

She was studying her orange juice the way she'd learned to study rooms — when to speak, when to let silence work. Jackie knew what she knew: the carton in the attic crawl space behind the Christmas decorations, sheet music boxed in 1960 and never opened, Juilliard competition programs at the bottom with his father's name on them. He'd found them at ten. He had never asked. Some doors in this house stayed shut.

"Your father wants what's best," Eileen said, a line worn smooth from use.

From upstairs a violin cut through — not careful scales. A melody that started with Vivaldi's precision and bent sideways into something warmer. Elizabeth improvising again, living in the cracks between the notes on the page.

Dr. Marsh glanced at the ceiling. Said nothing. You couldn't schedule what came that easily. So he let it be.

"May I be excused?"

"You have forty minutes before school."

"I want to practice."

His father nodded. Practice, in this house, was always the right answer.

---

The Steinway filled a third of Jackie's bedroom — bought the year he was seven, the year a conservatory teacher said remarkable hands. Tuned every three months by a man who drove up from Boston.

Jackie closed the door. Locked it — the small rebellion of a turned bolt.

He sat at the bench and let his hands rest on the keys without pressing them. Bach first. The Prelude in C Major — his father's favorite, the piece Dr. Marsh sometimes paused outside the door to hear, his shadow visible in the gap at the floor. Jackie played it perfectly because after six years the notes lived in his tendons. The Bach was beautiful the way this house was beautiful. Measured. Every note in its place.

Then he stopped.

He listened. His father's car starting in the driveway. The garage door closing. The house settling into the silence it held when Dr. Marsh left — an exhale, the walls relaxing by half a degree.

Jackie's hands found different keys.

The opening of "Bohemian Rhapsody" — soft enough that the notes barely carried past the bedroom walls. The melody ghosted out of the piano like something that had been waiting for the house to empty. Sustain pedal barely pressed, overtones muted so they wouldn't travel through the floorboards.

He played the ballad with his eyes closed. Left hand on the dark descending bass Mercury had borrowed from opera. Right hand on the melody, reaching for notes that weren't in any conservatory book. He wanted the lid all the way open, hammers at full weight, sound cracking the plaster.

He played quiet. Necessity, not choice.

When he reached the operatic section he sang — barely above a whisper, Galileo Galileo rising in his chest and dying in his throat. Volume was the one thing this house could not tolerate. Thunderbolts and lightning he kept close to the keys, pulling the sound inward, and even restrained it was the most alive thing in the room.

He ended soft — nothing really matters — and held the last chord until the strings stopped on their own.

A knock. Jackie flinched, hands off the keys.

"That was really pretty." Elizabeth's voice through the door.

He unlocked it. She leaned on the frame, violin case in one hand, Fleetwood Mac t-shirt their mother pretended not to see.

"You heard that?"

"The whole thing. You know he's gone, right? You could've played it louder."

"I know."

Elizabeth looked at him the way she looked at a score she already knew. "You practice Queen like you're hiding a body."

"Liz — "

"I'm just saying. You don't have to whisper when nobody's listening." She shifted the case. "I figured out how to get that sliding guitar sound on 'Don't Stop Me Now' with vibrato and an open D. Want to try it together after school?"

"Dad gets home at six."

"So we stop at five-thirty."

She said it like weather. Like the simplest fact in the world.

"Yeah," Jackie said. "After school. Five-thirty."

Elizabeth grinned — too wide, too bright — and disappeared down the hall.

Jackie closed the door. Chopin Ballade for the audition. Posture straightened. Fingers in their trained positions.

Under the first phrase — one measure, long enough to feel it — his left hand played the bass line from "Bohemian Rhapsody."

Then it was gone. Just the Chopin. Just the sound his father wanted to hear.

---

Dinner: roast chicken, quartered potatoes, crystal throwing chandelier light across the cloth. Salt and pepper at the exact center of the table.

"The audition is in six weeks," Dr. Marsh said, cutting his chicken into small even pieces. "Professor Aldridge at the conservatory will do a mock audition Saturday."

"Okay."

He set down his knife and fork. Looked at Jackie — really looked — and the mask slipped. What was underneath was not authority. In the chandelier light it looked like grief.

"I had chances," he said. His voice lower than usual. Eileen stopped chewing. Elizabeth looked up. "When I was your age. Different chances." A pause. "I made the practical choice. That's what you do when you come from where I came from." Another pause. "I want you to have the path I couldn't see. Not just somewhere safe."

The grandfather clock ticked in the hallway. Steady. Never fast. Never slow.

"I hear you, Dad," Jackie said. And he meant it.

Elizabeth reached under the table and squeezed his hand. Quick, hard, gone.

"Eileen, this chicken is excellent," Dr. Marsh said, and picked up his fork, and the conversation ended the way conversations in this house always ended — not with resolution but with redirection.

Eileen smiled her careful smile. "Thank you, Richard."

Sixty-eight degrees. Everything in its place.

---

Later, Jackie sat in the dark. He did not turn on the piano. He listened to the house — his mother downstairs with the radio on the classical station, because that was the station this house played. His father's study, the murmur of pages. Elizabeth's violin through the wall, something slow that might have been Barber or might have been hers — with Elizabeth you could never tell.

Classical earned his father's approval. Rock earned his breath. He couldn't have both.

Five-thirty, he thought. We stop at five-thirty.
