CHAPTER 14: "BABA O'RILEY"

April in Sudbury was all mud. Hop Brook ran brown under the Route 117 bridge, loud enough to hear from the Lincoln-Sudbury front walk, and the lawn in front of the school had gone to the color of a boot print that had filled with water and frozen and thawed a dozen times since March. The parking lot was a lattice of tire ruts. A kid came up the walk, stepped wrong, went in past the ankle of his sneaker, said a word his mother wouldn't like, pulled his foot out with a sucking sound, and kept walking. Nobody looked. April.

Seven-forty-five. First bell in four.

Carl Marquis's '66 Impala came in off Lincoln Road from the south, throwing gravel, the muffler louder than it had been yesterday. Nick was in the passenger seat, book bag between his feet, a quarter-eaten Hostess donut in his hand, his hair still wet from the shower he'd taken at six-thirty. The radio was on an AM station playing something neither good nor bad — noise to fill the seven miles from Marquis's driveway to the school lot. The speaker in the driver's door buzzed on the high notes. It had buzzed since Carl and his father put the dash back together in November. It was just the sound the car made now.

Nick got out. He slammed the door the way you slammed the door of a car like that — a second slam, because the first one never caught.

Up the walk, twenty yards away, a wood-paneled Buick Estate wagon pulled up to the curb. The wagon was always clean. Dr. Richard Marsh had Saturday-morning habits about wax and a chamois, and it showed. Jackie got out of the passenger side, closed the door with the small respectful click a kid closed a clean car with, and lifted his hand once to the driver's-side window without looking at his father's face. The Buick pulled away. Jackie stood on the curb a beat longer than he had to, adjusting the strap of his book bag, then moved up the walk.

Nick was fifteen yards to his left, coming in from the parking lot at a diagonal. He saw Jackie and his head turned before his mind caught up — old habit, muscle memory from a year when they had walked in together every Monday with brown bags and plans for a band. Then Nick looked down at his own boots, at the donut in his hand, at the cigarette butt somebody had ground out in the gravel by his toe, and kept walking. His line and Jackie's line converged toward the same set of double doors and stopped converging eight feet before the doors, because Jackie went toward the right-hand door and Nick went toward the left, and they passed through at the same moment through doors hinged at different walls, and neither one looked at the other, and then they were inside and the hallway noise took them and they went in separate directions without a sentence between them.

On his way to the east wing Nick cut through the Glass Corridor, where the Rats sat against the brick dado with their paperbacks and their leather, and the eight o'clock sun came through the dirty glass and turned their cigarette smoke into a column of yellow light. Nobody looked up. That was the deal in the Corridor — you could pass through, but you passed through like weather. Nick came out the far end smelling faintly of somebody else's tobacco and pushed into the shop wing, where the smell changed to the one he understood.

---

Third period. Bay three.

The auto shop sat at the far end of the east wing — cinderblock walls painted the color of instant mashed potatoes, corrugated steel doors that rolled up on greasy chains, a concrete floor that sloped a half-inch toward a drain Mr. Pacenka had been meaning to rod out for six years. Bay one was lecture. Bays two and three were alive. Bay two had a '68 Impala up on jack stands, hood yawning open, radiator pulled. Bay three had Nick's project and Carl's — a small-block Chevy 327 on an engine stand, heads off, the valve train laid out on cardboard in the order the parts had come out.

The shop smelled like Brakleen and hot metal and the gritty orange hand-soap in the gallon pump by the wash sink. A transistor radio sat on the bench by the compressor, tuned to WBCN, low enough that Pacenka wouldn't make them shut it off. A woman's voice was on the air talking about a record Aerosmith had coming in the fall — low, certain, the kind of voice that made you believe a song before you'd heard it.

Nick had a socket wrench and a head bolt that had decided it didn't want to come. He leaned into it. His wrists were oil-dark to the cuffs, and there was a smear of grease on his cheekbone from where he had scratched a mosquito bite an hour ago without thinking about his hands first. Carl was across the engine stand with a torque spec sheet in one hand and a cup of shop coffee in the other. The coffee was bad. Everybody said the coffee was bad. Everybody drank it.

"Break or lean on it," Carl said.

"I'm leaning on it."

"Lean harder. It's a bolt, not your grandmother."

Nick leaned. The bolt went a quarter turn and then another and then it broke free and came out in his hand clean, and he set it on the cardboard in its place in the line, and Carl made a small approving sound and went back to the torque sheet.

The door from the corridor opened and Anthony Rossi came through — Ant, to everyone — a junior who was not supposed to be in the shop during third period because he had study hall in the cafeteria. He had a folded green hall pass in his fingers and a face that said he had no intention of explaining himself to Pacenka because Pacenka had stopped asking. He came up the aisle, hands in the pockets of a Raytheon-blue work jacket that had been his uncle's before it was his, and stopped at the engine stand to look at the heads on the bench.

"You doing the valves today?"

"Reseating," Carl said.

"Pacenka letting you grind em?"

"He said he'd do it. He's not doing it. So it's gonna be me doing it and him telling me I did it wrong."

Ant laughed — short, no teeth, the laugh of a kid who had been the youngest in a loud house and had learned to fit his amusement into other people's sentences. He knew a carburetor the way a piano kid knew a scale, and he was going to work at his uncle's garage on Boston Post Road when he graduated, and his family already knew it, and Ant had been fine with it since he was twelve.

"Friday night," Ant said. "We going or we not going?"

"Going where."

"Kevin said the trails. His brother's got that Yamaha. Ride til dark and then Store 24."

"I told my mother I'd be home by nine."

"Why."

"Cause I told her I would."

"You heard about Gary."

"Gary who."

"Gary Hagan. Broke his collarbone on the trails last weekend. Went over the handlebars on the big hill behind the transformer. His mother told him he wasn't gonna play JV baseball this spring, and he cried. Sixteen-year-old kid, crying at the kitchen table over JV baseball."

"Jesus," Nick said.

"Yeah. So — Friday night. Trails. But carefully."

"Carefully," Carl said, and made the word sound like the opposite of itself.

Mr. Pacenka came out from his office at the end of bay one — Joe Pacenka, thirty years in the coveralls, oil-black at the knees and pockets, a cigarette tucked behind his ear he wasn't going to smoke until lunch. He gave Ant a look that was not a question so much as a timer. Ant pushed off the bench, folded the hall pass a little tighter, and started down the aisle.

"Nine o'clock," Ant called back.

"Nine o'clock," Nick said, and did not look up.

When the door closed, Carl tilted his head toward the engine. "Your mother's gonna let you out at nine."

"My mother's working the four-to-close at Friendly's. I'm letting myself out at nine."

Carl grinned. The grin stayed on the torque sheet and didn't find Nick's face. He set the coffee down on the bench, checked a number against the block, and then said, without any particular weight, the way you'd hand somebody a wrench:

"You used to run with that Marsh kid."

Nick fitted the socket onto the next bolt. "Yeah."

"Piano kid. Played that thing at Curtis." Carl had been in the gymnasium that night, sixth grade, four rows back. Everybody had. "You two had that whole act."

"That was middle school."

"So what happened?"

Nick leaned into the bolt. It didn't move. He set his feet and leaned again, and the bolt gave with a crack like a knuckle, and he backed it out the rest of the way with his fingers and set it on the cardboard, sixth in a line of eight.

"Nothing happened," Nick said.

Carl looked at him for a second. Then he nodded the way you nod at a torque number that's close enough, and went back to the sheet, and the radio played low on the bench, and the compressor kicked on, and that was the end of it.

Nothing happened. That was the true part, and Nick knew it was the true part, and it sat in his chest all the same. Nobody had thrown a punch. Nobody had said the thing you couldn't take back. One Monday there was a table, and the next Monday there wasn't, and every Monday since had stacked on top of that one like coats of paint.

The wall clock clicked up another minute. The shop went back to its own sound — compressor, radio low, Pacenka's pencil somewhere in bay one scratching figures that meant something to men who built things for a living.

---

Lunch A. The cafeteria.

Nick sat at the shop-corridor tables with Carl and two juniors arguing about the Bruins, and he ate a sandwich Grace had made at six in the morning before her split shift, and he did not look at the raised platform at the back of the room. Not looking at the platform was a skill he had been practicing since October, and like anything you practice, he had gotten good at it — good enough that he could tell you, without once turning his head, that Jackie sat at the second table from the wall, that the kid beside him was named Bellenghi, and that the girl three chairs down owned highlighters in more colors than the shop owned grades of sandpaper.

A laugh came off the platform and crossed the room. The cafeteria was three hundred kids loud, and the laugh should have drowned in it, but the ear is a stubborn instrument and it keeps the frequencies it was raised on. Jackie's laugh — the real one, the one that came out louder than he meant it to and made him duck his head after. Nick had heard that laugh across a cafeteria in eighth grade, across a garage, across a lake at sunset. He heard it now over forty feet of noise, and his hand stopped on the sandwich for half a second, and then it kept moving, and he said something to Carl about the Bruins' power play that he did not afterward remember saying.

Below the platform, at the milk coolers, a girl in mud-brown jeans and a red-checked flannel three sizes too big — a brother's flannel, cuffs rolled to the wrists — was lifting a half-pint of whole milk out of the case. A guitar case was tucked under her elbow, hard shell, scuffed at the corners. Her hair was the color of wheat and uncombed at the back, and she was not looking at anyone.

Nick's hand came halfway up off the table before he knew what it was doing — camp was eight months ago, and on the last morning she had torn a phone number in half like she was dealing cards — and then he saw, without looking at the platform, that Jackie had turned his head too. Two seconds. Both of them. Patty Lally paid the cafeteria lady with a folded dollar, turned, and walked toward the side door that went to the music practice rooms, and was gone before either raised hand or turned head could have been expected to register on anyone.

Nick put his hand back on the table.

Neither boy had dialed his half of the number. Nick knew his was still folded under a stack of homework on his dresser. He did not know where Jackie kept his, only that Jackie was the kind of kid who kept things, and that some Monday one of them was going to walk into this room and find out the other one had called her, and he had started, quietly, without ever deciding to, keeping score of a game she didn't know she was in.

The bell rang. Three hundred chairs scraped back at once, a sound like the building clearing its throat.

---

After school the parking lot was mud and exhaust again, tire ruts filling with meltwater, kids moving toward buses and cars and the trailheads behind the shop wing where the dirt bikes would fire up by four-thirty. Nick came out the east door with grease still on his cuffs and Carl beside him talking about the valve job. Jackie came out the west doors with the Bellenghi kid and a group from the platform, loafers careful on the gravel. They crossed the same lot without crossing paths, the way they had crossed the same doors that morning, and the school went on humming behind them — fluorescent ballast, locker slams, a gym class whistle — the ordinary noise of a town that had sorted its children into rooms and expected them to stay there until June.
