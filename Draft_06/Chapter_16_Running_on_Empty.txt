ACT II: A NIGHT AT THE OPERA

---

CHAPTER 16: "RUNNING ON EMPTY"

Three hours and change.

The ON AIR sign blinks red. Studio B smells like hot dust and old carpet and the bourbon in the paper cup at my elbow — Heaven Hill, two fingers, the kind of pour you measure with your thumb. The cassette on the console is warm from my palm. Maxell, ninety minutes, label in ballpoint: "Demo - Nick/Jackie - un-Authorized - May 1979." I keep picking it up and putting it down. The plastic doesn't get lighter. Nothing does.

Behind the glass, the engineer they've assigned me — Bruins hoodie, laptop open, Red Bull sweating a ring onto the board — thinks music starts when somebody opens a DAW. He doesn't know he's working my last shift. They drew straws for tonight. I drew short.

On the turntable behind me, the station's farewell record is cued. Pink Floyd. "Shine On You Crazy Diamond." The corporate playbook says that's how WBCN goes out — graceful, elegiac, the kind of ending you can put in a press release.

I reach for the needle. I don't touch it.

---

You just heard how a morning ends.

November 1977. Dan Johnson on a job site in Boston. Cable. Beams. The kind of death that makes the Metro section and disappears by Sunday. I won't walk you through the funeral again. You know about the closed lid and the cold coffee mug and the latch on the red toolbox clicking shut.

What you don't know yet is what happened to the two boys after the lid closed.

---

The Globe gave Dan a paragraph on page three. Wedged between a fender-bender on Route 9 and a three-alarm in Framingham. They misspelled the last name — Johnson with an s, or an extra letter, I don't remember which, only that Grace saw it at Friendly's during her break and folded the page so the name faced the table and kept pouring coffee.

That's what a working man's death buys you in print. A paragraph and a typo.

Nick was sixteen.

Grace went back to Friendly's the next morning. Not because she was tough. Because the mortgage doesn't wait for grief, and when you don't have a cushion, sorrow is a luxury you clock in for. You carry your dead to work with you and you smile when booth four asks for more decaf.

---

Nick stopped talking.

The garage spoke for him. Dutton Road, concrete cold through his socks, Dan's tools still on the pegboard in the order Dan left them — Phillips-heads in descending size, the coffee can of sixteen-penny nails, the empty slot where the 9/16ths socket used to live before it went to a job site and never came home. Nick played until his fingers split and then he played some more. Not practicing. Bleeding pressure the only way he knew.

Jackie showed up in the doorway. Clean jeans. One hand on the jamb. Homework in his other hand, or an offer to sit and not say anything — I heard both versions over the years and they all sound like the same boy trying to be kind.

Nick didn't look up.

What he said — Jackie told me later, at the Paradise, like a man reading a charge sheet — was four words.

"I don't need your pity."

Not shouted. Not cruel. True enough to cut.

Jackie left. Came back the next week. Nick sent him home again. By spring of '78 they were nodding in the Lincoln-Sudbury corridor like people who'd met once at a party and couldn't remember why they'd liked each other. The cafeteria told on them without saying a word. Nick at the shop tables with grease on his cuffs. Jackie on the platform with a polo shirt and a group that did homework out loud. Same room. Forty feet of linoleum. Different doors.

---

Two hours left.

You want a villain for this part? I can't give you one. Jackie didn't do anything wrong. He showed up. Nick couldn't accept help from the kid on the other side of Hop Brook without admitting the brook existed. And the brook was the thing he couldn't afford to see.

So he sent him home. And the day after that. And the day after that.

---

I look at the Pink Floyd LP. I look at the cassette.

Plenty of time left to get it wrong one last time. Plenty of time to get it right.

Before I tell you what I did with that tape — and what I didn't do — you need to stand on a piece of wood over Hop Brook on an October afternoon in 1978, oak leaves in the water, and watch two boys try to have a conversation their bodies remember even when their mouths won't.

The bridge is next.
