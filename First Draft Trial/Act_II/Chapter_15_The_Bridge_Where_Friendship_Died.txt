## CHAPTER 15: THE BRIDGE WHERE FRIENDSHIP DIED

Fall 1976, six months after Dan Johnson died, and the friendship between Nick and Jackie had become a casualty of grief and class and the terrible knowledge that the world was not a safe place. They were still friends in name only, still nodding to each other in the hallways at school, still maintaining the outward appearance of a relationship that had once been the most important thing in both their lives.

But the easy intimacy was gone, replaced by a distance that felt both inevitable and heartbreaking. Nick had learned to build walls around his heart, to carry his grief alone, to understand that there were some things that other people could never understand, no matter how much they wanted to help.

Jackie had learned that there were some problems he couldn't fix, some pain he couldn't solve, some chasms he couldn't bridge no matter how hard he tried.

The frequency, as Patty had called it, was still there. But it was buried under layers of grief and responsibility and the terrible knowledge that the world was not a safe place.

And then came the day that would define the rest of their lives.

It was a Saturday afternoon in October, the kind of day that makes you remember why you love New England in the fall. The leaves were turning gold and red, the air was crisp and clean, and the conservation land behind Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School was alive with the sounds of autumn.

Nick was leading a group of friends on dirt bikes through the trails, riding the Honda CR125 that he'd rebuilt from parts, the engine singing that two-stroke song that sounds like defiance. Behind him, Carl Dukowski and two other Rollers followed, their bikes idling with the kind of confidence that comes from knowing how to fix things, from understanding that sometimes the best way to solve a problem was to get your hands dirty.

Jackie was in the woods too, but on foot. He'd been walking the paths since childhood, knew every tree, every stone wall that marked some colonial farmer's ambition. He went there to think, to escape the pressure of being Richard Marsh's son, to hear music in wind through pine needles.

They met where the main trail crossed Hop Brook via a wooden bridge that was older than either of their families' time in Sudbury. Nick was leading, riding the Honda CR125 that he'd rebuilt from parts, the engine singing that two-stroke song that sounds like defiance.

Jackie was standing on the bridge, right in the middle, looking down at the water like it might tell him something important.

Neither one would back up.

"You need to move," Nick said, killing the engine but not dismounting.

"It's a walking bridge," Jackie replied, not looking up. "Always has been."

Behind Nick, the other bikes idled, their riders sensing the tension but not understanding it was about more than right of way.

"Lot of things used to be different," Nick said. "Doesn't mean they can't change."

Jackie finally looked up, and for a moment—I swear this is true, they both told me later, separately—they almost smiled at each other. Almost remembered being thirteen and fearless and making music in Nick's garage before the world taught them they were supposed to be enemies.

Then a voice called from the woods on Jackie's side. "Everything okay here?"

Dr. Richard Marsh emerged from the trail, dressed for his afternoon constitutional in pressed khakis and a Boston University windbreaker. He took in the scene—his son facing down four kids on dirt bikes—and his face set into the expression of a man who'd found evidence supporting his worst assumptions.

"These boys bothering you, son?"

And there it was. These boys. Not classmates, not neighbors, not even kids. These boys. The pronoun that built walls.

"No, Dad," Jackie said, but something in his voice had changed. The almost-smile was gone, replaced by the mask he wore at recitals. "I was just explaining about the walking bridge."

Dr. Marsh looked at Nick, and in that look was every letter to the town council, every property value concern, every fear that the wrong element was getting too close to what mattered.

"Perhaps you boys could find another trail," he said. "One more... appropriate for your vehicles."

Nick's hands tightened on the handlebars. Behind him, someone gunned their engine—not threatening, exactly, but not not threatening either.

"It's a public trail," Nick said. "We got as much right—"

"Rights," Dr. Marsh interrupted, "come with responsibilities. Something you might understand when you're older."

Even forty-five years later, the casual cruelty of that moment makes me angry. The way adults could sharpen class differences into weapons, could teach kids to hate each other with such pedestrian efficiency.

What happened next set the stage for everything that followed.

Jackie moved. Stepped aside, gave ground, chose peace over principle. The gesture was small—just a few steps to the right—but in the economy of teenage pride, it was everything.

Nick could have just ridden past. Could have taken the victory and moved on. But something in the way Dr. Marsh stood there, protective and possessive and so goddamn sure of his righteousness, made that impossible.

Instead, Nick revved his engine and rode across the bridge at full throttle. The old wood groaned, the sound echoing across the water like a scream. His friends followed, four bikes turning the walking bridge into a statement about whose freedom mattered more.

When they were gone, the silence in the woods felt like aftermath. Dr. Marsh put his hand on Jackie's shoulder—not comforting, but claiming.

"Those boys," he said again, and this time the pronoun carried even more weight. "They don't understand boundaries. That's why they'll never amount to anything."

"Dad," Jackie started, but what could he say? That one of those boys had once been his best friend? That they'd made music together that felt like truth? That the boundary wasn't about bridges or trails but about something neither of them had chosen but both were trapped by?

"Come on," Dr. Marsh said. "Your mother's expecting us for dinner."

Jackie looked back once as they walked away, his eyes meeting Nick's across the distance that had opened up between them. For a moment, it seemed like he might say something, might try to bridge the chasm that had grown wider with each passing month.

But then Dr. Marsh's hand tightened on his shoulder, and Jackie turned away, following his father down the trail toward a future that was already mapped out, toward a world where he belonged and Nick didn't, toward a life that would never include the kind of friendship they'd once shared.

Nick watched them go, understanding that this was it, that the friendship that had once been the most important thing in both their lives was over, that they would never speak again, that the frequency that Patty had talked about was gone forever, buried under layers of grief and class and the terrible knowledge that the world was not a safe place.

He sat on his bike for a long time, listening to the sound of the water flowing under the bridge, thinking about Dan and the garage and the music that had once brought them together, understanding that sometimes the most important stories are the ones that never get told, the ones that die in the silence between the notes, the ones that remind us that the world is not a safe place, that people you love can be taken away without warning, that sometimes the only way to survive is to build walls around your heart.

And sometimes, those walls are the most dangerous things of all.

That was it. Four bikes, one pronoun, and a friendship dissolved into the water below.
