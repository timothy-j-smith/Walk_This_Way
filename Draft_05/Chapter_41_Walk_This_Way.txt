CHAPTER 41: "WALK THIS WAY"

---

Final hour.

The Pink Floyd cart sits in the machine like a corporate tombstone — twenty-six minutes of accidental elegy somebody in North Carolina picked from a brochure. I leave it where it is. Shine On You Crazy Diamond can guard a suite that never airs.

On the console, the cassette.

*Demo — Nick/Jackie — un-Authorized — May 1979.*

The monitor beside the request line blinks amber. Voicemail — one message flagged PRIORITY from a listener in Somerville, logged at ten-forty-two. Kevin played it for me before the Cars block. I don't play it again. I don't need to.

A man's voice, older, cigarette-rough: *The Neighborhoods opened for the Cars at the Rat in seventy-nine. These kids never even made the card. If you're going out tonight, play something that matters.*

I almost laughed when I heard it. Not because it was wrong. Because it was the whole town in one sentence.

---

I lean into the pop filter.

"Corporate left me a cart. I'm not going to play it."

Through the glass Kevin's eyebrows go up. He doesn't stop me.

"I'm going to play a dub I shouldn't have. May nineteenth, nineteen seventy-nine. Studio C. Two boys from Sudbury who tied a Battle of the Bands and chose their people and recorded eleven minutes anyway. Unauthorized. Never sold. Never released. The best band this town ever had — and they were never a band. Not once. That's the story you've been listening to for four hours. This is the only proof."

I pick up the cassette. The plastic shell clicks into the deck — old habit, muscle memory from the Brickyard days when conviction went on a Maxell and into the world before anyone asked.

"I've carried this thirty years. I'm playing it now. Full volume. The engineers used to hate me for this. The listeners never did."

I punch the fader up.

---

The VU meters slam red.

D minor — Jackie's Yamaha, middle C blooming through Studio C walls, through the Wheatstone, through fifty thousand watts for the last time tonight. The chord hangs in the booth like a held breath the building has been waiting for since nineteen sixty-eight.

Then Nick's guitar — *Sweet Emotion* stripped to its bones, clean, no amp hiding anything. Low G, F-sharp, E, D. Heartbeat under everything.

I don't narrate. I listen.

Jackie's left hand doubles the line an octave up. Four bars in unison — guitar and piano, same phrase, same breath. Queen melody rising out of the piano like a leaf on a river Nick's strut answers on six strings. Fragments of *Walk This Way* creep in as texture — not the single, not the record, the **conversation** they started at thirteen when they didn't know enough to be afraid.

The operatic section — Yamaha bass to treble, thunder to glass. Nick's counter-melody where Brian May stacked three. A stumble at the twelfth fret turned into a bend. Jackie's crushed note matching. The crack that lets the light in.

Hard rock — thirty seconds of controlled fury, tenderness with nowhere else to go. They don't stop. Mike's red light still burning in my memory. The groove widens. D minor again. *Is this the real life* over the descending bass. A thread of *Dream On* on the high strings — not the whole song, just the ache Dan put in Nick's left hand one Sunday when the garage smelled like Quikrete and hope.

Eleven minutes.

Nick's right hand lifts. A last harmonic rings and dies.

Two beats.

Jackie's final chord — D minor, sustain down, note fading like the last light of a day neither of them wanted to end.

The cassette spins into leader. The meters fall.

The booth is quiet in a way no booth in this building has ever been quiet.

---

I lean toward the mic.

"This is Maxanne Sartori." A beat. "WBCN. The Rock of Boston."

Another beat.

"Goodnight."

The clock reads eleven fifty-nine.

The ON AIR sign dies at midnight. Not a second off. Forty-one years of red light — and then no red light. The booth drops to emergency fluorescent, the color of old newsprint, and the absence of the red is louder than anything I ever played through this microphone.

Transmitter silence. Not quiet. **Empty.** The space where something used to be.

Twelve-oh-one.

Static. Then smooth.

A man's voice mixed too hot, processed through reverb, the audio equivalent of a smile with nothing behind it.

*You're listening to JOY FM, bringing you the good news in music and ministry.*

The synthesized choir from down the hall is no longer rehearsal — it's **on the dial**. Praise without sweat. The frequency learning a language it was never built to speak.

I eject the cassette. The shell is warm from the deck. I slide it into my purse where it has lived through Brookline and New York and LA and every year I told myself not tonight.

---

Kevin opens the booth door. He stands in it. He does not speak. I understand we are not going to speak.

The hallway's moving boxes have not moved. The lobby is darker than when I came in. The chipped WBCN mug still sits in RECEPTION — MISC, cold ceramic, nobody coming back for it.

Outside, August in Boston. Asphalt and harbor heat. The antenna tower dark against the sky for the first time in forty-one years.

My Volvo is the only car in the lot. Twelve years old. I get in and close the door.

I leave the radio off.

The engine catches on the second try. I pull out onto the Pike heading west — toward Cambridge, toward home — and the silence wraps around me the way Hop Brook wraps around a stone.

---

I could put Nick somewhere too — a footlocker, a sergeant who looked away, strings that barely leave the wire. Ch 39 already did that work. The reader has his body in a barracks. I don't need to invent him again.

The piano is the part I can't stop hearing.

Somewhere in a practice room — Mass Ave, or West Sixty-Sixth, or a room I have never stood in — a man in his late forties sits at a Steinway with the lid open and his hands resting on his thighs. Not playing. Has not played seriously for himself in years. Plays for students now — voice leading, a chord to make a point, lid down at the hour. Back straight. Feet flat under the pedals. The shape his body settled into one November afternoon on Boylston Street when he stood in a doorway and looked at a wall of reels and said he had a train to catch.

He did not hear what I played tonight. Nobody told him. The broadcast is already gone — sold to a choir in North Carolina. He is alone with a piano that does not forgive hesitation.

His right hand lifts from his thigh. It does not lift because he decides to lift it. It lifts the way a hand lifts from a table when someone calls you from the next room and you are already rising before you know why. It moves over the keys and comes down on the D above middle C — finger on the ivory, key not moving, string not sounding. A beat. Two beats. His left hand comes up. The weight comes down.

D minor. Once. Just the chord.

He nods — very slightly — the way people nod when they finally hear somebody say out loud the thing they had decided they were alone with.

I don't know if this is Jackie Marsh. I don't know where he is tonight. I'm making him up in a Volvo on the Mass Pike at one-twenty in the morning because I need to. He might be out there. He might not. The imagining is what I have.

I drive west. The radio stays off.
