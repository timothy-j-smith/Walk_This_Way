CHAPTER 33: "SOMEBODY TO LOVE"

---

House lights up. Ten minutes.

Jackie Marsh sat on a metal folding chair backstage with the Martin D-28 across his thighs and tuned the low E by ear.

The guitar was there because it kept his hands busy. He had decided a week ago he would not carry it out. The D-28 would stay on its stand. He would walk on and go to the school's Yamaha, and the Yamaha would be the only instrument four hundred and twenty-seven people saw him touch. His father had said nothing about it either way, which had been its own kind of pressure.

The A off the D. The E off the A. One string at a time, patient, the spring in the tuning peg resisting just so before it gave.

Elizabeth stood beside a road case playing G-major scales into her chest, bow moving, eyes on nothing, a pink crease at the left side of her neck where the violin sat. Max Wesslen plucked the open A on the Precision and moved a peg a sixteenth of a turn. Ace Callahan ran one stick around the rim of a practice pad — tick tick tick tick, the dry small clock of a drummer's nerves.

Footsteps on the wing stairs. A measured tread.

"Jackie."

Dr. Richard Marsh stood in the curtain of the stage-left wing. He did not come all the way in. He stopped at the Steinway case, and his right hand — without Jackie seeing — came down and rested for half a second on the polished lid, wide and long-fingered and steady, the hand that had not played since before Jackie was born. He took it off again before he spoke.

"Fine?"

"Fine," Jackie said. "Just nervous."

"Good." Dr. Marsh let the silence do the next thing. Then, very quiet: "Play it the way you hear it." He turned and went back down the stairs to the house.

Jackie stood where he was after the footsteps were gone. His father had never said that sentence before. His father did not know he had said it now.

He laid his own hand flat on the lid where the hand had been. Warm. He took it away.

Someone called three minutes. Jackie set the Martin on its stand. Cold air in through the nose, warm out through the mouth — his father's trick for the pre-concert body. Eleven years of it.

"Hey," he said.

Ace, Max, Elizabeth looked up.

"We're playing Queen because we love Queen. That's it."

"What if we don't win," Ace said.

"Then we don't win."

"A-flat on the second," Max said.

"A-flat on the second."

Elizabeth tightened the horsehair a quarter turn and did not say anything.

One minute. Ace went through the blacks toward his kit. Max slung the Precision. Elizabeth followed with the violin at her side. Jackie walked past the Martin without looking at it.

---

Chuck Morrison's voice rolled down from the PA.

"Ladies and gentlemen. From Sudbury, Massachusetts — Crown Jewels."

The cove lights brightened. Applause — uneven, already sorted, southwest loud, northeast answering with volume instead of hands.

---

The house was bigger from the stage than it had looked from the floor. The lights cleaned depth and left a hot white wall — first three rows as faces, maybe four, past that charcoal haze. Jackie walked to the Yamaha. Half-open on the short stick. He had played this piano for the spring recital in March and the district assembly in April. His father had helped the school buy it in 1976. He had never thought of it as his before tonight.

He sat. Middle C without sound. The key cool. Key-dip two millimeters deeper than the Steinway upstairs. Hammer action leaning a hair to the treble — felted badly, maintenance filing once a year with sandpaper whose grit nobody asked about. A fact. You sit on a bad floor anyway.

Third row aisle: Dr. Richard Marsh a shape in a good suit, dark on dark. Hands on his knees or in his lap — impossible to tell from the bench. Jackie would not know what his father's face did tonight. That would have to be all right.

Elizabeth raised the violin. Ace clicked four on the sticks.

---

"Killer Queen" came out clean.

Sixteenths in the right hand, music-box clockwork rehearsed twenty times in the Marsh basement. He let the Yamaha's treble lean work for him, figures bright and close to the fallboard where the hammers spoke easier. Max walked the roots under Ace's brushes. Elizabeth threaded countermelody an octave above where the horn would have lived on the record.

The southwest section made the sound a crowd makes when expectation gets delivered — front-of-chest murmur, assent. Jackie did not look up. Last chord, pedal hung a breath longer than needed, the hall keeping the note and then not. Applause arrived as the note died.

---

He did not stand.

Nod to Elizabeth. A-flat.

Max found the root on the E string fifth fret, held under his palm. Ace to brushes. Jackie put his left hand on the A-flat second inversion, intro chord, first line into the cardioid at the piano shell. The floor monitor tilted too far back — he leaned to catch his own return. Fine.

"Killer Queen" was jewelry. This was a church.

Left hand into the big octave roll under the verse. Right hand answering up the stacked thirds. Ace's brushes to sticks at the pre-chorus. Max walking under on the off-beats. Elizabeth did not double the melody — he had said no in rehearsal. On the second chorus she played a long bowed descant in fifth position, eight bars, narrowing the field to a spotlight and widening it when she cut out. The southwest section went quiet through it. Jackie did not understand why until he was most of the way through the bridge and realized they were **listening**.

The climb into the last chorus is the climb into the last chorus. The voice has to go up. It has to break a little or it is a lie.

He hit the note on *find me somebody to love* and his voice cracked on the second syllable.

He heard it crack.

His left hand did not adjust. The note held anyway.

Down off the line into the vamp. Max up a fifth. Ace on the ride. Elizabeth's bow line back from under. Last twelve bars the way they had ridden them in Memorial Congregational on Tuesday — except the pews were four hundred folding seats and the lemon-oil smell was floor wax. Jackie lifted the sustain pedal on the downbeat and killed the Yamaha clean. Ace caught the crash. Elizabeth pulled the bow off. Cymbal decay alone for four seconds before the room knew it could clap.

---

Third row aisle: Dr. Richard Marsh's left hand opened once in his lap and closed again. Nobody beside him saw it. Grace Johnson, three rows forward, had both hands on her purse and did not let go.

Maxanne Sartori wrote a number in the originality box and underlined it twice.

---

The hall did not come up all at once. Back first. Then front. Then middle. Not thunder — four hundred separate decisions. Jackie sat at the bench with his hands shaking on his thighs. Sweat at his collar. The A-flat key under his left thumb still warm.

He stood. Elizabeth was already off stage-right. Ace carried the snare. Max slung the bass. Jackie followed through the wing curtain — cooler air, fluorescent backstage, the Martin D-28 on its stand where he had left it. He walked past without looking. It had done its job: the thing he did not pick up.

Stephen Murphy's voice, stage-right, not loud: "Walking the Line. On deck."

Nick Johnson somewhere behind the velvet with a guitar on the strap and a throat gone dry did not answer.

Jackie kept walking.
