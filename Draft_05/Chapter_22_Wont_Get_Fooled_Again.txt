CHAPTER 22: "WON'T GET FOOLED AGAIN"

---

Two hours and eighteen minutes.

Grace Johnson just walked out of a PTA meeting in her Friendly's uniform and told a room full of professionals what they didn't want to hear. Forty-two to eleven. I won't replay her speech — you heard what oak sounds like when someone grips it hard enough.

What you haven't heard yet is how the Battle got its rules. That happened earlier — January 1979, third floor above Studio B, a man in Italian shoes and a portfolio full of charts. Not three years before. Not with two boys' names in my pocket. I didn't know their names yet.

The ON AIR sign pulses. The engineer behind the glass has headphones on and eyes on his phone. I have a paper cup of coffee gone cold and the legal pad photocopy in the folder Tom Andrews filed under *L-S Battle — '79* and forgot about until I asked for it last week.

Won't get fooled again. Pete Townshend wrote that for people who keep signing the same contract with different ink. I signed mine in January. The Battle was still four months out.

---

WBCN's management office sat on the third floor above the studios — a room that had once been somebody's idea of cool and had aged into somebody's idea of a health code violation. Bruise-colored carpet. Gold records on the walls at angles that suggested whoever mounted them had been in a hurry or half in the bag. Aerosmith. J. Geils. Boston. The Cars.

The conference table was pressboard buckled in the middle, ring-stained from ten years of arguments about what rock and roll owed its audience.

Ten AM. January 1979.

Charlie Morris sat at the head — station manager, joint-smoker, believer in letting DJs run free. To his right: Gerald Kowalski. New Program Director, six weeks in from New York. Late thirties, suit in a building where nobody wore suits, leather portfolio the color of money, shoes polished to a funhouse shine.

Tom Andrews sat across from me. Tom — at WBCN since '71, who'd broken J. Geils when Peter Wolf was still his overnight roommate. Tom caught my eye. The smallest nod. *Hang on.*

"Let's talk about the future," Kowalski said.

Charts. Graphs. Demographic columns — 18-24 down twelve percent, automotive sponsors nervous, Clear Channel swallowing independents like pythons swallowing rabbits.

"We need the 18-34 demo," he said. "Or we lose the dollars that keep this station independent."

Charlie tapped his pen. "Gerald has a proposal."

Kowalski spread a single sheet. **WBCN COMMUNITY ENGAGEMENT INITIATIVE — BATTLE OF THE BANDS.**

"Regional high school competition. We brand it. Lincoln-Sudbury Regional to start — their music teacher, Bob Wentworth, is already enthusiastic. Spring '79. Winner gets five hundred dollars and a recording session. Sponsorship packages — Coca-Cola, local dealerships. Repeatable every spring."

Silence. Four seconds.

"Annual franchise," Tom Andrews said. Not a question.

"The data supports it," Kowalski said. "WJLB in Detroit. KROQ in LA. Fourteen percent lift in target demos within two rating periods."

I looked at Tom. In that look: the fork in the road.

"What if we use it to actually find talent?" I said. "Not a brand exercise. Discovery. We send DJs who know the difference between a kid who can play and a kid who can make you feel something."

Tom leaned forward. "If we put our name on it, it has to be authentic. WBCN discovers talent. That's the brand."

Kowalski watched us the way a chess player watches an opponent move where he wants them. "I agree completely. Discovery. Authenticity. That's the narrative we sell."

When the man in the suit agrees with the rebels, the rebels just walked into the cage.

"First Battle — May sixteenth," he said. "Lincoln-Sudbury. Maxanne, I want you to judge. You're the face of WBCN discovery."

"I'll judge," I said. "But I'm writing the rules with Tom."

"Of course. Friday."

Kowalski gathered his portfolio and stood. At the door he paused.

"One more thing. This needs to work. First time, every time. The sponsors are watching." He smiled. "Make it pay."

He said it the way you'd tell a vending machine to deliver the soda after you've already put in the coins.

The door closed. Charlie lit a joint. Tom rubbed his eyes.

"If we write the rules," Tom said. "If we keep the sponsors at arm's length and the kids at the center."

"Then we write the rules," I said.

---

That night. Brookline apartment. Kitchen table. Legal pad. Red wine — one glass, not a bottle.

Entry requirements. Judging criteria — originality and emotional connection weighted double. Prize: five hundred dollars and a Studio C session for the winner.

Article 7, Section 3: *In the event of a tie in the final round, tied contestants shall share the studio recording session equally, collaborating on material of their choosing.*

I read it back. Boring. The kind of line sponsors like — cooperation, youth, no ugly single winner on a press release. Ties happen in contests. I'd seen them. I'd also seen what happens when two kids from opposite sides of a brook land on the same stage and the room wants a victor. The clause was in the closet with everything else — class, spectacle, the machinery that turns music into content. I wasn't thinking about two names. I didn't have two names yet. I was thinking about *make it pay* and a station that needed to survive.

I added the line. Initialled the page. Filed the pad.

I did not know I was writing the door we'd walk through in May. I knew I'd signed another contract with different ink.

---

Two hours and eleven minutes.

The photocopy sits in the folder. Article 7, Section 3 in Times New Roman, third page, between judging weights and liability waiver.

I don't play Queen in the booth — not yet. I cue nothing. I let the dead air sit three seconds, the way we used to before a track we believed in.

"That's the meeting," I say. "January. Not '76. Kowalski and his charts. Me thinking I could keep it real."

I glance at the cassette on the console. I don't pick it up.

"The tie language was one boring line. Standard packet stuff. I wasn't aiming it at Nick Johnson or Jackie Marsh — I hadn't met them. What I did was worse and smaller: I helped build a game where a tie could be useful. Where nobody had to lose ugly in front of a town that was already picking sides."

The engineer looks up. Dead air on the meter. He doesn't say anything.

"In May I'll tell you what I did with that line when it mattered. Tonight I'm telling you where it came from."

I lean into the microphone.

"The Battle is six weeks out. Grace just won her vote. The boys are in their garages. And the rules are already on the copier at Lincoln-Sudbury, third page down, the one nobody reads until they need an exit."
