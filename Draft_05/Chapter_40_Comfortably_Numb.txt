CHAPTER 40: "COMFORTABLY NUMB"

---

Fifty-one minutes.

Kevin runs the block — a Cars deep cut, low in the mix, something safe for the laptop to chew while I leave the chair. The ON AIR sign stays red. I'm still live; the mic is hot in the room even when I'm not leaning on it. My voice has been going four hours. It doesn't feel tired. It feels **used**, the way a good pair of headphones feels used — leather cracked where your head lives, foam gone soft, still the only thing that fits.

"I need five," I tell him through the glass.

He nods without looking up. Twenty-five. Bruins hoodie. Phone face-down for once. Progress.

I take my purse and I walk.

---

The hallway outside Studio B is stacked with cardboard — institutional death in tidy stacks. STUDIO A — EQUIPMENT. ARCHIVES 1971-1980. PROMO — UNSORTED. AWARDS / PLAQUES. The Gold Records are gone. They left clean rectangles on the wall where forty years of nicotine couldn't reach, ghosts the exact shape of things that used to matter.

My heels click. I haven't worn heels in the booth since 1996. Tonight I wore them on purpose. You don't go barefoot when you're walking through your own wake.

The building hum is **wrong**.

For forty-one years WBCN had a heartbeat you could lean your palm on — electricity through the board, signal up the tower, the floor vibrating like a bass note you felt in your teeth. I pressed my hand to this plaster in '76 during a Springsteen broadcast and the wall **moved**. I felt it in '73 when Steven Tyler heard his own voice on the radio for the first time — the look on his face in the hallway afterward, like a man who'd just learned he was real.

Tonight the hum is thin. Mechanical. Bleeding through the Promotions door down the hall — Christian contemporary test tones, a synthesized choir finding pitch in an empty room, the new tenant rehearsing salvation five hours early. K-LOVE moving in before the body is cold. The frequency learning a language it was never built to speak.

I stop. Listen. My skin prickles — not grief yet, **revulsion**. Alive revulsion. The animal part of you that knows when the air in a room has turned.

I keep walking.

---

Reception is stripped to laminate and dust.

The desk where some kid buzzed visitors for decades — promo records, chewed pen on a string, calls from artists who thought they were both — is a shell. Computer gone. Phone gone. A box labeled RECEPTION — MISC holds a tangle of cords and a chipped mug: WBCN — THE ROCK OF BOSTON.

I pick it up. Ceramic cold against my thumb. I put it back. The cold stays on my skin.

---

The station manager's office door is open. Lights on. Nobody home.

I shouldn't go in. Thirty years of not doing what I should have done makes small crimes feel like balance.

The desk holds three items. Telephone. Lamp. Document in clear plastic.

ASSET PURCHASE AGREEMENT

Between: WBCN Radio, Inc. (Seller)

And: Educational Media Foundation (Buyer)

The Globe said it. The Herald said it. The trades covered it like a terminal prognosis. Effective August twelfth, 2009, 12:01 AM, 104.1 becomes automated praise music out of North Carolina.

Below the header, blue ballpoint — somebody's human moment:

*41 years. Thank you.*

Four words. I stand there until my calves ache. The phone rings — once, twice, five times — and nobody answers because nobody works here anymore except ghosts and a kid in a hoodie who thinks rock history started with Nirvana.

I leave the lamp on. I leave the document where it is.

---

ARCHIVES 1971-1980.

I cut the box open with my keys. Tape smell hits first — oxide and cardboard and the particular dust of magnetic memory. Promo singles. Cart labels. Seven-inch reels spine-out, dates in black marker running down the edges.

My handwriting on one spine.

May 1979.

I don't pull the sleeve. The station keeps the master — eleven minutes Mike didn't stop, filed the way institutions file what they can't sell. I already have the dub in my purse. Unauthorized. Warm from thirty years of body heat. That's the one I'm willing to lose on the air.

I close the box. The tape smell follows me down the hall.

---

Back in Studio B the Cars track is ending. Kevin fades it. Through the glass he watches me return — purse in one hand — the way you watch someone carry something that might be explosive.

I sit. The broadcast chair creaks the same way it creaked in 1974. From my purse I take the cassette.

I've carried it Brookline to New York to LA to Nashville and back. Warm from body heat. Label gone the color of weak tea — "Demo - Nick/Jackie - un-Authorized - May 1979" — my block letters blurred but legible. Hairline crack along one edge I Scotch-taped in '93 and check every few months the way you check a pulse that hasn't changed.

It weighs like bird bones.

I set it on the console to the right of the Wheatstone, four inches from the cart machine. Not a plan. An arrangement. Proof laid out beside corporate's picked ending — the way Grace set Dan's watch beside his wedding ring the first night, the way you decide what you're willing to lose.

---

The cart machine holds one cartridge. Masking tape. Sharpie in somebody else's tidy hand:

SHINE ON YOU CRAZY DIAMOND — PINK FLOYD — FINAL TRACK — DO NOT REMOVE

Corporate's picked ending. Twenty-six minutes of elegy committee. Gilmour reaching for a man who wasn't coming back. The right song for a rock station dying — accidentally right, the way a stopped clock is right twice a day.

I pick up the cart. Warm from standby heat. I smell the plastic and for one second I'm twenty-nine in September 1975, shrink wrap on a new LP, needle down, six phone calls during the fade and one of them a man crying who couldn't say why.

I set the cart down. Gentle. Respect isn't numbness. Respect is knowing what you're holding.

---

"You want coffee?" Kevin asks on the talkback.

"I want bourbon."

Pause. "I've got Red Bull."

"That'll have to do."

He retreats. Through the glass his phone stays face-down. Born in '84. Never felt a city lean in when a needle dropped on something nobody had heard yet. Never felt the booth when the board was **alive**.

I pour Heaven Hill into a paper cup from the second drawer — two fingers, thumb measure. One pour. The budget's almost spent.

I lift the cup toward the cart. Toward the cassette. Not a toast speech. A breath.

I drink.

---

You've heard the judges' room. You heard Studio C — Saturday, May nineteenth, four in the afternoon, Yamaha middle C blooming, eleven minutes Mike didn't stop. You heard the garage and the tie and the forty feet of wet pavement. I won't gloss it again. Guilt doesn't need another coat of paint.

Nick enlisted — Army. Alive last I heard. Jackie is in New York. Alive. Juilliard whole-looking, which is its own kind of gone. I won't pretend otherwise tonight.

The Pink Floyd cart waits. The corporate ending. Comfortable. Numb. The easy exit.

The cassette waits. Eleven minutes of unauthorized proof that two kids could still merge when the world had finished sorting them.

Forty-six minutes until I have to choose which truth goes out on a frequency that's already half a ghost.

---

The ON AIR sign holds red.

Outside the booth windows, Boylston shimmers — August heat, glass towers where dive bars used to breed bands, luxury condos where The Rat used to make your grandmother blush. The antenna on the roof points at something nobody wants to look at.

I put the MDR-7506s around my neck — electrical tape on the headband, three black rings where it cracked in '98, the only equipment in this room older than my regrets.

My knuckles crack. One hand. The other.

Down the hall the synthesized choir swells — hopeful, ascending, praise without sweat. The old hum is gone. My pulse isn't.

I lean toward the pop filter.

The board glows amber. Little lights doing their small electrical business. Waiting for a hand that still knows what music is for.

I'm not numb. I'm **electric**.

Final hour. Let's go.
