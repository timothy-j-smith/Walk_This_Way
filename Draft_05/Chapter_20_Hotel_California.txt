CHAPTER 20: "HOTEL CALIFORNIA"

---

Early April, 1979. The Howard Miller grandfather clock in the hall struck six-thirty and the Marshes sat. That was how it worked on Maple Street in southwest Sudbury — the clock began to strike, and by the fourth tone the four of them were at the table with napkins across their laps. The tick kept going after the chime. Seventy-two to the minute. Andante.

The table was mahogany, the grain running the long way, a single dark whorl near the head like a water-stain from someone else's grief. The silver had belonged to Eileen's father — W.B.H. engraved at the shaft end of each knife, W. B. Hollister, a Beacon Hill man who had lived long enough to see his daughter marry a psychiatrist and long enough to let her have the forks. The money in this house had come from her side. Jackie had worked that out by eleven without anyone telling him. Richard had worked out something else — that Hollister forks and a Concord address were a starting line, not a finish, and that the old money in the northwest quarter of Sudbury still looked through people who had only arrived by marriage.

Eileen brought the plates in on a tray. Chicken piccata. The capers sat in a thin gold sauce that caught the chandelier light and threw it back in small wet stars. The chicken pounded thin as stationery and laid in overlapping half-moons. Against the long wall, between the windows and the sideboard, stood the walnut upright Jackie had never seen his father touch. Its ivories had yellowed. Its music stand held an open hymnal nobody sang out of. Above it hung a Rothko reproduction — deep red over deep black, tasteful and just off-center in a way that pretended not to be. The brass switchplate by the door was polished Tuesdays.

"Jackson received his acceptance letter," Eileen said. She had said it twice already today — to Mrs. Caldwell on the phone, to Jackie in the kitchen. This was the performance. "Early admission. Juilliard. Full scholarship."

"Congratulations," Elizabeth said.

"Thank you," Jackie said.

Dr. Marsh set down his wine glass. The base touched the mahogany and made a small definite sound. "We're proud, Jackson." He cut a piece of the chicken with the same clean motion he'd used for twenty years. "I assume you'll keep with Mr. Holloway through graduation. No reason to let the hands go soft before fall."

"Of course."

"The Jordan Hall recital is April twentieth. The program coordinator telephoned on Thursday. They want the Nocturne in E-flat. Opus nine, number two. The one you took to Tanglewood."

Jackie put his fork down. The capers moved in their sauce.

April twentieth was sixteen days out. The Battle of the Bands at Lincoln-Sudbury was May sixteenth — five weeks after the recital, five weeks of Chopin in the cathedral acoustic of Jordan Hall and then Freddie Mercury's gospel choir stacked over A-flat. He could feel both pieces in different parts of his body. The Chopin was in the first and fourth fingers of his left hand. The Queen was in his throat.

"I can do both," Jackie said.

"Do both."

"The recital and the competition."

Dr. Marsh looked at him. Not unkindly. That was the thing — the look never went unkind. "The recital draws reviewers from the Globe, Jackson. Faculty from the Conservatory. That is a career. The other — "

"Is also real."

The words came out faster than he'd meant them. The word *real* sat in the air with the capers and the lemon and the tick of the clock in the hall.

Jackie watched his father's hand go to the wine glass.

Dr. Marsh's fingers closed around the stem. On the word he had not yet spoken but was about to, his first three fingertips curled in against the crystal in a shape the stem did not require — the shape of a hand laid across three strings and pressed. It lasted the breath before the word and was gone when the word came.

"Jackson. This is a high school competition. Sponsored by a rock station. It is beneath your level."

Jackie did not answer.

Eileen's smile did the thing it did, the tightening at the inside corners. "Sweetheart. Your father only means — "

"May I be excused?" Elizabeth said.

"Finish your beans," Eileen said.

Elizabeth ate three beans the way a person counts steps on a staircase she does not want to climb. Then she folded her napkin and left. Her door upstairs did not quite slam. The clock ticked.

"We'll discuss this later," Dr. Marsh said. "When everyone has had time."

"Yes, sir."

Jackie finished the chicken because he had been raised to finish the chicken. Under the table the first and fourth fingers of his left hand walked the Nocturne on his thigh through the denim, andante, seventy-two. His throat remembered the Queen chorus without being asked.

---

Upstairs, the hallway carpet deadened everything. His door closed behind him with the soft brass click of a door hung correctly.

The Henle edition of the Nocturne was open on the Steinway's music stand — E-flat major, Opus 9 No. 2, the *Andante* marking in the engraver's italic. On the shelf above the desk sat the Tanglewood trophy: a brass treble clef on walnut, *Young Artists — First Prize — Piano — 1977*. On the desk lay the Juilliard envelope. Cream laid paper. *The Juilliard School, 144 West 66th Street, New York, NY 10023*. Opened with a letter knife along the top edge, not torn.

Jackie sat on the bed. He did not go to the piano yet.

In the bottom desk drawer, under the old Curtis programs, lay a small Panasonic transistor the size of a deck of cards. The dial was taped at 104.1 with a strip of electrical tape so it would not wander off WBCN in the night. His father did not permit rock stations in the house. Jackie listened at a whisper after midnight with the radio under the pillow, and Maxanne's voice came through it low and steady and went somewhere in his head and stayed.

A knock on the door. "It's me."

"Come in."

Elizabeth was thirteen and small for it. She wore her plaid uniform skirt and one sock to the knee and one sock rolled at the ankle, the asymmetry absolutely deliberate. She sat on the bed cross-legged. Her thumbnail found a loose thread in the chenille bedspread and began to work at it — the slow careful plucking their mother did at the pearls at her throat during the Globe crossword.

"You're going to do the Battle of the Bands anyway," she said.

"Yeah."

"Even though he'll lose his mind."

"Yeah."

Her thumbnail kept at the thread. "Is it because of Nick Johnson?"

Jackie's hand went still on the bedspread. The Juilliard envelope shifted half an inch under his elbow, paper whispering against paper. A small physical thing. He felt it without moving.

"It's complicated," he said.

"You guys used to be best friends."

"Yeah."

"What happened? I always wanted to know but I was afraid to ask."

Jackie looked past her at the window. Maple Street at dusk. The streetlights were coming on in sequence — the Caldwells', then the Petersons', then the Schiffs', each one striking a second behind the last, a small orderly arpeggio of sodium glow down a row of hedges clipped to the same height.

"We grew up," Jackie said. "People change."

"That's what adults say when they don't want to explain things."

The corner of his mouth moved. A breath caught and came out as nothing.

"Yeah," he said. "It is."

Elizabeth pulled the loose thread another eighth of an inch. She did not look at him. The thread came free in her thumbnail and she rolled it into a small tight bead between finger and thumb.

"I heard you practicing last night," she said. "After they went to bed. That Queen song. The one."

"'Somebody to Love.'"

"You sounded different."

"Different how."

She thought about it. She thought about it a long time. The clock downstairs kept its andante. The sodium lamps on Maple Street came on in their order.

"Like you actually cared about the notes," she said, "instead of just hitting them right."

The silence held. Jackie did not fill it. Elizabeth did not fill it. The Juilliard envelope lay under his elbow with its Manhattan address pressed into the paper. Neither of them broke it.

"Don't tell Dad," Jackie said at last.

"Obviously."

She stayed a minute longer. Then she got up and went to the door with her one sock up and her one sock down, and she paused with her hand on the knob.

"Jackie. I hope you win the other one. Not because of Juilliard. Because last night you sounded like a person."

The door closed behind her.

---

Jackie sat where he was until the hallway carpet stopped conducting her footsteps. Then he went to the desk.

The Curtis talent-show photograph stood in a small silver frame next to the lamp. May 1975. Two thirteen-year-old boys on the auditorium stage — Jackie in a clip-on tie, Nick in a flannel shirt with the cuffs rolled — a plywood trophy held between them at chest height, their free arms around each other's shoulders. Nick's grin had a tooth missing. They had figured out, in that photograph, the only thing either of them had ever figured out.

He held it a long time. The desk lamp threw his own seventeen-year-old reflection faintly on the glass over the thirteen-year-old grin.

He opened the bottom desk drawer, laid the photograph face down on the stack of old programs, and closed the drawer.

The house was quiet. The piano was not playing itself.

Jackie crossed to the Steinway and sat and set the Henle edition square on the stand and put his hands on the keys. The fingers found the E-flat chord at the top of the page without being asked, the left hand low and open, the right lifting for the andante. His throat, on its own, remembered the Queen chorus and did not sing it.

He began to play the Chopin.

---

Downstairs, Eileen Marsh went into the small back bedroom she had not invited anyone into since 1966 and closed the door behind her.

The room had a single north window and a floor lamp with a cracked ceramic base she had meant to replace for eleven years. Along the far wall, a deep utility shelf held four canvases turned to the wall, the backs of their stretcher bars the only thing a visitor could have seen if there had been visitors. The topmost was dated in pencil on its stretcher, *9/23/65*. Beside the shelf stood her old easel, the oil stains on the bar the color of winter sunset under forty years of skin cells and dust. Her palette knife lay where she had put it down on the kitchen of a third-floor Huntington Avenue walk-up in the fall of 1959, a week before the acceptance letter from Richard's department chair changed her address for good.

She did not open a canvas. She did not touch the knife. She stood in the middle of the room with her arms loose at her sides and she looked at all of it the way a widow looks at her dead husband's coat closet — an inventory of a life she had agreed to put down, conducted in private so the agreement would hold.

Through the door and up the stairs, the Chopin came to her in fragments. The E-flat opening. The lift into the andante. A measure Jackie played twice. Then a third time. Then the return. When he played the first phrase of anything lyrical her right hand lifted involuntarily to her sternum as if to press a brush back against a canvas that was not there.

She crossed to the easel and stood in front of it. She laid her right hand flat on the wood of the bar where the oil stains were, and the hand remembered. It did the small unconscious thing a hand does when it has held a brush for five hundred hours — the thumb lifted, the first two fingertips curled, the pinky steadied against the heel of the palm — and for half a breath she was twenty-two, standing in a narrow Cambridge kitchen with a canvas on the sink counter and the afternoon light coming sideways across a still life she would never finish. The hand stayed there. It did not move.

From the study across the downstairs hall she heard the particular sound Richard made when he was trying to get up from his chair and the chair was not cooperating — the soft grunt, the scrape of the leg against the floorboard, the pause, the second attempt. He would call her in a moment. He did not like being heard calling her; the call would be quiet.

"Eileen?"

She did not answer immediately. The hand was still on the bar. She looked at the four canvases turned to the wall and she looked at the palette knife and she looked at the easel her hand was flat against, and then she lifted the hand away with the small care a person uses laying down something that did not belong to her in the first place. She turned off the floor lamp. She left the room and closed the door behind her, and her footsteps across the hall were even and unhurried, the footsteps of a woman on her way to the study.

"I'm here," she said. "I'm coming."
