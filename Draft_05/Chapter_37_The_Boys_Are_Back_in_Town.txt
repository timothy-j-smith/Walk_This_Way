CHAPTER 37: "THE BOYS ARE BACK IN TOWN"


---

Stephen Murphy's truck smelled like wet retriever and gear oil. Nick sat in the passenger seat with the window cracked two inches. May air, damp after the rain — fog on the inside of the glass. Sodium lights made the wet Lincoln-Sudbury lot look like a record under a needle.

Grace had taken the wagon home twenty minutes ago. Navy dress, Friendly's double tomorrow, exhausted. *Come home when you come home.* Touch on his elbow. Gone.

Stephen was inside helping David load the Ludwig. Nick had said he'd wait. He did not want the auditorium handshakes — the *great job* from people who meant it and the *great job* from people who didn't.

A tie. He rolled the word against his teeth. Two hundred and fifty dollars. The wagon's transmission slipping in second since March. The money would delay it, not fix it.

Knuckles on the passenger window.

Nick's hand went to the lock. Jackie Marsh stood outside in his father's peacoat, collar up, breath ghosting. The coat Dr. Marsh had worn on Saturday mornings when they were twelve and the world was simple.

Nick rolled the window down three inches.

Jackie held up the contract, paper softening at the folds. "We have to decide about the studio time."

"You take your half."

"Doesn't work that way." He turned the contract toward the dash light. "*Shared.* Together or forfeit."

"Then we forfeit."

"A hundred hours. Professional studio. Engineer."

"I know what it is."

"Do you know what it's worth?"

Jackie's face half-lit, half-shadow, split down the middle.

"What do you want me to say?"

"I want you to get out of the truck."

Stephen came out carrying the kick drum by its lug. Looked at Jackie. Looked at Nick. Big kid who didn't ask questions you didn't invite.

"Stevie. Run us to my house?"

"Yeah. Lemme get the floor tom."

---

Stephen drove them up Route 20 past Store 24 on Route 217, Ludwig rattling under a moving blanket. Nobody spoke. Dash light made Jackie's cheekbone the yellow of old varnish. At Horse Pond Road the Chevy with the bad belt was out again; Stephen lifted two fingers off the wheel.

Thin Lizzy came on the radio — *The boys are back in town* — and Stephen reached for the knob without looking. The song cut to static. He found WBCN mid-sentence and left it there.

Dutton Road. The Johnson house white paint going gray on the north side, bay window dark, kitchen light on. Stephen put it in park.

"Call me tomorrow."

"Yeah."

Jackie got out first. Nick followed with the pawn-shop guitar case — Dan's money, crack in the upper bout packed with wood glue, handle re-riveted the summer Nick was fifteen. Tail lights took Stephen down Dutton toward his own kitchen and his own night.

---

Nick put his shoulder to the bay door.

The springs groaned at quarter past eleven the way they had for twenty years — two-tone complaint that belonged to the house. The door rose. The smell hit them both.

Sawdust and motor oil. WD-40 and cold concrete. Dan Johnson's workshop, preserved past the point where anyone had decided to preserve it. Tools on pegboard in Grace's Sharpie outlines — ghost of the tool each outline held. Bare sixty-watt bulb on exposed Romex. April 1975 Aerosmith calendar above the workbench, Joe Perry in a white scarf. On the Maxwell House lid, Dan's 9/16ths socket, set there the week after the funeral and not moved since.

Red toolbox under the calendar. Latch shut. Paint chipped at the corners. Nick did not look at it.

Jackie stopped in the doorway.

He hadn't been here since the summer before eighth grade. The Brubeck summer. Three weeks on *Take Five* because Jackie said five-four was impossible and Nick said impossible was just a word for things that hadn't been done yet. Dan had brought lemonade in Mason jars with too much sugar and sat on the workbench with his boots swinging, listening like a man watching a magic trick he couldn't explain.

"It's the same," Jackie said.

"Yeah." Nick plugged the Marshall on the milk crate. Tubes warmed to a hum under the bulb's own hum a whole tone lower. "Sit down or go home."

Jackie sat. Pulled a folding chair to the middle of the floor. Eyes went — once — to the red toolbox, then away.

"Behind the salt," Nick said.

Leaning against the workbench behind a bag of Morton's road salt Dan never put out: the Wurlitzer 200A Jackie had left spring of '76. Power cord coiled on top. Three keys stuck. Middle C cracked — timbre bent toward a small broken bell. Jackie pressed it twice, listening.

Nick strapped on the pawn-shop guitar. Hit an E. The walls shivered.

"What do you want to play?"

"*Walk This Way.*"

"Fine."

Nick counted off — one, two, three, four — too fast. Anger tempo. Right hand attacked the riff, all downstrokes, Joe Perry's groove replaced by fury. The garage filled with sound. Notes piled like cars in a chain collision.

Jackie came in on the Wurlitzer too controlled — melody flattened clean and mathematical, every note accountable, no room for accident or breath.

They crashed at the chorus. Nick pushed faster. Jackie pulled tighter.

Nick stopped. Last chord rang out and died against the concrete.

"That was garbage."

"Your tempo —"

"My tempo was fine. You played it like sheet music."

"You played it like a bar fight."

Six feet of oil-stained concrete between them. The Marshall hummed.

"*Bohemian Rhapsody,*" Jackie said.

"No."

"You picked first."

Nick exhaled through his teeth. "Fine."

Jackie counted it proper. Opening chords, hands moving without instruction. Too respectfully — afraid of breaking something.

Nick came in on guitar for the operatic section and overplayed. Notes strained and sharp.

Fell apart at the hard-rock section. Nick heavy. Jackie theatrical. Two different bands in one room.

Silence.

"We can't do this," Jackie said.

"No."

"We sound like strangers."

"We *are* strangers."

The Marshall hummed. Headlights swept the small window above the workbench and vanished. Somewhere in the house a faucet ran and stopped.

The side door opened.

Grace stood in the frame, backlit by the kitchen. Friendly's sweatshirt. White Bradlees sneakers with the new insoles. Hair down. Tired past sleep into something structural.

She looked at Nick. At Jackie. At the Wurlitzer plugged in on the folding chair.

"Your father would've liked that," she said. "Both of you winning."

She went back inside. Door clicked shut.

---

The silence after was different. The silences before had been walls. This one was a window.

Nick sat on the workbench edge, guitar in his lap, heel of his hand muting the strings. Jackie stared at the closed door.

"Don't," Nick said.

"He used to bring us lemonade."

"I said —"

"Mason jars. Too much sugar. Sat right where you're sitting."

Nick's throat closed. The bench was solid — Dan had built it from reclaimed oak, sanded smooth enough to run a palm across. Grain through his jeans.

"Remember the Brubeck?" Jackie said.

Nick's hands loosened. A harmonic escaped — thin, bright, gone.

"Yeah."

"*Take Five.* You said five-four was impossible."

"You said that."

"Did I?"

"You sat right there and said *nobody plays in five-four*, and I said *Brubeck does*, and you said *Brubeck is an alien.*"

Something moved in Jackie's face. Not a smile — too much scar tissue — but the muscles around his eyes loosened, and for a second he was the kid with lemonade who believed music was the only honest language.

"Want to try?" Jackie said.

"You know what."

Nick counted off slower. Not angry. Not controlled. Honest.

Guitar found the *Sweet Emotion* bass line clean — no distortion, notes breathing the way Dan used to say you let wood breathe before you sealed it. Once. Twice. Finding the groove.

Jackie played one chord. D minor. Let it hang. Played it again, added a ninth — door opening into a room locked two years.

Nick's hands responded before his brain could object. Aerosmith groove inside Queen harmony. Two songs breathing the same air.

Jackie added melody — tentative. *Is this the real life* over the *Sweet Emotion* groove. Nick looked once. Jackie's eyes closed, right foot tapping quarter notes on the concrete, loafer heel catching grit. Looked back at the fretboard.

The guitar started to sing the way it only sang when Nick forgot to be angry — hands talking to the instrument instead of fighting it. Fragments of *Walk This Way* crept in as texture, thread in the weave, not a separate song.

Tools vibrated on the pegboard. Screwdriver walked to the edge of its hook. Phillips-head chattered against wood. The 9/16ths socket rolled a quarter-inch on the Maxwell House lid and stopped. Concrete hummed under their feet. Joists overhead picking up a sympathetic drone not in either instrument but somehow in both. The red toolbox did not move. Jackie's eyes went to it once — latch shut, paint chipped — then back to the broken middle C.

Six minutes. No structure. No arrangement. Two musicians remembering what their hands had always known and their lives had made them forget.

When they stopped, the silence rang.

Nick's fingers trembled on the fretboard. Adrenaline. Or something older.

"That's what we could have been," he said.

Jackie looked at him. Two years of silence. Two years of choosing sides and crossing streets. "We could still do it."

The words sat in the garage like a chord nobody resolved.

Nick stared at the pegboard. Jackie stared at the Wurlitzer. Neither one said yes. Neither one said no.

"We forfeit if we don't use it," Jackie said finally. "Both of us. That's the deal."

"Yeah."

Nick went through the side door into the kitchen. Grace was at the table with a glass of water and her checkbook open to a page she wasn't writing on — the way she looked when she was waiting for a sound she couldn't name.

The WBCN card was still in his wallet from March. 104.1 FM. Edges soft where his thumb had worn the cardboard. He unfolded the contract from the Battle and found the station number on the back in Tom Andrews's block print.

He dialed. Jackie stood in the doorway behind him, arms crossed, not coming all the way in.

Three rings.

"WBCN."

"It's Nick Johnson. Jackie Marsh is here." He looked at Jackie. Jackie didn't look away. "We want to use the studio time. The shared hours."

A pause — not long, the pause of a woman who already knew what the contract said.

"Both of you?"

"Both of us."

"Saturday. Four o'clock. Studio C, Boylston Street. You drive yourselves. You walk in together. You forfeit if one of you doesn't show." Another beat. "Don't be late."

She hung up.

Nick set the receiver in the cradle. The kitchen clock said eleven-seventeen.

"Saturday," he said.

Jackie nodded once. "Four o'clock."

Grace closed the checkbook. She didn't ask where Boylston was. She didn't offer to drive. She looked at both of them the way she had looked at the Wurlitzer through the side door — tired, careful, not filling the room with hope it couldn't afford.

"Keys are on the hook," she said to Nick. That was all.

---

Jackie called home from the wall phone by the refrigerator. Nick heard one side of it — *yes*, *Saturday*, *I'll find a ride* — and then Jackie hung up and stood with his hand still on the receiver like he didn't trust his legs.

"Stephen can drop me Saturday," he said. "I'll meet you there."

"I know where it is," Nick said.

They looked at each other for a second that didn't become anything. Then Jackie went out through the side door into the damp May dark. Footsteps on the drive. A car door — not Grace's wagon, Stephen's truck starting up the road.

Nick pulled the bay door down slow — both hands, easing the springs the last six inches so the counterweight didn't slam, the way Dan had taught him. He did not look at the toolbox on the way out.

Thursday and Friday would be school. Hallways. Forty feet. The question mark still hanging.

Saturday was three days away.
