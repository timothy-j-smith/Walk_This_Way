CHAPTER 10: "DREAM ON"

May 15, 1975.

Ephraim Curtis Middle School gymnasium. Seven-thirty PM.

The folding chairs had been arranged in rows by Mr. Petrocelli, the custodian, who'd waxed the basketball court that afternoon with the same lemon-industrial compound he used every Thursday, so the whole room smelled like floor polish and the particular sourness that happens when two hundred people sit close together under fluorescent lights on a warm spring evening. The lights hummed at a frequency you couldn't hear so much as feel behind your eyes — a sixty-cycle drone that flattened every color to the same institutional yellow-green. Programs had been photocopied on the school's Xerox machine, the ink still tacky, and parents fanned themselves with them because the gymnasium had no air conditioning and somebody had propped open the fire doors, letting in the May air that smelled like cut grass and warm asphalt and did nothing to help.

Parents sat in their good clothes. Kids fidgeted. Teachers patrolled the aisles like nervous ushers, shushing the sixth-graders who were already throwing wadded-up programs at each other.

Backstage was a storage room that smelled like rubber kickballs and chalk dust. Karen Bowers was in the corner doing vocal exercises, her voice climbing chromatic scales with the mechanical precision of a music box while her mother adjusted the collar of her navy dress for the third time. Karen would sing "Tomorrow" from Annie, the closing act, the slot everyone understood was reserved for the winner.

Nick and Jackie sat on a folding table near the emergency exit, their acoustics resting against their thighs. Nick's was the pawn-shop Fender with the crack in the pickguard. Jackie's was his birthday Gibson, the frets worn smooth in the first position from six months of daily work. They weren't warming up. They were just sitting, the way you sit when you've rehearsed something so many times your hands know it better than your brain does and all that's left is waiting.

"You ready?" Nick asked.

Jackie pressed his thumb across the low E string and released it. A quiet thud. "Yeah."

That was all they said.

In the audience, Dan Johnson sat with his work boots still on, concrete dust in the creases of his jeans. He'd come straight from the job site because Grace told him he was coming. Grace sat beside him in her blue church dress, her hands folded in her lap.

Six rows back, Dr. Richard Marsh sat with Eileen. He wore a gray suit, no tie. He'd come because Jackie asked him to, which was unusual enough that Eileen had noted it without comment. Beside him, Elizabeth was reading the program with the critical eye of someone who'd already found three spelling errors.

The first five acts were what they were. "Für Elise" on a piano tuned a quarter-tone flat. A boy whose card tricks kept slipping. Three girls whose harmony drifted like a radio signal from a distant station. A kid reaching for Jimmy Page and landing somewhere around a campfire in the Berkshires. The audience clapped after each act with the polite enthusiasm of people fulfilling an obligation.

Then Mr. Hennessey, the vice principal, read from an index card. "Next up — Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh."

They walked out carrying their guitars by the necks. No music stands. No sheet music. Two thirteen-year-old boys in jeans and t-shirts — Nick in a faded Aerosmith shirt with a hole near the collar, Jackie in a plain white tee his mother had ironed that afternoon. They sat in two chairs at center stage and adjusted their guitars in their laps. Nick tilted his chair back on two legs the way he always did, then let it drop forward.

They looked at each other. Nick raised his eyebrows a fraction of an inch. Jackie nodded.

Nick's right hand came down on the strings.

The opening riff of "Walk This Way" hit the gymnasium like a sentence in a language the room didn't know it spoke. Not the album version — you couldn't get that from a single acoustic. What Nick played was Joe Perry's riff stripped to its rhythmic bones, all muted downstrokes and hammer-ons, the strings slapping against the frets with a percussive crack that bounced off the cinderblock walls and the waxed floor and came back harder. He played it with his palm half-damping the bridge, so the notes came out short and punchy and mean, more rhythm than melody, the guitar turned into a drum that happened to have pitch.

Four bars in, Jackie entered.

Not with "Walk This Way." With the descending piano chords from "Bohemian Rhapsody," transposed for guitar, fingerpicked — each note placed separately, deliberately, the way you'd set glasses on a table. The chords dropped through the spaces between Nick's riff like water through a grate. Where Nick's guitar was all attack and percussion, Jackie's was sustain and air. The notes hung and opened the sound upward while Nick's riff drove it forward, and for a beat — maybe two — the audience didn't know what they were hearing.

Then the two patterns locked.

It happened on the fifth bar. Nick's riff landed on the downbeat and Jackie's chord caught the upbeat, and the syncopation clicked into place like a latch finding its strike plate. The Aerosmith groove gave the Queen harmony a pulse. The Queen harmony gave the Aerosmith groove a ceiling. Together the two acoustics produced a sound bigger and stranger than either song alone.

The audience stopped moving. Not the polite stillness of attention. The other kind — the kind where the woman three rows back stops fanning herself with her program and doesn't notice.

Nick sang first. The verses of "Walk This Way" in his rough, untrained voice — a voice that cracked on the high notes and didn't care, that sounded like a kid who'd learned to sing by singing along to records in a garage with the door closed. He leaned into the microphone and the words came out hot and a little too loud, the way Aerosmith was supposed to be too loud.

Then Jackie answered. Not the next verse. The "Is this the real life" passage from "Bohemian Rhapsody," his voice clear and placed, each note supported from the diaphragm the way his teacher had drilled into him, a choirboy's instrument carrying Freddie Mercury's question. His tone cut through the room the way a bell cuts through traffic — clean, high, impossible to ignore.

Nick's voice was gravel. Jackie's was glass. In the call and response they'd built over five months in Dan Johnson's garage, Nick threw a line out raw and Jackie caught it and sent it back polished, and then Nick roughed it up again.

The middle section was where they'd spent the most rehearsal time and it showed. Jackie's guitar shifted into the arpeggiated bridge of "Bohemian Rhapsody" — the operatic section, all ascending figures and dramatic pauses — while Nick wove the "Walk This Way" riff underneath in a lower register, palm-muted to a whisper, just enough to keep the pulse alive. Their voices braided on a harmony they'd invented themselves, not Freddie's and not Tyler's but a third thing, a major third that split the difference between precision and abandon.

Then the crash. Both guitars open-strummed, full volume, both voices rising on the final chorus — Nick's rasp and Jackie's clarity fused into a sound that rattled the folding chairs and buzzed in the fluorescent light casings and hit the back wall and kept going, through the propped-open fire doors, into the May evening.

They ended together. A single chord, struck hard, left to ring.

The silence lasted three seconds. Nick glanced at Jackie. Jackie glanced at Nick.

Grace Johnson pressed both hands to her mouth. Her eyes were wet.

Then the gymnasium broke open. Not applause — noise. Parents standing. Kids yelling. Mr. Hennessey clapping with his hands above his head, which no one had ever seen him do. Mrs. Stephens, the librarian who'd shushed them a thousand times, on her feet with her fist in the air like she was at a concert and not a middle school event in a room that smelled like floor wax.

Six rows back, Dr. Richard Marsh did not stand. He sat very still. His left hand had opened once in his lap, thumb and pinky spanning an octave the hand had not been asked to hold in a very long time, and then it had closed again. Elizabeth was standing on her chair, clapping, her program on the floor.

Karen Bowers sang "Tomorrow." Her voice was flawless. Every note arrived on time, technically irreproachable. The audience clapped when she finished — polite, appreciative, the way you clap for something you admire but that hasn't changed the shape of the room you're sitting in.

The judges deliberated for eleven minutes. Everyone already knew.

"First place — Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh."

They walked back onto the stage grinning, holding their guitars, and the applause hit them again and they stood in it. Dan Johnson was on his feet, work boots on the waxed floor, clapping in the slow hard way of a man who built things with those hands every day and knew solid construction when he heard it. Grace stood beside him, her mascara ruined, laughing and crying at the same time.

Someone had a Polaroid.

The photograph: two thirteen-year-old boys, arms around each other's shoulders, guitars at their sides. Nick's grin enormous, crooked, his Aerosmith shirt riding up. Jackie's smile quieter but no less total, the kind that uses the whole face. Behind them, the banner — EPHRAIM CURTIS MIDDLE SCHOOL ANNUAL TALENT SHOW 1975 — in hand-painted letters on butcher paper, folding chairs askew, programs on the floor.

Dan drove the Johnsons home. Windows down, the May air carrying the smell of lilac from someone's yard on Dutton Road. Nick sat in the back seat holding his guitar case and the first-place ribbon and he couldn't stop talking — the riff, the moment the harmony locked, the look on Karen Bowers' face. Grace reached into the back seat and squeezed his knee, once, and let go. Dan watched the road and said nothing, but in the rearview mirror his eyes were bright.

At the Marsh house, Jackie came through the door still holding the ribbon. Elizabeth said, "You were the best thing that's ever happened in that gym, including basketball," and went upstairs.

Dr. Marsh was in his study. Jackie stood in the doorway.

"Your harmonics were good," his father said quietly. "The voicing in the bridge. That was sophisticated work."

Jackie didn't move. His father had never commented on his rock arrangements. Not once.

"Goodnight, Jackie."

"Goodnight, Dad."

Jackie sat on his bed with the ribbon in his hand and the window open to the May night. From down the street, through the trees, the slow rhythmic tock-tock-tock of someone's sprinkler. He thought about the fifth bar when the riff and the chord locked. He thought about Nick's face when the silence broke. He thought about his father's left hand opening once in his lap and closing again.

On Dutton Road, Nick lay on his bed with his hands behind his head, still hearing the sound. In the kitchen, Dan and Grace talked in low voices, and he could hear them laughing — Grace's high laugh and Dan's low one, two tones braiding the way two voices will when they've known each other long enough to harmonize without trying.

The Polaroid sat on Nick's desk, still tacky, the colors still deepening. Two boys. Two guitars. The gymnasium behind them already emptying, the folding chairs being stacked, the banner coming down.

But in the photograph, they're still up there. Still grinning. Still thirteen.

May 15, 1975. A Thursday.
