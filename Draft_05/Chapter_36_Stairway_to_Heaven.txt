CHAPTER 36: "STAIRWAY TO HEAVEN"


---

The Regulators had cleared the stage forty minutes ago — Framingham kids, loud, forgotten by the time Rumours walked on barefoot.

Now the guidance-office conference room off the main hall smelled like burned coffee and indecision. Four metal folding chairs around a formica table. Fluorescent tube on uneven ballast, low hum under the voices like a note somebody had left on. Coffee urn brewed at five, color of wet tar. Sloppy joe smell when the door cracked. Nobody had cracked it in twenty minutes.

Maxanne Sartori sat with her back to the door, fingers on a styrofoam cup she hadn't drunk from. Carter Alan leaned on his elbows, jaw working. Bob Walker from Strawberries stood at the window watching the parking lot fill with headlights.

"We've been at this forty minutes," Carter said.

"I'm aware." Maxanne set the cup down. A ring bloomed on the table.

"Walking the Line," Chuck Morrison's voice wasn't in the room — he was stacking chairs in the auditorium — but his scores were. Carter tapped the legal pad. "Johnson's *Dream On* — that wasn't a cover. That was a conversation."

"Crown Jewels," Bob said from the window, not turning. "Marsh on *Somebody to Love*. Session control. The sister on violin — architecture, not rock."

"Rumours," Carter said. "The Lally girl. You felt that room."

Bob nodded once. "Felt it. Not a tie between three bands. Between two sides of town."

Maxanne had been quiet through most of it. Clipboard on her knees. Technical execution. Arrangement. Stage presence. Originality weighted double — language she and Tom had written in January so discovery could win on paper. Also weighted so neither side of Hop Brook would win clean on arithmetic alone. She had known that going in.

The numbers had landed close. The room outside was already humming with sides.

"Can you honestly pick one?" she said.

Carter looked at her. Bob turned from the window. The fluorescent buzz filled the space where an answer should have gone.

Carter shook his head.

Bob looked at his hands. "No."

Maxanne pulled the packet from her bag — photocopied on the Lincoln-Sudbury office machine, toner fading at the margins. Page three. Article 7, Section 3.

*In the event of a tie in the final round, tied contestants shall share the studio recording session equally, collaborating on material of their choosing.*

Boring line. Sponsor-friendly. She had added it at her kitchen table in January, before she knew Nick Johnson's name, before she met Jackie Marsh in a garage on Dutton Road in March. An exit for a room that might need one. Not a trap aimed at two boys who didn't exist yet for her.

She saw it now anyway.

"Shared studio time," she said. "Split prize. Per the packet — collaborative use or forfeit."

"That's not judging," Carter said. "That's paperwork."

"Yes," Maxanne said. "It is."

Silence filled the room the way water fills a glass — completely, with nowhere for air to go.

"Then it's a tie," Bob said.

Maxanne did not recuse herself. She did not throw out the clause. She did not pretend she didn't see it.

She wanted them both in Studio C. She wanted the photograph. She wanted the story. She used the machinery.

---

The auditorium held four hundred and twenty-seven people and sounded like twice that.

Nick Johnson stood stage left with Stephen Murphy, David Cassel, and Mark Pianka behind him. Wentworth's butterscotch Tele on the strap. Jackie Marsh stood stage right — Elizabeth with her violin case at her thigh, Ace and Max flanking him.

Between the bands: twelve feet of stage boards taped in black gaffer, and a single Shure SM58 on its chrome stand, cable coiled at the base.

Maxanne walked to the microphone. House noise dropped to murmur, then breathing.

"This has been one of the finest competitions I've witnessed." The FM warmth that had introduced Aerosmith to Boston in '73 came out like she'd never left the booth. "Both bands performed at a level that would be remarkable for professionals. For high school musicians, it borders on the extraordinary."

Pause. Dead air the way a drummer uses space between beats — the long climb before the verse you're not sure you've earned.

"The judges' decision is unanimous. The 1979 WBCN Battle of the Bands ends in a tie."

The auditorium inhaled.

Not cheering. Not booing. The confused electric sound of four hundred people processing something that wasn't in the script. Murmur, scattered applause, murmur again. A whistle from the back, high and piercing.

"Prize money of five hundred dollars split equally. Two hundred and fifty to Walking the Line. Two hundred and fifty to Crown Jewels."

She held up the contract. Paper caught the par-can light.

"Recording time at WBCN Studio C on Boylston Street — shared. Per the contract, used collaboratively by both bands — or forfeited entirely."

Now the murmuring stopped. Now the room understood.

Nick's head turned toward Jackie. Jackie's head turned toward Nick. Twelve feet of boards. Microphone at knee height. Light through the margins of the paper Maxanne held.

"Gentlemen. Come forward and sign the award acknowledgment."

They walked from opposite directions and met at the mic like two tides that didn't want to touch. Maxanne held the contract between them. Nick took the left edge. Jackie took the right. Shoulder to shoulder, same piece of paper, neither looking at the other.

Two hundred and fifty dollars. Grace could use it. The studio came with a chain: shared, collaborative, with Jackie Marsh, who had not spoken to him since the funeral.

Jackie stared at the words. His father would tell him to forfeit. Walk away clean. Take the money and the moral victory and pack for Juilliard.

They signed. Separate pens. Same line.

---

Third row left: Grace Johnson stood.

She didn't applaud. She didn't shout. Straight-backed, dry-eyed — the iron that got her through double shifts and mortgage payments and PTA votes. Navy dress with the buttons. Winter coat folded over the seat behind her. She would not wear this dress again after tonight.

Her son on stage holding a contract with the Marsh boy. Dan in her head, clear as one seat to the right: *Take it. We need it.*

Grace sat. Hands folded in her lap. Waited.

---

Three rows back on the aisle: Dr. Richard Marsh unclenched his hands.

Red grooves across his palms where the armrests had been. He looked up at Jackie in the stage lights with someone else's contract in his hand — one flashing second of himself at twenty-two, fallboard lowering, sheet music into a box sealed before sundown. The rational choice.

The pinky of his left hand uncurled once, an octave-reach his thumb had not been asked to make since he was twenty-two. He closed his hands again. Gently. The marks would fade.

---

Back row: Patty Lally sat alone.

She hadn't won. Her name wasn't on the contract. Wouldn't be in the Sudbury Town Crier tomorrow. Wouldn't be mentioned at Friendly's when the town replayed the Battle.

She had played *Go Your Own Way* barefoot with her eyes closed and her voice wide open, and for four minutes the auditorium had belonged to her — a girl from the pig farms who sang Fleetwood Mac because Stevie Nicks knew what it meant to love something already leaving.

She hummed it now. Quiet. *Go your own way.*

People filed out. Suburbans and pickups and Volvos in the lot. Families on the sidewalk, animated — everyone had an opinion, no one sure what had happened.

Patty didn't move. She'd wait until the auditorium emptied. Then walk.

---

Outside, rain finally broke over Sudbury — not the held thunder from seven o'clock, real rain on new maple leaves, sodium lamps throwing shadow on wet asphalt. Peepers from the dump half a mile west, thin silver on the low air.

Nick and Jackie came out separate doors, contract folded between them like a custody agreement for something that hadn't been born yet. Kitchen windows lit down the hill. Route 20 hiss east toward Wayland.

Two hundred and fifty dollars would pay an insurance bill somewhere. Two hundred and fifty would sit in a savings account thick with Juilliard money, barely noticed.

A hundred hours of studio time hung in the air between them, unclaimed.

Grace came out the lobby with her coat over her arm, walked to the wood-paneled wagon in the second row. Dome light on, off. Engine caught on the second turn. She sat with hands on the wheel before she put it in gear.

On Route 20 a quarter mile east, Patty was already walking — boots in her left hand, guitar case in her right, hum in her throat.

From a car pulling out of the lot, two bars of Led Zeppelin bled through an open window before the door shut and the rain swallowed it. *Stairway to Heaven.* The intro climbing. Then gone.

Nick stood under the shop-corridor overhang with the contract in his jacket pocket. Jackie stood under the east entrance awning with his in his. Forty feet of wet pavement between them.

Neither crossed it.
