CHAPTER 17: "BRIDGE OVER TROUBLED WATER"

---

The second Saturday in October, 1978. Eleven months since the closed lid on Dutton Road — near enough to a year that Nick had stopped counting and started rounding. The woods between the Johnson house and Cavicchio's back field were conservation land now, the old stone walls running through them waist-high, moss-headed, the farmers who'd stacked them a century gone and the sheep gone longer than that.

Nick led on the CR125. He'd rebuilt it over two summers out of scrap Dan had let him haul home from the dump — a '72 frame, a '74 engine set in one August Saturday with a jack and a two-by-four, which was how you did it if you were Dan and his son. The pull-start took three today before the plug cleared. The bark of the two-stroke came up through his wrist thin at idle, angry at half-throttle, the pre-mix sweet in the cold air. The grips had worn smooth to the shape of his hands. The handguards were Cavicchio-mud, spring-brown, because Nick didn't wash a bike for weather he'd just put back in the weather.

The trail was their runway. You rode it because you owned it, and you owned it because nobody else wanted it, and you didn't own much else.

Behind him: Stephen Murphy on a YZ80 with a bent kickstand, which meant Stephen parked it leaned against whatever tree was nearest. Behind Stephen: Mark Pianka, who'd grown four inches over the summer and whose jacket sleeves rode up past the wristbones, so his thumbs showed pale at the throttle when he came on the pipe. Nick didn't call them a rhythm section. He just knew what a rhythm section felt like at his back.

The trail climbed a low rise through the pines and opened on the bridge.

The bridge was older than either family's time in town. A CCC crew had put it in for a fire road in the thirties; some county man had patched the deck in the fifties with a sheet of diamond-plate steel where a plank had rotted through, bolted into the oak with carriage bolts gone orange at the heads. In spring the brook under it ran loud as a kitchen sink. In October Hop Brook ran amber and thin, and you couldn't hear it except right under the bridge, where a flat slate rock split the current and made it say something.

Jackie was on the bridge.

Nick rolled the throttle off. The CR125 dropped to an idle and the idle filled the clearing. Jackie had both hands in the pockets of a Boston University windbreaker — his father's, Nick had known the jacket since they were twelve — and he was looking at the water like the water might answer something. He looked up when the engine came through the pines.

For a beat neither of them moved.

Nick's front tire was four feet from Jackie's loafers. Stephen and Mark had rolled up behind and let their engines idle down to the same nothing-sound. Three bikes coughing in a clearing and the brook under its rock.

"You need to move," Nick said.

"It's a walking bridge," Jackie said, not moving.

The almost happened then. The corners of both their mouths did a thing neither of them had asked them to do. Nick saw it on Jackie and Jackie saw it on Nick and for one half-count of the pipe they were thirteen again in a garage that smelled like sawdust, running a change that nobody in Sudbury could play but the two of them, together.

Then the footsteps came down the path.

---

Dr. Richard Marsh walked the conservation loop every Saturday in every month that wasn't January. He walked it in pressed khakis and rubber-soled Rockports and, today, the bottle-green half-zip he kept for mornings the windbreaker wasn't on its hook. His son had borrowed the windbreaker without asking. Richard had seen the absence on the hook and felt a small pleased thing and taken the half-zip down without comment.

He came up the path and saw his son on the bridge and three dirt bikes across from him, and his first thought was protective and clean. He came onto the plank deck. His eyes moved over the four boys the way they moved over an X-ray — pattern first, detail after. The lead boy he recognized. The Johnson boy. He had been in the house once, four years ago, holding a Coca-Cola in both hands the way a boy holds a Coca-Cola when he has not been in many houses like this one.

Richard's eyes moved down to the boy's left hand on the throttle grip.

The fingertips were callused in a line across the tops — not on the pads, on the very tops, the way a year of pressing steel strings into a fretboard too tall for a boy's hand shaped the skin and kept shaping it. Richard was not a guitarist. He had never owned one. But a musician's hand was a musician's hand; he had lived in conservatory practice rooms long enough to know the shape of sustained commitment even when it wore a different grain than his own. The boy's thumb rode loose at the heel of the palm. The first knuckle carried a little white scar, not from labor. Something moved in Richard's chest that he had not invited and did not name. He looked away.

"Everything all right, Jackson?"

His son didn't answer right away.

"These boys bothering you, son?"

---

*These boys.*

Jackie felt the two words land the way a bruise lands — not at the surface, underneath. His mouth closed down on the thing that had almost happened, and his face put itself on the way he put it on at Mrs. Caldwell's Sunday recitals, the face his father thought was composure.

His lungs were tight with cold air. Pine sap had come off a low branch onto the khakis on his left thigh, a dark streak. His father's Old Spice came off the windbreaker in small warm waves when he moved — the smell of every morning he'd watched his father leave the house — and the smell now felt like a hand on his shoulder he had not asked for.

"No, Dad," Jackie said. "I was just telling them it's a walking bridge."

The almost-smile was gone. He had felt it go. Some muscle at the corner of his mouth that the body had kept ready for Nick for five years gave up its readiness, and the face was the face again.

He stepped aside.

He didn't decide to step aside. His legs did it before he did. Two measured steps to the right, the planks spongy under the loafers, his weight coming off the centerline of the bridge and onto the downstream rail — the same two steps he'd learned for recital wings and cocktail-party doorways.

---

Nick heard *these boys* through the CR125 idle and heard it clean.

Something in his chest wanted to kill the engine and step off the bike and say *Jackie, it's us.* Something else in his chest wanted to put a fist into the Boston University windbreaker. The thing that won was neither. His right wrist tightened on the throttle half a turn, and the pipe bark jumped from nothing to something, and his jaw hinge tightened against itself, and the inside of his cheek cut against a molar and he tasted the small bright copper of his own blood.

He opened the throttle.

The front tire rolled onto the metal plate.

The plate rang.

One note, clean — the note a horseshoe makes dropped on a rail — the whole bridge saying it at once. Then the oak planks took the weight and groaned, the deep dry groan of old white-oak under a load it had not been asked to take, and under the planks the stringers shook, and from the underside of the stringers a fine red rain-rust sifted down into the amber water.

Stephen came next. The plate rang. The planks groaned. Mark came last. The plate rang a third time, flatter, because the plate was warm now.

Four bikes on a walking bridge. Three rings. Three groans. The stringers complaining in a register Nick felt up through the footpegs and into his shins.

He did not look back.

On the far bank the trail opened into the fire road, and the fire road ran down toward Cavicchio's back field, and the corn there was cut to stubble and the crows were working the rows in that patient black way crows work a field in October. Nick rolled through the stubble edge and opened the throttle, and the engine bark got smaller in the woods behind him, and then there was no bark at all — just the small flat sound of Hop Brook under its rock, and the smell of hot two-stroke oil staying in the pines the way a thing stays after the thing that made it has gone.

---

Stephen peeled off at the Y for home. Mark peeled off at Dutton. Nick did not go home.

He circled back the long way — past the stone wall where the sheep had been and weren't, up the rise through the pines — and came down on the bridge from the south side, alone, and cut the engine.

The silence came back with the smell of hot oil.

Jackie was gone. Dr. Marsh was gone. The plate where Nick's tire had rung it was warm still — he could feel it through the sole of his boot when he set the foot down — and a little dust of rain-rust lay in a half-moon at the downstream edge of the plate where the stringers had shaken it loose.

He sat the bike and looked at his left hand on the grip.

Nobody had ever looked at his hand the way the man in the half-zip had looked at it. He did not know what the look had meant.

In his jacket pocket, three Fender mediums — tortoiseshell, worn smooth at the edges — clicked against each other when he shifted his weight. He set his palm flat over the pocket to keep them quiet and rode home the long way, past the corn stubble, with the crows lifting and settling behind him in twos.
