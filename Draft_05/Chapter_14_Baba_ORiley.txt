CHAPTER 14: "BABA O'RILEY"

April in Sudbury was all mud. Hop Brook ran brown under the Route 117 bridge, loud enough to hear from the Lincoln-Sudbury front walk, and the lawn in front of the school had gone to the color of a boot print that had filled with water and frozen and thawed a dozen times since March. The parking lot was a lattice of tire ruts. A kid came up the walk, stepped wrong, went in past the ankle of his sneaker, said a word his mother wouldn't like, pulled his foot out with a sucking sound, and kept walking. Nobody looked. April.

Seven-forty-five. First bell in four.

Carl Marquis's '66 Impala came in off Lincoln Road from the south, throwing gravel, the muffler louder than it had been yesterday. Nick was in the passenger seat, book bag between his feet, a quarter-eaten Hostess donut in his hand, his hair still wet from the shower he'd taken at six-thirty. The radio was on an AM station playing something neither good nor bad — noise to fill the seven miles from Marquis's driveway to the school lot. The speaker in the driver's door buzzed on the high notes. It had buzzed since Carl and his father put the dash back together in November. It was just the sound the car made now.

Nick got out. He slammed the door the way you slammed the door of a car like that — a second slam, because the first one never caught.

Up the walk, twenty yards away, a wood-paneled Buick Estate wagon pulled up to the curb. The wagon was always clean. Dr. Richard Marsh had Saturday-morning habits about wax and a chamois, and it showed. Jackie got out of the passenger side, closed the door with the small respectful click a kid closed a clean car with, and lifted his hand once to the driver's-side window without looking at his father's face. The Buick pulled away. Jackie stood on the curb a beat longer than he had to, adjusting the strap of his book bag, then moved up the walk.

His loafers were polished. The bottoms of his jeans were an inch dark with Sudbury mud because he had walked across the lawn yesterday to save thirty seconds. He noticed it now, the small pull of dried mud against the denim at every step.

Nick was fifteen yards to his left, coming in from the parking lot at a diagonal. He saw Jackie and his head turned before his mind caught up — old habit, muscle memory from a year when they had walked in together every Monday with brown bags and plans for a band. Then Nick looked down at his own boots, at the donut in his hand, at the cigarette butt somebody had ground out in the gravel by his toe, and kept walking. His line and Jackie's line converged toward the same set of double doors and stopped converging eight feet before the doors, because Jackie went toward the right-hand door and Nick went toward the left, and they passed through at the same moment through doors hinged at different walls, and neither one looked at the other, and then they were inside and the hallway noise took them and they went in separate directions without a sentence between them.

The bell rang. In the window of the front office, a secretary picked up a rotary phone and began to dial.

---

Third period. Bay three.

The auto shop sat at the far end of the east wing — cinderblock walls painted the color of instant mashed potatoes, corrugated steel doors that rolled up on greasy chains, a concrete floor that sloped a half-inch toward a drain Mr. Harrington had been meaning to rod out for six years. Bay one was lecture. Bays two and three were alive. Bay two had a '68 Impala up on jack stands, hood yawning open, radiator pulled. Bay three had Nick's project and Carl's — a small-block Chevy 327 on an engine stand, heads off, the valve train laid out on cardboard in the order the parts had come out.

The shop smelled like Brakleen and hot metal and the gritty orange hand-soap in the gallon pump by the wash sink. A transistor radio sat on the bench by the compressor, tuned to WBCN, low enough that Harrington wouldn't make them shut it off. A woman's voice was on the air talking about a record Aerosmith had coming in the fall, and the boys in bay three didn't know that the voice would be gone from the dial in a few years and wouldn't have made anything of it if they had.

Nick had a socket wrench and a head bolt that had decided it didn't want to come. He leaned into it. His wrists were oil-dark to the cuffs, and there was a smear of grease on his cheekbone from where he had scratched a mosquito bite an hour ago without thinking about his hands first. Carl was across the engine stand with a torque spec sheet in one hand and a cup of shop coffee in the other. The coffee was bad. Everybody said the coffee was bad. Everybody drank it.

"Break or lean on it," Carl said.

"I'm leaning on it."

"Lean harder. It's a bolt, not your grandmother."

Nick leaned. The bolt went a quarter turn and then another and then it broke free and came out in his hand clean, and he set it on the cardboard in its place in the line, and Carl made a small approving sound and went back to the torque sheet.

The door from the corridor opened and Anthony Rossi came through — Ant, to everyone — a junior who was not supposed to be in the shop during third period because he had study hall in the cafeteria. He had a folded green hall pass in his fingers and a face that said he had no intention of explaining himself to Harrington because Harrington had stopped asking. He came up the aisle, hands in the pockets of a Raytheon-blue work jacket that had been his uncle's before it was his, and stopped at the engine stand to look at the heads on the bench.

"You doing the valves today?"

"Reseating," Carl said.

"Harrington letting you grind em?"

"He said he'd do it. He's not doing it. So it's gonna be me doing it and him telling me I did it wrong."

Ant laughed — short, no teeth, the laugh of a kid who had been the youngest in a loud house and had learned to fit his amusement into other people's sentences. He owned, with his brother Frankie, a 1970 Nova that lived in the driveway of a split-level on Route 20 behind the sheet-metal shop where his father was foreman. The Nova had a rebuilt 350 and rust through the back quarter panels and a bench seat his mother had reupholstered herself in brown vinyl from a roll she'd found at a yard sale in Marlborough. He knew a carburetor the way a piano kid knew a scale. He was going to work at his uncle's garage on Boston Post Road when he graduated, and his family already knew it, and Ant had been fine with it since he was twelve.

"Friday night," Ant said. "We going or we not going?"

"Going where."

"Kevin said the trails. His brother's got that Yamaha. Ride til dark and then Store 24."

"I told my mother I'd be home by nine."

"Why."

"Cause I told her I would."

"You heard about Gary."

"Gary who."

"Gary Hagan. Broke his collarbone on the trails last weekend. Went over the handlebars on the big hill behind the transformer. His mother told him he wasn't gonna play JV baseball this spring, and he cried. Sixteen-year-old kid, crying at the kitchen table over JV baseball."

"Jesus," Nick said.

"Yeah. So — Friday night. Trails. But carefully."

"Carefully," Carl said, and made the word sound like the opposite of itself.

Harrington came out from his office at the end of bay one, coveralls oil-black at the knees and pockets the way they had been for thirty years, a cigarette tucked behind his ear he wasn't going to smoke until lunch. He gave Ant a look that was not a question so much as a timer. Ant pushed off the bench, folded the hall pass a little tighter, and started down the aisle.

"Nine o'clock," Ant called back.

"Nine o'clock," Nick said, and did not look up.

When the door closed, Carl tilted his head toward the engine. "Your mother's gonna let you out at nine."

"My mother's working the four-to-close at Friendly's. I'm letting myself out at nine."

Carl grinned. The grin stayed on the torque sheet and didn't find Nick's face.

The '66 Chevelle hood leaning against the wall gave back the radiator's clank as a faint metal shiver. Primer grey. A pencil mark on the fender where the paint guy had measured for the body line. The wall clock clicked up another minute, and the shop went back to its own sound — compressor, radio low, Harrington's pencil somewhere in bay one scratching figures that meant something to men who built things for a living.

Glass Corridor. Fourth period. Lunch A.

Lenny Henderson was sixteen and had been smoking for fourteen months, still in the phase where he was proud of it and not yet in the phase where he was tired of it. He sat on the floor of the Glass Corridor with his back against the brick dado wall, legs out straight, a paperback copy of *The Stranger* open on his thigh. The paperback had no cover. It had come off on the T in February when he'd stuffed the book in his jacket pocket with a roll of dimes and sat on it for forty minutes, and now it was just pages and a bent spine, which he preferred.

The Corridor ran twenty yards between the cafeteria and the arts wing, dirty glass on the south side and brick on the north, and the two o'clock April sun came through at an angle that turned the smoke into a physical thing — a column of soft yellow light in the middle of the hallway, ash motes moving through it the way plankton moves through a shaft of undersea sun. An ashtray on a metal stand at the cafeteria end, chrome top, overfilled. The custodian emptied it Monday.

Lenny was rolling a cigarette one-handed because he could. Left hand holding the book, right hand with the paper between the first two knuckles, the Top tobacco tin balanced on his thigh, and he shook tobacco into the paper with a tilt that was almost elegant. He rolled, licked, sealed, tucked the cigarette behind his ear next to a pencil.

Two dado sections down, Maggie Doherty sat with her knees up and her head against the glass. Her leather jacket was cracked at the elbows, bought from a cousin in Dorchester last summer for twenty dollars. She was writing in a composition book with a ballpoint pen that kept skipping. Farther down, a junior named Walt was reading a paperback Dostoevsky in worse shape than Lenny's Camus. The three of them weren't talking. The point of the Corridor was you could be with other people without having to perform being with other people. The smoke did the work.

A chemistry teacher came through from the cafeteria end carrying a stack of lab reports, on her way to the arts wing. She walked past the ashtray, past Lenny, past Maggie, past Walt, without turning her head. Heels on waxed linoleum. She was gone through the far door in seven seconds. The ashes from her passing shifted in the yellow sunlight and resettled. Lenny took the cigarette from behind his ear and lit it with a Zippo that had been his grandfather's. He drew, held, let it out toward the glass. The glass fogged a square of itself and cleared.

Lenny Henderson lived on Goodman's Hill Road in a five-bedroom colonial with a flagstone walk and a tennis court behind the garage. His father was a dermatologist with an office in Newton and a second house on the Cape. The tennis court had been resurfaced in October. Lenny had not played on it since he was eleven, when he had gone one day without being told to and beaten his father in three sets, and his father had smiled and hugged him and then never asked him to play again.

Nobody in the Corridor knew any of this. Maggie had been to Lenny's house exactly once, the previous October, on a Friday when the family was in Chatham, and Lenny had taken her out the back slider to the court and they had stood on the baseline in the dark with their hands in their pockets and Maggie had said *is this yours* and Lenny had said *it's the house's* and they had gone back inside. That was as close as any Rat had come to the Henderson foyer. Lenny had done the math on what it would cost him in the Corridor if Goodman's Hill were known, and he had decided the Corridor was the first thing in his life he had chosen, and he was not going to let Goodman's Hill take it from him.

"What time is Hagan home," Maggie said without looking up.

"I heard four."

"His mom letting anyone see him."

"His mom is mad at the trails, at the Yamaha, at Kevin, at the transformer, and at the state of Massachusetts for building a dirt road behind her house."

Maggie laughed without moving. "Tell him to call me."

"He can't hold a phone, Mag. He broke his collarbone."

"He's got another arm."

Lenny smiled. He hadn't smiled in twenty minutes. "I'll tell him."

The fire alarm at the far end of the Corridor was behind a glass panel with a little red hammer attached to a chain. Someone had set it off in December and nobody had ever proved it. The kids in the Corridor knew who it had been. The kids in the Corridor were not going to say. Lenny let smoke out toward the glass square he had fogged before, and the smoke moved into the sunbeam, and Camus lay open on his thigh at a page he had stopped reading because the sentence had been good enough that he wanted to hold it for a minute.

Raised platform. Same period.

The Rampers' tables sat eight inches above the main floor on a section of poured concrete some architect in 1960 had thought would be used for public speaking. Six long tables. Folding chairs. A sight line of the whole cafeteria that nobody on the platform used except to check whether the hot lunch line was long.

Jackie sat at the second table from the wall with his brown bag open in front of him. Inside — his mother had packed it at six-fifteen — was a chicken salad sandwich on wheat with the crusts cut off, a peeled orange in a baggie segment by segment, two Pepperidge Farm Milanos in a napkin folded into a tight square. His mother was like that. His mother would be like that forever.

Kathy Bellenghi was three chairs down showing her day planner to a sophomore named Brooke, saying *the Junior Prom is literally six weeks away*, the third time in the lunch period she had used the word *literally* incorrectly and the fourth time today, and Jackie had started to count out of fondness more than irritation. Her highlighters were lined up on the formica in the order of the colors of the rainbow. She was filling a square on the sixth of May with pink. The prom.

Across from Jackie, Donnie Rossi was eating a meatball sub. Three sports this year — soccer, basketball, baseball — and the knuckle-scrape on his right hand from a headfirst slide on Feeley's mud Wednesday. The sub came from his uncle's deli off Concord Road, wrapped in white butcher paper saturated with sauce and sitting on an open section of the *Boston Globe* sports page so the sauce wouldn't stain the formica. Donnie did everything fast. His dress shirt had been ironed on Sunday night by a mother who woke at five-thirty six days a week to work the breakfast shift at the Friendly's on Route 20, and by Friday the shirt was a different object from the one that had come off the board — cuff-rolled, collar limp, a stripe of sauce at the second button.

"So, Doctor," Donnie said. He had called Jackie Doctor since the first week of September, and Jackie had stopped trying to make him stop. "What's the answer."

"The answer to what."

"Am I gonna ace the calc quiz sixth period."

"Did you study."

"I studied enough."

"Then you're gonna do fine."

"That's a non-answer. Give me an expert opinion."

Jackie set down his sandwich. "Expert opinion. You're gonna get a B-minus. You're gonna tell your mother you got a B-plus. She's gonna know you're lying but not make you take it back because she wants you to feel good about it."

"See," Donnie said to Kathy, who wasn't listening. "This is why I talk to the Doctor. He knows my mother."

"Everybody knows your mother, Donnie," Kathy said without looking up. "Your mother works the counter at Friendly's. I had pancakes from your mother's hand on Sunday morning."

"And were they good pancakes."

"They were fantastic pancakes."

"That's all I need to hear."

He went back to the sub. Jackie watched him a beat longer than he meant to. Donnie's right cuff had sauce on it now too. His mother would wash it tonight after the four-to-close, and it would be on the board by Monday morning, and Donnie would put it on with his eyes half-closed the way any kid put on a Monday shirt.

David Bellenghi was at Jackie's right, reading aloud from the Junior Prom ticket that had been mimeographed and handed out in homeroom, reading the part that said *semi-formal*, and laughing.

"Semi-formal means what, exactly."

"Means you can wear your father's tie," Donnie said.

"I don't own a tie."

"You can wear my father's tie. He's got three. One of them's paisley. You'll look like a lounge act but you'll be in regulation."

David laughed. Kathy did too, absently. Jackie did a small thing with his mouth that was the piano kid's version of a laugh.

Below the platform, at the milk coolers, a girl in mud-brown jeans and a red-checked flannel three sizes too big — a brother's flannel, cuffs rolled to the wrists, hem at the thighs — was lifting a half-pint of whole milk out of the case. A flute case was tucked under her elbow. Her hair was the color of wheat and uncombed at the back, and she was not looking at anyone. Patty Lally paid the cafeteria lady with a folded dollar and turned and walked toward the side door that went to the music practice rooms, and was gone before she could have been expected to register on anyone. Jackie's eye tracked her for two seconds, and then he was back to Donnie, and Donnie had said something that had made Kathy laugh, and the platform went on being the platform.

The bell would ring in four minutes. Kathy capped her pink marker. Donnie balled up his butcher paper with the sauce-stained *Globe* sports page inside it and carried the bundle to the trash can in a single walking motion, and on his way back he flicked the back of Jackie's neck with a forefinger as a hello and as a goodbye, and Jackie did not move.

---

After school the parking lot was mud and exhaust again, tire ruts filling with meltwater, kids moving toward buses and cars and the trailheads behind the shop wing where the dirt bikes would fire up by four-thirty. Nick came out the east door with grease still on his cuffs and Carl beside him talking about the valve job. Jackie came out the west doors with David Bellenghi and a group from the platform, loafers careful on the gravel. They crossed the same lot without crossing paths, the way they had crossed the same doors that morning, and the school went on humming behind them — fluorescent ballast, locker slams, a gym class whistle — the ordinary noise of a town that had sorted its children into rooms and expected them to stay there until June.
