CHAPTER 4: “MORE THAN A FEELING”

SUDBURY.  March, 1979

The bike had belonged to somebody else first, and Patty Lally had never once been ashamed of that.

It came up out of the reclaim corner at the town landfill the winter she was fourteen—the corner past the glass piles and the flattened cardboard, where people left what was still good because pitching it down the trench felt like a sin even to people who'd stopped going to church. A Schwinn, one speed, the blue gone chalky, the chrome freckled orange. A family up on the Northwest side had set it there when they moved their daughter up to a ten-speed with hand brakes and toe clips, the kind of bike that cost what the Lallys spent on heating oil between Thanksgiving and March.

Her father brought it home balanced across his own handlebars. He spent the back end of that winter on it in the shed with a trouble light hung from a nail—new tubes, new tires, the frame sanded and brushed and painted the blue of a robin's egg, the bars soaked in a coffee can of vinegar until the chrome came back bright enough to hold her face. He wired a basket to the front. When he rolled it out to her he didn't say much; her father never said much. "Somebody quit on it too early," was all, and he went in to wash the paint off his hands.

That was about the whole of what Patty believed, handed to her on two wheels: that a thing other people had quit on was not the same as a thing without worth. You only had to be the kind of person who'd stop at the reclaim corner instead of driving past it.

She rode it everywhere. That was the other thing about her—she went. Her mother said she'd been a wanderer since she could walk, and said it the way you'd describe weather, not a flaw. Both her parents had been the same once, kids who'd known these woods before the conservation signs went up, and what they handed down instead of money was a map nobody else carried: every cut-through and deer path and mini-bike rut between the four corners of town, the trails that showed on no plat in the town hall. She knew which stone walls had gaps you could walk a bike through and which you had to lift it over. She knew the town the way you know a face you've looked at all your life.

The Lallys lived on the southeast side, close enough to the Cavicchio farm that when the wind swung south the smell of it got into the wash on the line and the pages of her schoolbooks, and the bus kids learned young to say it to her like an accusation. Pig. As if she kept them herself. She didn't. Her father fixed things—furnaces, mowers, the occasional outboard—for people on every side of Hop Brook, which meant Patty had stood in the kitchens of all four quarters and watched how a Northwest mother's smile worked when a repairman's daughter reached for the wrong fork. She'd stopped minding the smell a long time ago. It was the price of the air on her side of the brook, and she'd decided early she would not apologize for the air.

Monday was the first warm morning of the year, so she took the long way to the high school, because the long way was the better way.

She cut down behind the farm and picked up the trail along Hop Brook. The brook was up with the snowmelt, running fast and brown over the granite ledges, and she stopped on the plank somebody's father had laid across it years ago and listened, the way she always did. In spring you could hear the whole register of it—a bright chatter in the riffles, a lower note in the bends where the current dug under the roots, and beneath all of it the steady hiss where it narrowed between the boulders the glacier had dropped like loose change. By July it would barely whisper; you'd have to crouch at the bank to hear it at all. In March it had everything to say.

The trail ran beside the old walls most of the way—fieldstone hauled by hand two hundred years ago, laid without mortar by farmers who'd come back from the Old North Bridge to find the fields still needed clearing. Half-buried now under oak leaves and moss, frost-heaved a little wider every winter. She'd laid her palm flat on one once in August and felt the cold still down in it, a cold that went all the way back to the ice. The walls had divided the town before the roads did.

Where the trail crossed the landfill road she slowed, the way she always slowed there, out of a kind of loyalty. It was where the bike was born. Saturdays the whole town lined up at the gate—station wagons from the Northwest waxed and quiet, pickups from the Northeast rusted at the wheel wells, the Cavicchio flatbed trailing its pennant of smell—and every one of them backed up to the same trench and pitched in. Glass in one pile, paper in another, the rest into the dark. At the Dump nobody's garbage was better than anyone else's. As far as Patty could tell, it was the only place in Sudbury where that was true.

From the rise past the landfill she could see the rest of it laid out the way she carried it in her head. Northeast, where the houses smelled like coffee by six and motor oil by seven, where a used guitar ran forty dollars at the pawn shop and a working family could afford to lose that bet—there was a boy over there who played in his dead father's garage; the whole town knew that now. Southwest, the professors' streets, the colonials with names instead of numbers, where another boy had been given lessons the way other kids were given vegetables. Two boys she'd watched circle each other since the eighth grade. Northwest, the old money, the dogs trained out of barking. And her own corner at her back, smelling of what it smelled of.

The high school sat where the southeast quarter ran out, brick and glass dropped between Route 117 and the brook's last loop, and it had four doors, one on each face—which Patty had always thought was the truest thing about it. It was the one building the whole town had to come to. Four doors, and the town poured itself through all of them at the same hour every weekday, and then did the strangest thing she knew of: it sorted itself all over again, by something other than the brook.

She coasted in past the loading ramp where the ramp kids sat in a row with their feet hanging, watching everyone arrive like judges who'd already ruled. Past the auto-shop bay, up already, where a Camaro hung on the lift and a boy she'd known since second grade lay under it grease to the elbows, happier than she ever saw him in a classroom. Through the breezeway off the glass corridor where the rats kept to their leather and their own company, cigarettes that weren't allowed and always there, somebody laughing too loud for ten of eight in the morning. And everywhere in between, the regulars—the great middle, books crossed over their chests, heads down, working hard at being nobody in particular, which was its own full-time job.

Patty chained the blue bike to the rack by the gym door—her door, the one nobody had assigned her; she'd just decided on it freshman year—and stood a second in the cold sun, watching the town she knew by heart pretend it was four different towns. In here, for thirty-five hours a week, they got to choose. Out on the brook the geography would be waiting for all of them at three o'clock.

Marie was at the door with two coffees from the machine, the way she always was, and handed one over without being asked.

"You took the long way," Marie said.

"I always take the long way."

"I know." Marie held the door. "That's why I bring two."

They went in.
