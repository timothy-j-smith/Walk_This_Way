CHAPTER 13: "BREAKDOWN"

Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School. September 1975.

The cafeteria hit you before you saw it.

Fryer grease and industrial tomato sauce and the sweet chemical fog of whatever they sprayed on the tables between periods — a smell that got into your clothes and stayed there, so your mother would sniff your jacket that evening and say you smelled like school, which was another way of saying you smelled like everyone and no one. Three hundred kids talking at once made a sound with no words in it, just a continuous roar, punctuated by lunch trays hitting formica and the occasional shriek of a chair leg dragged across linoleum. The fluorescent lights buzzed at a frequency that lived behind your teeth.

Nick and Jackie walked in together that first Monday, brown bags in hand because the hot lunch line was forty minutes long and neither of them knew the system yet. They stood just inside the double doors and looked at the room the way you look at water when you can't see the bottom.

The room had its own geography.

Six tables sat on a raised platform at the back — eight inches higher than everything else, which was all it took. Textbooks stacked. Spiral notebooks open. Highlighters uncapped and lined up by color. One girl had a day planner. It was September. She was already marking dates in November.

The glass corridor between the cafeteria and the arts wing caught the afternoon light through dirty windows and turned everything the color of an old photograph. Someone was always rolling a cigarette. Someone was always sketching in a composition notebook. A boy with safety pins through his jacket collar was reading a paperback with no cover.

The middle was the big undifferentiated mass — loud, safe, forgettable. The center of the room and the center of the bell curve.

The two tables nearest the auto-shop corridor smelled like motor oil and Brakleen and the metallic sweetness of fresh-cut steel. Hands stained in the creases the way Nick's father's hands were stained — the kind of clean soap could reach and the kind it couldn't. They ate fast. Lunch was what happened between engines.

Nick and Jackie didn't know the names yet. They just knew the room was enormous and loud and that they needed to sit down somewhere before the standing itself became a statement.

"There," Jackie said, chin toward a half-empty table in the middle section.

They sat.

For two weeks that was the arrangement — Regs table by the water fountain, brown bags, talking about the band they were going to start, about Patty's voice at camp, about whether the community center on Route 20 would rent them a practice room after school. The cafeteria roared around them and they were an island in it, the way the garage had been an island, the way the practice meadow at Elbanobscot had been an island.

It lasted fourteen days.

---

Monday of the third week.

Nick came through the double doors and Jackie wasn't at their table — not at the water fountain, not in the middle section where they'd sat for fourteen days with their brown bags and their plans for a band. Nick scanned the glass corridor, then the raised platform at the back, and found him.

Jackie was up there, second table from the wall, next to a kid in a polo shirt who was showing him something in a textbook. Jackie's notebook was open. He was laughing.

Forty feet. Nick could have crossed it in ten seconds. He stood with his brown bag and looked at the distance the way you look at a door you know you aren't going to walk through.

He walked to the shop-corridor tables instead, where Carl Marquis was eating a sandwich with black grease worked into the knuckles. Carl looked up. Nick sat down. Carl went back to his sandwich.

"You got shop second period?" Carl said after a while.

"Third."

"Harrington?"

"Yeah."

Carl nodded. The conversation was over and it was enough.

Nick ate his lunch. The sandwich tasted like nothing.

Across the room, on the platform, Jackie was still talking to the kid in the polo shirt — David Bellenghi, though Nick didn't know the name yet. Jackie's back was angled slightly away from the main floor. Not fully turned. Just angled. The way you angle away from a window when the light's too bright.

Jackie never looked for him — never scanned the room, never checked the table by the water fountain — or maybe he had and chosen not to, and Nick couldn't tell which was worse, careless or careful, and both left him sitting at a table that smelled like motor oil, eating a sandwich he couldn't taste, forty feet from the kid he'd walked in with on the first Monday.

Near the milk coolers, at the end of a middle table, Patty Lally sat alone with an apple and a canvas bag on the bench beside her, wheat-colored hair falling past her shoulders, bare feet tucked under the bench even though the linoleum was cold. She smelled like something faintly animal and earthy that the other kids couldn't quite place — southeast Sudbury, the wind off the Cavicchio fields, a smell that lived in jackets and didn't wash out all the way. From where she sat she could see Nick at the shop tables with his grease-stained company and Jackie on the platform with his polo-shirt friend and the forty feet of formica and noise between them, and she bit her apple and watched without waving.

---

That night Jackie showed up at the garage on Dutton Road at six-thirty with his guitar, as if nothing had changed.

He tuned up, ran through a chord progression, and waited for Nick to come in on top of it the way Nick always did — and Nick did, his line finding Jackie's architecture the same fit it had been since the talent show, since camp, since the conservation trails when they didn't have names for what they were building.

They ran through three songs. The garage filled with sound and the sound was right.

In the gaps between songs, in the silences where they used to talk, something was different. The silence had weight now — the weight of two people not mentioning lunch.

"Good session," Jackie said when they finished. He said it the way you close a door.

"Yeah," Nick said. "Good session."

Jackie packed his guitar and rode his bike home through the September dark, up Dutton Road toward the north side where the lawns got wider and the porch lights came on by themselves at dusk. Nick stood in the garage doorway and watched the red reflector on Jackie's rear fender shrink to a point of light and then nothing before he went inside.

Grace was at the kitchen table paying bills with the calculator that printed on the little paper tape — click, whirr, click — the sound filling the room like a mechanical heartbeat counting down something. Dan's work boots stood by the door, leather cracked, dried plaster in the treads. Nick passed through without saying anything and climbed the stairs to his room.

On his dresser, folded under a stack of homework, was half of a camp schedule with a phone number written on the back in Patty's block print. The other half was somewhere Jackie kept. Nick had known that since the last morning at Elbanobscot. He hadn't dialed it. He hadn't asked Jackie if he had.

He picked up his guitar and played nothing — just held it, the body warm against his ribs, and listened to the house.

Outside, the leaves on Dutton Road were just starting to go pale at the edges.
