ACT I: TOYS IN THE ATTIC

---

CHAPTER 1: "WISH YOU WERE HERE"

BOSTON.  August 11, 2009

Four hours. That's what I've got left.

Four hours before WBCN becomes another frequency in the graveyard. Four hours before the suits flip the switch and turn us into automated Christian contemporary—because apparently Jesus rocks harder than Freddie Mercury ever did. Four hours before forty-one years of breaking bands, of late-night conversations with musicians who poured their souls into three-minute songs, of watching Boston transform from steel and sweat into whatever the hell it's become—four hours before all of that gets erased like a bad demo tape.

I'm Maxanne Sartori. Fifty-nine years old, sitting in Studio B with a paper cup of Heaven Hill at my elbow and the ON AIR sign blinking red like a dying heartbeat. The station called and brought me back. This one last time. Same studio where I watched Steven Tyler hear his own voice on the radio for the first time in '73—the look on his face when that raw sound went out to thousands and he understood it was real. Same turntables. Same microphone that carried me through the seventies.

"Maxanne?" The program director's voice crackles through the intercom. The Kid's twenty-eight, wears sneakers that cost more than my first Back Bay studio, and thinks rock history started with Nirvana. "You've got four hours left. Make them count."

Make them count.

As if four hours could hold what this frequency meant to a city that lived on rock and roll. As if the story I'm about to tell could be wrapped and ribboned. It doesn't work that way.

But endings make liars of us all. You promise dignity. Then you find yourself in a booth with a cup warming your palm and a note Steven Tyler sent when he heard we were closing. *For the first lady who ever gave a damn.* Even after all these years, he still remembers. I take a sip.

I adjust the headphones—the same Sony MDR-7506s I've worn since 1971, held together now with electrical tape and pure stubbornness. The console lights pulse. Outside the booth windows, Boston glitters in the August dark—glass towers where dive bars used to breed bands, where the Rat used to host shows that would make your grandmother blush.

"This is Maxanne Sartori, and for the next four hours, you're listening to the final broadcast of WBCN, 104.1 on your FM dial. The Rock of Boston. The station that launched Aerosmith when they were just kids from New Hampshire with more attitude than money. The station that gave J. Geils his first shot, that broke The Cars when they were still a garage band, that made Boston believe it could rock as hard as anywhere else."

"But tonight isn't about the bands that made it. Tonight is about two kids from Sudbury I could never get out of my head—and the only tape that proves they ever breathed into the same microphone."

The silence goes out over the city. In the engineering booth, the kid they've assigned to babysit my final show looks up from his phone. Something in my voice warns him this won't be the nostalgia trip he expected.

Spring, 1979. Disco was gasping. The Clash were about to teach America that punk had something to say. MTV was three years away from making image matter more than sound.

I spin my chair to face the wall of albums. The vinyl spines catch the booth's amber light, each one a ghost. My fingers find the right cart without looking—muscle memory, hands knowing these grooves better than my own face in the mirror.

"Nick Johnson lived in a house where the mortgage was late. His father had died in a construction accident seventeen months earlier, crushed beneath a load of steel beams when a crane cable snapped—page three of the Metro section, forgotten by everyone except the family that ate the silence afterward. His mother worked doubles at Friendly's, came home smelling like fryer oil and other people's happiness, and never once complained about her son spending grocery money on guitar strings."

Through the booth window, the billboard across Boylston Street advertises luxury condos where The Rat used to host bands too raw for radio. Twenty-four hundred square feet starting at 2.2 million—you could buy the entire block of Kenmore Square for that in '77. The Ramones played that club when Joey had to duck under the pipes backstage, and the sound system was one blown fuse away from silence.

"Jackie Marsh lived less than a mile away as the crow flies, but between them stretched Hop Brook—named a century ago for its hops, the old mill long gone. The brook divided Sudbury like a scar. In winter, kids from both sides skated the same pond trying to emulate Bobby Orr. When the ice melted, they returned to their proper territories. Jackie's father was a psychiatrist and a professor at Boston University. He published papers about childhood trauma."

I pause, letting the silence breathe.

"I met them both in the spring of 1979, about two months before the Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School Battle of the Bands. I was there as a judge, WBCN sponsoring the event, back when we could afford to care about local music. We told ourselves we were there to scout talent. That was half the truth."

I reach for the cassette on my desk. The plastic case is warm from my grip, the label slightly faded: *Demo - Nick/Jackie - un-Authorized - May 1979* in block letters. My letters. Plastic and magnetic oxide and the faint trace of cigarette smoke from a room I haven't stood in since. The only proof they ever played together.

The rest of the story? That's what the next four hours are for.

The ON AIR sign pulses. Four hours left to tell the truth.

I crack my knuckles and lean into the microphone one more time.

"But first, we need to talk about a garage on Dutton Road—and a pegboard with an empty slot where the nine-sixteenths used to live."

I close my eyes. March 1979. Mud season—the snow gone gray at the edges of the yards, the brook running high and brown with melt, the maples still bare, the sky the color of old steel. That's when I first drove out to Sudbury, following rumors about a kid who played guitar like he was trying to resurrect the dead.
