CHAPTER 32: "BARRACUDA"


---

Seven o'clock. May 16, 1979.

The auditorium had filled while the sky outside went green at the edges — four hundred and twenty-seven bodies in four hundred and forty-three seats, the empty chairs in the back like teeth missing from a grin. Popcorn butter and Aqua Velva and girls' perfume layered over floor wax and adolescent heat. The balcony groaned when the seniors stood to wave at other seniors. Somewhere in the lobby the booster club's cash box clicked.

Chuck Morrison tapped the microphone once. The tap came back through the house PA like a door closing.

"Good evening, Lincoln-Sudbury." A good-natured roar. "First ever WBCN Battle of the Bands, brought to you by the Rock of Boston and by Strawberries Records and Tapes, where for seven ninety-nine you can take home an album that will change your life or your sister's." He let the laugh build and cut it off. "Eight bands. Five hundred dollars. Recording time on Boylston Street. Let's go."

---

The judges' table sat stage-left, draped in black cloth, four folding chairs behind it.

Maxanne Sartori had been in the building since noon — leather jacket over a plain shirt, jeans, boots, styrofoam cup of WBCN coffee with the red logo still on it. Clipboard on her knees. Pencil sharpened to a point that would snap before the night was over. On page three of the packet, Article 7, Section 3: *In the event of a tie in the final round, tied contestants shall share the studio recording session equally.* Sponsor boilerplate. Boring line. She had added it at her kitchen table in January before she knew any names from Sudbury.

Beside her Carter Alan took notes like a man filing taxes. Bob Walker from Strawberries had forearms laid across a second clipboard as if selecting the draft. Neither of them had read page three. Maxanne had. That was the difference between guilt and paperwork.

Chuck introduced her and the room gave the warmest hand of the night — half of Sudbury had heard her on 104.1 before bed. Carter got applause. Bob Walker got the loudest — studio time, album contract, the kind of future a kid could carry out of a gymnasium in a cardboard box.

---

Grace Johnson sat third row left in the navy dress with the buttons, winter coat folded over one arm, programs folded once in her lap, purse on top, hands on top of the purse. Friendly's had let her off at four. Tom Andrews's WBCN button caught the cove light when she shifted.

Three rows back on the aisle, Dr. Richard Marsh sat gray suit, pressed shirt, no tie loosened, palms flat on his knees, thumbs in line with the seam of his trousers. The way he sat at department meetings.

Along the rake the waiting arranged itself without a map. Leather and denim colonized the back rows, boots on armrests. Grease under the fingernails leaned against the side wall near the shop-corridor door because folding seats put the ache in the wrong part of the back. Study-hall kids held the first two rows in neat sections, pens out as if there would be a quiz. Nobody had planned it. Bodies just went where they recognized themselves.

---

Backstage, Nick Johnson stood at the stage-right gap in the velvet curtain where the hem didn't reach the floor.

Through the slit he watched Chuck hold up the lineup card and read the first slot — no band name, just *Lincoln-Sudbury freshmen, take it away,* the way you throw chum to see what rises.

Four kids walked on with borrowed gear and faces too young to be embarrassed yet. The bass player — tall, wire-rim glasses, Route 217 haircut — missed the turnaround twice on the opening number and kept going anyway, sweet and wrong, the way you keep going when the room hasn't decided if you're food yet. Nick had seen him once at a register on Route 217 buying strings he couldn't afford. *You're in the Battle, right?* The kid had nodded like a war was a weather report.

The room clapped because it was a start.

Nick did not look at Jackie at the far wing. Jackie did not look at Nick. Patty Lally stood behind the Steinway case with her Martin on the strap and counted the claps the way you count seconds between lightning and thunder.

---

Detroit Rock City came from Acton-Boxborough in greasepaint and platform boots, a blood packet gone off early in the first verse, a fire-breather reduced to steam by the custodian's nervous glance at the sprinkler heads. The first five rows loved them for trying. The balcony loved them for the mess. Chuck flicked the house lights twice when the smoke got thick and they landed it into applause that was kind and a little exhausted.

The Dead Heads — Wayland, patchouli, Birkenstocks — opened warm and rolled toward a long jam the back half of the balcony lost around minute seven. Kids drifted to the lobby for popcorn, to the smoking corner outside the fire doors, to the bathroom in groups of three because nobody went alone. The house mood wasn't electric yet. It was **waiting** — circling, patient, the way something with teeth holds still in clear water before it moves.

Talking Heads Talking from Boston Latin wore matching thrift-store suits and played "Psycho Killer" with clinical tightness. A whole row of older brothers in the back threw their hands up, delighted — Rats in training, recognizing precision when it wasn't theirs. The southwest section murmured assent. The northeast section answered with volume, not applause — a rumble in the chest, boots on floor, the first real split in the room's single body.

Maxanne wrote a number in a little box. Originality weighted double. She wrote another number. Stage presence. The pencil moved faster than she wanted it to.

---

By the end of the fourth band Chuck Morrison had sweat on his upper lip and the WBCN banner behind him had started to peel at one corner where the duct tape gave up.

He read the next name from the card and grinned at the room.

"When we come back — Sudbury. The part you've been waiting for."

House lights to half. The balcony stood first. Popcorn line in the lobby. Outside the fire doors, cigarettes flared in the green light before the storm.

---

Backstage the locals became three separate weather systems.

Nick Johnson stood at the stage-right gap with Stephen Murphy's hand on his shoulder — not comfort, anchor. Mark checked the Rhodes mute. David tightened the high hat for the fourth time since seven o'clock.

"Crown Jewels first," Stephen said. "We're after them."

Nick nodded. He did not look across the stage to where Jackie Marsh had his palm on the Yamaha fallboard without making a sound. Jackie did not look at Nick. Forty feet of wing and velvet and gaffer's tape between them — linoleum's indoor cousin.

Patty Lally tuned the B string once because the B string was always the one. Robin leaned against the amp case. Steve Bankuti counted change in his pocket like a nervous metronome. Rumours was two slots out. That was far enough to breathe and close enough to choke on.

---

Out in the house Grace Johnson's hand stayed closed on the folded programs. Richard Marsh did not move. The thunder that had been holding all day finally broke somewhere west of Worcester — not here, not yet, just a long roll through the auditorium's concrete rake, pendant lights swaying a millimeter, a metronome nobody had asked for.

Maxanne Sartori flipped to page three of the packet. Article 7, Section 3. Boring line. She closed the clipboard.

The opening acts had been the small fish — freshmen, greasepaint, patchouli, thrift-store suits — and the room had clapped the way you clap a warm-up band, polite, already leaning toward what came next. Strawberries logo on the judges' table. WBCN red on the banner. Five hundred dollars on Chuck's tongue. The water had gone from clear to stocked. The teeth were waiting in the wings.

Barracuda don't stay for the opening act.

When the house lights came back up, Chuck Morrison would call a name from Sudbury. The painted maple backdrop would still hang behind the empty mic stands — red gone brown at the edges, October forever — and four hundred and twenty-seven people would decide which side of Hop Brook they were on before a single local note got played.

Nick breathed in. Jackie breathed out. Patty's pick rested on the string without moving.

The room leaned forward.
