CHAPTER 9: "JUST WHAT I NEEDED"

Three hours and forty-seven minutes left.

I'm sitting in Studio B, the clock on the wall ticking past two a.m., and behind the glass my engineer — a college kid in a Bruins hoodie who thinks music starts with a laptop — has queued up The Cars' "Just What I Needed." Ric Ocasek's guitar figure bleeds through the monitors at low volume, clean and hungry, the kind of riff that sounds like wanting before you know what you're wanting. I realize I've been feeding you the end of the story without ever serving up the start. The Battle of the Bands in 1979 — that's the big finale, sure — but tonight we need to rewind the tape. Not the first garage jam. Not even the friendship. The instant two best friends looked at a middle school talent show and thought, hell, why not give it a shot?

I take a sip of coffee — gone cold hours ago, but it's fuel either way — and I think about May 1975. Not the night. The decision. The Tuesday in April when Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh stopped being two kids who played music in a garage and became a problem for every parent who thought the world came with a seating chart.

Outside the booth, August still burning on the glass, but I'm seeing Sudbury in spring — cut grass, warm asphalt, thirteen-year-olds who still believe the next four minutes might change something.

I need you to understand something about middle school talent shows in suburban Massachusetts in 1975. They weren't about discovering new voices. They were about maintaining the social order — proving that the kids who were supposed to win would win. I spent seven years at WBCN looking for kids who could win a Rumble at The Rat. The talent show circuit was the opposite. The Rat rewarded danger. Talent shows rewarded safety.

I reach for the cassette on my desk, the one labeled "Demo - Nick/Jackie - un-Authorized - May 1979." But tonight I'm not playing that tape. Tonight I'm telling you about the day they decided to make it.

Nick told me the story years later, in Studio C, while we waited for an engineer who was late and the coffee was bad and neither of us was in a mood to be polite about it. Jackie had been sitting on Dan Johnson's workbench, feet dangling, watching Nick fight with a Zeppelin riff on his acoustic. Middle school talent show — May 15th — Mrs. Reed had mentioned it in music class.

"We should enter," Jackie said.

Nick looked up. "Why would we do that?"

"Because we're good," Jackie said. Not bravado. Recognition. "Because we're really good, and nobody knows it except us."

That was the hinge. Not when they became friends. Not the first mashup in Dan's garage. When Jackie said those words and Nick realized he was right.

They spent three weeks on it — garage magic translated for a gymnasium full of fluorescent lights and parents who thought Freddie Mercury was a Roman god. Queen meets Aerosmith. Risky as hell. Middle school talent shows aren't built for that kind of risk.

I glance at the console — the old Neve 8068 with its rows of faders and the coffee ring I've been meaning to wipe off since 1986 — and let the moment settle.

An ambulance wails down Tremont. Behind the glass, my engineer looks up from the mixing board, peering at me the way you watch a pot that might boil over. I lean into the mic and keep going.

I'm not going to tell you how May 15th ended. You don't get the final score from the booth. You get the fluorescent lights first, the lemon floor wax, the photocopied programs still tacky from the Xerox machine, the rabbit that escaped into the bleachers during the magic act. You get two kids walking out with two acoustic guitars and no safety net.

But I'm ahead of myself again, and that's the habit I'm trying to break tonight — narrating the recital from the third row instead of letting you sit in the gymnasium.

May 15, 1975. Ephraim Curtis Middle School. Seven-thirty p.m.

The folding chairs are waiting.
