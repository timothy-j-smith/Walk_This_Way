## CHAPTER 8: NICK'S WORLD

The Johnson house on Dutton Road sat like a tired dog in the March mud. Inside, the kitchen held the kind of warmth that comes from a family still whole, from a father who came home dirty and tired but always ready to listen, from the particular kind of love that happens when people are still figuring out how to be themselves without breaking apart.

Grace Johnson moved through her morning routine with the efficiency of someone who'd learned that time was money and money was survival. The coffee pot percolated its familiar rhythm while she checked the bills spread across the Formica table like a deck of cards that needed to be played carefully. The mortgage payment, the electric bill, the grocery list that always seemed to grow longer—each one a reminder that dreams were luxuries that came after rent and food and all the other mathematics of keeping a family together.

Nick Johnson sat at the kitchen table, thirteen years old but already carrying himself with the restless energy of someone who'd learned that music could make you feel bigger than your circumstances, that if you played loud enough and hard enough, you could escape anything. He was practicing scales under his breath, his fingers moving across an invisible fretboard on the table, the notes so quiet they were almost prayers.

"Morning, Mom," he said, looking up from his invisible guitar work.

Grace turned from the stove where she was scrambling eggs that would have to stretch for three people. "Morning, baby. You sleep okay?"

"Like a rock," Nick said, grinning. He'd been up half the night, listening to Aerosmith's "Rocks" album on the headphones his father had given him for his thirteenth birthday, the ones that still smelled like sawdust and motor oil and the particular kind of love that came from a father who understood that music mattered.

Grace sat down across from him, and for a moment, they were just mother and son sharing breakfast in a house that held the comfortable chaos of a family still figuring things out. But then she looked at the bills, and he looked at the guitar case leaning against the wall, and the weight of their circumstances settled between them like a conversation they'd been having for months.

"Where's Dad?" Nick asked, glancing toward the empty chair at the head of the table.

"Already left for the job site," Grace said, pouring coffee into a mug that said "World's Best Dad" in chipped letters. "He's working on that new building downtown. Said he'd be home for dinner if the weather holds."

"Nick," Grace said quietly, "we need to talk about school."

Nick's fingers stopped their invisible playing. "What about it?"

"About how much time your spending with school and not losing your focus."

Nick looked at his mother, and for a moment, he saw the woman who'd once had dreams of her own, who'd once believed that hard work and determination could conquer anything. But then he saw the worry in her eyes, the kind of worry that came from loving someone who dreamed bigger than their circumstances could afford.

"Mom, I'm not failing anything. I'm just... focused on other things."

"Like what?"

"Like music. Like getting good enough to maybe make something of myself."

Grace studied her son's face, and for a moment, she saw Dan Johnson's stubborn determination, the same look he'd had when he'd decided to take the construction job that would eventually provide for their family. "Nick, your father works hard every day so you can have opportunities he never had."

"Dad understands about music. He gets it."

"He gets that music is important, honey. But he also gets that you need to finish school, that you need to have options, that dreams are wonderful but they don't pay the bills."

Nick stood up, and for a moment, he looked like someone who was tired of having the same conversation over and over again. "Mom, I'm not saying I'm going to drop out of school. I'm just saying that maybe music is more important than geometry."

Grace watched him walk toward the door, and for a moment, she wanted to call him back, to tell him that she understood, that she believed in him, that she was proud of his talent. But the words caught in her throat, replaced by the fear that came from loving someone who dreamed bigger than their circumstances could afford.

Later that afternoon, Nick sat in the garage that was his father's workshop, surrounded by tools that told stories of honest work and the particular kind of pride that comes from building things with your hands. The space was small—maybe twelve by fifteen feet—but it held everything that mattered: Dan Johnson's workbench, his pegboard with tools arranged by size, the socket set that had been passed down from his own father, and Nick's guitar, a twenty-five-dollar pawn shop miracle held together with electrical tape and determination.

The garage was Nick's sanctuary, the one place where he could be himself without apology. Here, he didn't have to explain why Aerosmith meant more to him than classical music, why Steven Tyler's raw emotion spoke to him in ways that Freddie Mercury's theatrical precision never could. Here, he could practice for hours without worrying about disturbing the neighbors or explaining to his mother why music mattered more than a steady paycheck.

As Nick played, he could hear the sound of his father's truck pulling into the driveway, the familiar rumble of a vehicle that had seen better days but still carried its load with dignity. The garage door opened, and Dan Johnson stepped inside, still wearing his construction clothes—jeans that had been washed so many times they'd gone soft as flannel, a flannel shirt with the sleeves rolled up to reveal forearms that looked like they'd been carved from oak, and work boots that had walked through more concrete dust than most people saw in a lifetime.

"Hey, sport," Dan said, his voice carrying the particular kind of exhaustion that comes from eight hours of lifting steel beams and operating heavy machinery. "How's the practicing going?"

Nick looked up from his guitar, and for a moment, he saw his father as he really was—not just a construction worker, but a man who'd built his life with his hands, who'd taught his son that hard work was its own reward, who'd never once complained about the calluses on his fingers or the ache in his back at the end of a long day.

"Pretty good, Dad," Nick said, setting down his guitar. "I've been working on that new Aerosmith song. The one you said you liked."

Dan smiled, and for a moment, he looked like someone who understood that music could be more than just entertainment—it could be a way of life, a way of expressing everything that couldn't be put into words. "That's the one about dreams, right? The one that makes you feel like you can do anything?"

"Yeah, that's the one." Nick picked up his guitar again and began to play, his fingers finding the opening riff to "Dream On" with the kind of muscle memory that came from playing something a thousand times until it became part of your DNA.

Dan sat down on the workbench, and for a moment, they were just father and son sharing something that mattered to both of them. The sound filled the garage, bouncing off the concrete walls and wooden rafters, creating the kind of acoustics that made every note sound like a prayer for escape.

As Nick played, he could see his father nodding along with the rhythm, could see the way Dan's callused hands moved in time with the music, could see the particular kind of pride that comes from watching your child do something they love.

When Nick finished, Dan was quiet for a moment, and then he said, "That was beautiful, son. You're getting better every day."

"Thanks, Dad." Nick set down his guitar and looked at his father with the kind of intensity that comes from someone who's finally found the courage to ask for something they really want. "Dad, can I ask you something?"

"Sure, What's on your mind?"

"Mom says I'm spending too much time with the guitar, that I should focus more on school. But I feel like this is what I'm supposed to be doing. Like this is who I am."

Dan studied his son's face, and for a moment, he saw himself at thirteen, saw the same stubborn determination, the same refusal to settle for whatever the world thought he should be. "Nick, your mother's right that school is important. But she's also right that you have to be true to yourself."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean that I work construction because that's what I'm good at, what I know how to do. But that doesn't mean you have to do the same thing. If music is what you love, if it's what makes you feel alive, then you should follow that. Just make sure you're good at it."

Nick looked at his father, and for a moment, he saw the man who'd taught him that hard work was its own reward, who'd never once complained about the daily grind, who'd built a life for his family with his hands and his heart. "Dad, what if I'm not good enough? What if I try and fail?"

Dan stood up and walked over to his son, putting a callused hand on Nick's shoulder. "Nick, I've been working construction for twenty years. I've seen a lot of people try and fail. But I've never seen anyone regret trying. The only regret I've ever seen is from people who never tried at all."

Nick looked up at his father, and for a moment, he saw the man who'd taught him everything he knew about being a man, about working hard, about never giving up on your dreams. "Dad, I want to be good at this. I want to be really good."

"Then practice," Dan said simply. "Practice until your fingers bleed, until you can play that guitar in your sleep, until you're so good that nobody can ignore you. That's how you make it in this world, son. You work harder than anyone else, and you never give up."

That evening, the Johnson family sat around the kitchen table for dinner, and the conversation turned to the day's events and the kind of small talk that holds families together. Grace had made meatloaf and mashed potatoes, comfort food that filled the house with the smell of home and the particular kind of warmth that comes from people who love each other despite their differences.

"So," Grace said, looking at Nick across the table, "did you and your father have a good talk in the garage?"

Nick looked at his father, and for a moment, they shared a look that said everything without words. "Yeah, Mom. We had a really good talk."

Dan smiled and reached for another helping of mashed potatoes. "Your son's getting pretty good on that guitar, Grace. I think he might have a real talent for this."

Grace looked from her husband to her son, and for a moment, she saw the two men she loved most in the world, united by something that she didn't quite understand but that she was beginning to respect. "Dan, you're not encouraging him to drop out of school, are you?"

"Of course not," Dan said, putting down his fork. "But I am encouraging him to follow his heart. There's nothing wrong with having dreams, Grace. There's nothing wrong with wanting to be more than what you are."

Grace studied her husband's face, and for a moment, she saw the man she'd fallen in love with, the man who'd never been afraid to take risks, who'd never been afraid to believe in something bigger than himself. "Dan, I just want him to have options. I want him to be able to take care of himself, to have a future that doesn't depend on luck or timing or whether some record executive thinks he's good enough."

"Mom," Nick said quietly, "I'm not saying I'm going to drop out of school. I'm just saying that maybe music is part of who I am. Maybe it's not just a hobby."

Grace looked at her son, and for a moment, she saw the boy she'd raised, the boy who'd learned to walk in this house, who'd learned to talk in this kitchen, who'd learned to dream in this garage. "Nick, I want you to understand something. Your father and I both want you to be happy. We both want you to have the kind of life that makes you proud."

"Then let me try," Nick said, looking from his mother to his father. "Let me try to be good at this. Let me practice until I'm so good that nobody can ignore me. That's what Dad said. That's how you make it in this world."

Grace looked at her husband, and for a moment, she saw the man who'd taught their son that hard work was its own reward, who'd never once complained about the daily grind, who'd built a life for their family with his hands and his heart. "Dan?"

Dan reached across the table and took his wife's hand. "Grace, I think our son is right. I think he should follow his dreams. But I also think he should finish school, should have options, should be prepared for whatever comes next."

Nick looked at his parents, and for a moment, he felt the weight of their love, the weight of their hopes for his future, the weight of everything they'd sacrificed to give him opportunities they'd never had. "Mom, Dad, I promise I'll finish school. I promise I'll do my homework. But I also promise that I'll practice until I'm so good at this that nobody can ignore me."

Grace studied her son's face, and for a moment, she saw the man he was becoming, the man who would carry Dan Johnson's name into a future that neither of them could imagine. "Nick, I believe in you. And I believe in your father's judgment. If he thinks you have talent, then I think you should follow it."

Dan smiled and reached for his son's hand. "That's my boy. That's the kind of determination that gets things done in this world."

Nick looked at his parents, and for a moment, he felt the particular kind of love that comes from people who believe in you, who support your dreams, who want you to be more than what you are. "Thanks, Dad. Thanks, Mom. I won't let you down."

The Johnson family sat around the table, united by love and shared dreams, and for a moment, the kitchen felt like a place where anything was possible, where working-class kids could make music that mattered, where dreams weren't luxuries but necessities, where a family could support each other through whatever came next.

The Johnson house on Dutton Road was small, there was no denying that. But it held something that couldn't be measured in square feet or dollar amounts. It held the love of a family that believed in each other, the dreams of a son who believed in honest music, and the particular kind of hope that comes from knowing that sometimes, just sometimes, love and determination are enough to change everything.

And Nick Johnson sat in the center of it all, surrounded by the people who loved him most in the world, dreaming of a future where working-class kids could make music that mattered, where dreams weren't luxuries but necessities, where a garage on Dutton Road could be the birthplace of something that would change the world.
