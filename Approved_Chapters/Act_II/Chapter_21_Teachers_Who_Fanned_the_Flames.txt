# CHAPTER 21: TEACHERS WHO FANNED THE FLAMES - THE JAZZ BAND INCIDENT

---

May 5th, 1979, and Bob Wentworth was about to make the mistake that would go down in Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School history as the day a music teacher tried to heal a broken friendship and accidentally made everything worse.

The jazz band rehearsal had started like any other Tuesday afternoon. Seventeen students scattered around the music room, instruments in various states of readiness, sheet music arranged with the kind of careful precision that suggested they were taking this seriously, even if they weren't entirely sure what they were doing. The Battle of the Bands was two days away, and everyone was feeling the pressure.

But Bob Wentworth had been thinking about something else entirely. He'd been thinking about Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh, about the way they'd been avoiding each other in the hallways, about the way the school had become a battlefield divided along lines that had nothing to do with geography and everything to do with class and privilege and the kind of wounds that music was supposed to heal.

He'd been thinking about the 1975 talent show, about two thirteen-year-old boys who had stood on a gymnasium stage and made magic happen, about the photograph he still kept in his desk drawer—Nick and Jackie at age twelve, arms around each other, both holding guitars, smiling ear to ear, innocent, with all of life's possibilities in front of them.

"Gentlemen, ladies," Wentworth said, looking around the room at seventeen teenagers who were about to become witnesses to something they'd never forget, "I want to try something different today."

## **THE ATTEMPT AT HEALING**

"'Take Five' is in 5/4 time," Wentworth explained, holding up the sheet music for Dave Brubeck's jazz classic. "It's about being off-balance, about finding beauty in the unexpected. I want Nick and Jackie to work on this together. See if you can reimagine it through your own musical perspectives."

The room went quiet. Seventeen teenagers suddenly aware that they were about to witness something that had nothing to do with jazz and everything to do with the two boys who had once been best friends and were now something else entirely.

Nick looked at Jackie across the room, and for a moment, it was like looking at a stranger. Two years of careful distance, of pretending they didn't know each other, of walking past each other in hallways without acknowledgment—it had done its work. They were no longer the boys who had won the middle school talent show together. They were something else now.

"Mr. Wentworth," Nick said, his voice careful, controlled, "I'm not sure this is a good idea."

"Nick," Wentworth said, and his voice carried the kind of gentle authority that comes from twelve years of teaching teenagers how to be human beings, "sometimes the best way to move forward is to remember where you started."

Jackie was already moving toward the piano, his movements precise, controlled, the way they always were. "I'll take the bass line," he said, and his voice was steady, professional, completely unlike the voice he'd used when they were thirteen and everything was still possible.

They started to play. Jackie's classical training made the unusual time signature easy—his left hand finding Brubeck's rhythm while his right hand began adding flourishes that were pure Freddie Mercury, theatrical runs that transformed jazz sophistication into something more operatic, more emotionally expansive.

Nick listened, found the spaces between Jackie's notes, and started threading Aerosmith's blues-based rock through the jazz structure. His guitar work was careful at first, tentative, like someone testing the ice on a pond that might not support his weight.

For thirty seconds, it worked.

For thirty seconds, Bob Wentworth was right and music could heal anything. The two boys weren't looking at each other, but they were listening, and in that listening was the ghost of what they'd been at thirteen—two kids who could make magic when they stopped thinking about why they shouldn't.

## **THE INTELLECTUAL INTERFERENCE**

Then Mr. Don Gould walked in.

Gould taught AP American History, coached the debate team, and considered himself a maverick educator because he wore jeans on Fridays and encouraged his students to call him "Don" during informal discussions. He was thirty-four, had a master's degree from Harvard, and believed that everything was a teachable moment if you were just clever enough to frame it correctly.

"Sounds like democracy in action," he announced, his voice loud enough to break the spell that had been forming between two boys who'd almost remembered how to harmonize. "The American experiment playing out in real time. You know what this reminds me of? The Lincoln-Douglas debates. Same fundamental tension, different century."

Nick's fingers stumbled on the fretboard. Jackie's right hand fell silent, leaving only the bass line carrying a rhythm that suddenly felt lonely.

The jazz band had gone completely silent. Seventeen teenagers watching a car crash in slow motion, understanding that they were witnessing something that would be talked about in hallways for weeks, something that would become part of the school's mythology about the year music turned into warfare.

"Think about it," Gould continued, warming to his theme because Don Gould loved nothing more than the sound of his own pedagogical relevance echoing through rooms full of teenagers who were trying to navigate their own lives without turning everything into a history lesson. "Johnson here represents the Jacksonian tradition—man of the people, authentic democracy, rough edges and all. Marsh embodies more of the Jeffersonian ideal—educated, refined, believing that excellence elevates everyone around it. Both American traditions! Both valid perspectives on what this country should be!"

## **THE EXPLOSION**

"We're just playing music," Nick said, but his voice carried danger now, the particular edge that comes from being reduced to a symbol when you're trying to be a person.

"Are you though?" Gould asked, doing that thing teachers do when they think they're Socrates but sound more like someone who's read too many education journals and not enough actual books. "Or are you playing out the eternal American tension between populism and intellectualism, between—"

"Between the kids who matter and the kids who don't?" Nick interrupted, standing up so quickly his guitar strap caught on the music stand, sending sheet music cascading to the floor like snow that had given up trying to be beautiful. "Between the ones whose parents can donate new instruments to the music program and the ones whose parents die in industrial accidents that get blamed on worker error?"

Gould's face showed the confusion of an adult who'd thought he was facilitating dialogue but had accidentally detonated something much more dangerous.

"You want to make us a lesson?" Nick continued, unplugging his guitar with movements that were precise, controlled, furious. "Fine. Here's your lesson. Jackie's going to Juilliard or Yale or wherever kids like him go when they're done slumming with real music. I'm going to work construction like my dad, maybe get lucky and die quick like him too. That American enough for your educational purposes?"

"Nick," Jackie said quietly, and it was the first time he'd said Nick's name in two years, the first time his voice had carried anything other than careful politeness when addressing the boy who'd once been his best friend.

"What?" Nick turned to face him directly, all the careful distance they'd maintained dissolving into raw confrontation. "You want to pretend this is about music? Like we're equals because we can both play some notes? Your dad made it pretty clear on that bridge yesterday. These boys don't understand boundaries."

"That's not what I—" Jackie started, but Nick was already packing his guitar, already moving toward the door, already proving that some conversations are too late before they even begin.

"Yeah? Then why'd you move? Why'd you step aside like a good little prince letting the peasants pass?"

"Because I was trying to avoid this!" Jackie gestured at the space between them, at the unbridgeable everything that had opened up like a chasm in the music room floor. "Because I thought maybe we could get through one week without—"

"Without what? Without admitting that Hop Brook might as well be the Atlantic Ocean? Without pretending that your Queen covers aren't just you slumming with the real people?"

"Slumming?" Jackie's voice cracked, and suddenly he wasn't the professor's son or the classical prodigy or the kid who was destined for better things. He was just seventeen and confused and tired of being blamed for advantages he'd never asked for but couldn't afford to give up.

"You think this is slumming? You think I want—" He stopped, looked around the room full of their classmates, their teachers, their judges. "You know what? Forget it. You want a war? Fine. Friday night, we'll see whose America wins."

## **THE AFTERMATH**

He left then, didn't storm out so much as evacuated with dignity intact, the way his parents had taught him to exit situations that had become beneath his dignity. The piano bench scraped against the floor, a harsh note that hung in the air like an accusation.

Gould looked around the room at the wreckage of his teachable moment, at the seventeen teenagers who were staring at him like he'd just burned down something they'd been trying to build. "I was just trying to provide historical context—"

"Don," Wentworth said, and his voice carried the exhaustion of every teacher who'd ever watched good intentions detonate in a room full of kids who were trying to figure out how to be human beings while adults insisted on turning their lives into educational opportunities.

"Get out."

The jazz band never quite recovered from that afternoon. They still met, still practiced, still performed at the spring concert. But something had been broken that couldn't be fixed with better arrangements or more practice time. The magic that happens when individual musicians become a single organism had been replaced by the careful professionalism of people who were doing their jobs without expecting miracles.

## **THE FINAL COUNTDOWN**

May 7th, 1979, the day after the jazz band explosion, Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh walked the hallways of Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School like former friends who'd discovered they were actually enemies, who'd been pretending to coexist when they should have been preparing for war.

The Battle of the Bands was forty-one hours away. The school was choosing sides not just in a musical competition, but in the dissolution of something it had once celebrated, something that had proved harmony was possible between kids from different sides of anything.

But harmony, it turned out, was more fragile than anyone had realized. More vulnerable to adult interference, peer pressure, and the American insistence that everything, even friendship, even music, even the dreams of teenagers, must be reduced to competition with clear winners and obvious losers.

Bob Wentworth had tried to build a bridge with music. Don Gould had tried to build one with historical analysis. Both had failed because you can't bridge a divide by explaining it, can't heal a wound by describing its characteristics, can't make harmony happen by pointing out that it should be possible.

The teachers had fanned the flames, not with malice, but with the kind of well-meaning interference that makes everything worse. And now the Battle of the Bands was no longer just a competition. It was a referendum on everything that had gone wrong, on everything that could have been right, on the particular tragedy of watching something beautiful die at the hands of people who thought they were trying to save it.

The only question was: what would happen when it all came crashing down?
