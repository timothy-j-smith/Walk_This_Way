## CHAPTER 16: SENIOR YEAR - NICK'S WORLD

The cafeteria at Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School smelled like industrial food service and teenage desperation. Nick Johnson sat at his usual table with Stephen Murphy and Mark Pianka, three working-class kids who'd learned to navigate the social minefield of a regional high school where economic divisions played out in lunch table geography.

Nick was seventeen now, hardened by three years of being the man of the house since his father's death. His hands showed the calluses of someone who worked for a living—weekend shifts at the auto shop, helping Grace with repairs around the house, anything to keep the lights on. His clothes were clean but worn, his boots resoled with duct tape, his hair cut short in the practical style of someone who couldn't afford to care about fashion.

Across the cafeteria, Jackie Marsh sat with the college-bound crowd, kids who talked about SAT scores and summer programs in Europe. Jackie looked like he'd stepped out of a J. Crew catalog—clothes that fit like they'd been born on his body, hair that fell across his forehead in that carelessly perfect way that took serious money to maintain.

The tension had been building for weeks since the Battle of the Bands poster appeared. Kids had chosen sides, drawn invisible lines in the cafeteria, created an us-versus-them dynamic that turned every interaction into a potential confrontation.

That's when Jackie stood up.

"Somebody to Love," he announced to the cafeteria, his voice carrying that prep school confidence that turned statements into declarations.

The room went quiet. Students turned in their seats, sensing something was about to happen.

Jackie began to sing, his classically trained voice soaring through the cafeteria with operatic power. His band members joined in, moving with theatrical precision—the Queen influence evident in every gesture. The cafeteria transformed around them—lunch tables became concert seating, fluorescent lights became stage lights, and Jackie commanded the space like a born performer.

"We are the champions, my friends," he sang, technically perfect and undeniably impressive.

The college-bound students began clapping in rhythm, swept up in the performance. For four minutes and thirty-two seconds, Jackie Marsh owned that cafeteria.

But when the song ended, Nick stood up.

"Dream On," he announced, stepping forward without any instruments.

Nick began to sing a cappella, his voice rougher than Jackie's but carrying something deeper—pain, hope, authentic human experience. Stephen Murphy joined in with harmonies, Mark Pianka played air piano with fingers that moved like magic in the cafeteria air, and the three of them created something raw and real.

"Half my life's in black and white photographs," Nick sang, building to the climax. "Dream until your dreams come true!"

The final note hung in the air. The cafeteria was silent.

Both performances were brilliant, but in completely different ways. Jackie's was technically perfect, emotionally vacant. Nick's was technically flawed, emotionally devastating.

Mr. Wentworth broke the silence. "You should save something for Battle of the Bands. Now come with me."

Nick and Jackie couldn't look at each other as they exited with Mr. Wentworth, heading toward the principal's office.

Principal Brad Sargent sat across from both boys, looking exasperated. He'd been doing this job for two decades, typically dealing with fist fights and bloody noses or confiscating warm Budweiser and marijuana from the working-class kids. Suspensions be damned.

"Gentlemen," he said, "I'm hearing reports of students skipping classes to practice, arguments in the hallways, and now cafeteria performances that disrupt lunch periods."

"We're just preparing for the competition, sir," Jackie said, his voice carrying that prep school politeness that made everything sound like a reasonable explanation.

"Music is supposed to be passionate," Nick countered, his voice rougher, more direct.

"Music is supposed to bring people together," Principal Sargent said. "You two are tearing this school apart."

He leaned back in his chair, considering his options. "I should shut this dang thing down, but we have other respectable performers from neighboring schools and the WBCN crew..."

Nick and Jackie exchanged a look. Neither of them wanted the Battle of the Bands shut down.

"Here's what's going to happen," Principal Sargent continued. "You two will keep your musical differences off school property until March 15th. No more cafeteria performances, no more hallway arguments, no more disrupting classes. Understood?"

"Yes, sir," they both said simultaneously.

"Good. Now get out of my office and try to remember that you're supposed to be setting an example for the younger students, not teaching them how to wage war."

As they walked down the hallway, the tension between them was palpable. Three years of silence, three years of choosing different paths, three years of becoming exactly what the other had feared they'd become.

"It's on," Nick finally said, his voice low and determined.

"Let the best band win," Jackie replied, his voice carrying that prep school confidence.

Nick stopped and looked at him directly. "I'm counting on it."

The words hung between them like a challenge, like a promise, like the sound of a door closing that would never open again.

That evening, Nick sat in the garage that Dan had built with his own hands, surrounded by tools that would never be used again, holding the guitar that had once brought him and Jackie together. The Battle of the Bands poster was taped to the wall above the workbench, a reminder of what was coming, of the moment when he would finally have the chance to prove that working-class kids could make music that mattered.

Grace appeared in the doorway, still wearing her Friendly's uniform, her hands red from washing dishes all day. "You okay, honey?"

Nick looked up at his mother, seeing the lines of worry that had deepened since Dan's death, the exhaustion that came from working double shifts to keep the lights on. "I'm fine, Mom. Just thinking about the competition."

Grace sat down on the workbench, her movements careful and tired. "You know, your father would be proud of you. He always said you had a gift."

Nick felt something tighten in his chest. "I know, Mom. I just... I just want to make him proud."

"You already do," Grace said, her voice carrying the particular warmth that came from a mother's love. "Every day, you make him proud."

Nick looked around the garage, at the tools that Dan had left behind, at the memories that hung in the air like smoke from a campfire. "I miss him, Mom. I miss him every day."

Grace reached out and touched his shoulder, the gesture carrying the weight of three years of grief, of carrying on when everything felt impossible. "I know, honey. I know."

They sat in the quiet for a long time, listening to the sounds of the neighborhood settling in for the night, understanding that the Battle of the Bands was about more than music—it was about proving that working-class kids could make something beautiful, that they could create art that mattered, that they could honor the memory of a man who had believed in the power of music to transcend class and circumstance.

Nick picked up his guitar and began to play, his fingers finding the chords that Dan had taught him, the melodies that had once brought him and Jackie together. The music filled the garage, carrying the weight of grief and hope and the terrible knowledge that the world was not a safe place, but that sometimes the only way to survive was to create something beautiful.

Grace listened, her eyes closed, understanding that her son was carrying the weight of the world on his shoulders, that he was trying to honor his father's memory while building a future that would have to be earned one day at a time.

The Battle of the Bands was ten days away, and Nick was ready to prove that working-class kids could make music that mattered, that they could create art that transcended the circumstances of their birth, that they could honor the memory of a man who had believed in the power of music to bring people together.

Even if it meant going to war with his former best friend.
