## CHAPTER 14: MAXANNE #3 - THE FRIENDSHIP ENDS

*[The amber lights of the WBCN booth cast long shadows across the empty studio as Maxanne takes another sip of bourbon and lights a fresh cigarette. The hour is late, the audience dwindling, but she has a story to tell—the story of how a friendship died.]*

You know, folks, I've been thinking about Nick and Jackie tonight, about how sometimes the things that bring us together can also tear us apart. Music, friendship, the simple act of being young and believing that the world was yours for the taking.

But here's the thing about being young—you don't know yet that the world has other plans. You don't know that there are forces at work that are bigger than friendship, bigger than music, bigger than the simple desire to make something beautiful together.

Dan Johnson's death in the spring of 1976 changed everything. Not just for Nick, but for the friendship that had once seemed unbreakable. You see, when you're fifteen and your father dies, you don't just lose a parent—you lose your childhood. You lose the luxury of being young and carefree and believing that tomorrow will take care of itself.

Nick became the man of the house overnight, and let me tell you, there's nothing more dangerous than a fifteen-year-old boy trying to be a man before he's ready. He stopped playing guitar because every note reminded him of Dan, every chord progression brought back memories of the man who had taught him that music was the one thing that didn't care where you came from.

But here's what Nick didn't understand—music doesn't care where you come from, but people do. And when you're grieving, when you're carrying the weight of responsibility that no fifteen-year-old should have to carry, you start to see the world differently. You start to understand that there are people who will never understand your pain, who will never understand what it means to lose everything and still have to keep going.

Jackie tried to be there for Nick. God knows he tried. He called, he stopped by, he offered to help with homework or just to talk. But Nick was building walls, pushing his friend away with the kind of stubbornness that comes from not knowing how to accept help, from feeling like he had to carry this burden alone because that's what men did.

"I don't want to talk about it," Nick would say, and the words came out harsher than he intended, carrying the weight of grief and anger and the terrible knowledge that the world was not a safe place.

Jackie would nod and say he understood, but he didn't. How could he? His father was alive and well, his family was intact, his future was mapped out in ways that didn't include the kind of practical concerns that Nick's family faced every day.

The chasm between them grew wider with each passing week, until it felt like they were living in different worlds, speaking different languages, carrying different kinds of pain.

But here's what I want you to understand—this wasn't just about grief. This was about class, about the way America teaches its children to divide themselves, about the way we learn to see each other as threats instead of allies.

Nick was learning that the world was a dangerous place, that people you loved could be taken away without warning, that sometimes the only way to survive was to build walls around your heart and hope that the pain couldn't find a way in.

Jackie was learning that there were some things he couldn't fix, some problems he couldn't solve, some pain he couldn't understand no matter how much he wanted to help.

And the music? The music that had once brought them together, that had once seemed like the answer to everything? It became a reminder of what they'd lost, of the innocence they could never get back, of the friendship that was slipping away like water through their fingers.

By the time summer arrived, Nick and Jackie were still friends in name only. They would see each other in the hallways at school, nod in recognition, maybe exchange a few words about homework or the weather. But the easy intimacy they'd shared in the garage, the perfect harmony they'd discovered at camp, the sense that they understood each other in ways that transcended words—all of that was gone.

The frequency, as Patty had called it, was still there. But it was buried under layers of grief and responsibility and the terrible knowledge that the world was not a safe place.

And then came the fall of 1976, six months after Dan's death, when everything would finally shatter on a wooden bridge over Hop Brook, in a moment that would define the rest of their lives.

But that's a story for another time, folks. Right now, I want you to think about Nick Johnson, fifteen years old, sitting alone in his father's garage, surrounded by tools that would never be used again, holding a guitar that he couldn't bring himself to play, understanding for the first time that the world was not a safe place, that people you loved could be taken away without warning, that sometimes the only way to survive was to build walls around your heart.

And I want you to think about Jackie Marsh, trying to reach across a chasm that was growing wider every day, not understanding why his best friend was pushing him away, not knowing that the distance between them was about more than grief—it was about the way America teaches its children to divide themselves, about the way we learn to see each other as threats instead of allies.

The frequency was still there. But it was beginning to feel like a memory of something that had once been possible, rather than a reality that could be maintained in the real world.

And sometimes, folks, that's the most heartbreaking thing of all—not the loss itself, but the knowledge that what you had was beautiful and temporary, that it couldn't survive the return to the real world, that the forces that brought you together were no match for the forces that would tear you apart.

But that's a story for another time. Right now, I'm going to take another sip of bourbon and think about Nick and Jackie, about the friendship that died in the spring of 1976, about the music that was never made, about the harmony that was lost forever.

Because sometimes the most important stories are the ones that never get told, the ones that die in the silence between the notes, the ones that remind us that the world is not a safe place, that people you love can be taken away without warning, that sometimes the only way to survive is to build walls around your heart.

And sometimes, folks, those walls are the most dangerous things of all.

*[Maxanne takes another sip of bourbon, the amber liquid catching the light as she stares out into the darkness beyond the booth, thinking about Nick and Jackie, about the friendship that died, about the music that was never made, about the harmony that was lost forever.]*
