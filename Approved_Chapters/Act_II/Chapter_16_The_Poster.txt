## CHAPTER 16: THE POSTER - THE BULLETIN BOARD THAT STARTED A WAR

April 2nd, 1979, a Monday morning that felt like any other Monday morning, except for one professional poster that had been mounted on the bulletin board that would change everything.

The Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School music room occupied a corner of the building where acoustics went to die. Cinder block walls painted institution green, fluorescent lights that buzzed in B-flat (seriously, B-flat—the janitor swore it was true), instrument lockers that hadn't been properly tuned since the Ford administration. But on the bulletin board between the jazz band schedule and a faded poster for the Boston Symphony that had been hanging there since disco was still cool, someone had mounted a professional poster that looked like it belonged in a record store instead of a high school music room.

**BATTLE OF THE BANDS**
**May 15, 1979**
**Open to all regional high schools**
**Grand Prize: $500 and professional recording session**
**Sponsored by WBCN "The Rock of Boston"**

What you need to understand is that five hundred dollars in 1979 was real money. Like, "buy a used car" money. Like, "pay for a semester of college" money. For a kid like Nick Johnson, it was three months of his mother's grocery budget, a new pair of boots that didn't have duct tape on the soles, maybe even a guitar that didn't have strings held together with dental floss. For a kid like Jackie Marsh, it was... well, it was validation that cost couldn't buy, proof that talent mattered more than trust funds, evidence that he could succeed on his own terms instead of his father's.

Bob Wentworth found them both standing there at 7:47 AM, staring at that poster from opposite sides of the room like gunslingers who'd accidentally chosen the same saloon. Wentworth was one of those music teachers who'd given up dreams of symphony halls for steady paychecks and summers off, but he'd never quite lost the ability to hear potential in the noise teenagers made—even when that noise sounded like someone was torturing a cat with a violin. He'd been trying to drum up interest in music lately, watching kids gravitate toward the new Apple II computers that had started appearing in the school's computer lab, worried that the next generation might choose keyboards over guitars.

"Gentlemen," he'd said, because Wentworth called all his students gentlemen or ladies, even the ones who'd never live up to either title (and let's be honest, most of them wouldn't). "I see you've noticed the announcement."

Neither boy moved. They just kept staring at that poster like it might reveal the secrets of the universe if they looked hard enough, like it was some kind of musical Rosetta Stone that would unlock the mysteries of rock and roll.

"We'll need to submit band names by Friday," Wentworth continued, trying to navigate the minefield he suddenly realized he was standing in. "Performance slots are limited. And by limited, I mean we can only fit about eight bands in the auditorium before the fire marshal starts asking uncomfortable questions."

That's when Nick finally looked at Jackie. Really looked at him for the first time in three years. And what he saw made his hands clench into fists—not because he was angry, exactly, but because he was trying to hold back the flood of emotions that came with seeing his former best friend for the first time since... well, since everything had gone wrong.

Jackie had grown into everything Nick had suspected he'd become: tall, clean, wearing clothes that fit like they'd been born on his body. His hair fell across his forehead in that carelessly perfect way that took serious money to maintain. He looked like the kind of kid who'd never wondered if the electricity would stay on till the end of the month.

And Jackie looked back at Nick, seeing what he'd feared: someone who'd hardened instead of grown, whose talent had been sharpened into a weapon by circumstance. Nick's clothes were clean but worn, his boots resoled with duct tape, his hands already showing the calluses of someone who worked for a living. He looked like the kind of kid who'd never had the luxury of treating music as art instead of escape.

"I suppose," Wentworth said into the silence that stretched between them like a tripwire, "you'll both be entering."

"With a Queen tribute," Jackie said, his voice steady and clear, making it sound more like a challenge than a suggestion.

"Aerosmith," Nick countered, the single word loaded with three years of stored resentment.

Wentworth sighed the sigh of a man who'd just watched a fuse being lit. "The competition is meant to celebrate musical diversity, not—"

"It's about proving who's real," Nick interrupted, still looking at Jackie. "Who understands what rock and roll actually means."

"It's about excellence," Jackie shot back. "About showing that rock can be more than three chords and teenage angst."

Wentworth looked from one boy to the other, understanding that he was witnessing something that went beyond music, beyond competition, beyond the simple desire to win five hundred dollars and a recording session. This was about proving something fundamental about who they were and what they believed in.

"Gentlemen," Wentworth said, his voice carrying the particular weight that comes from understanding that you're about to witness something important, "I attended the '75 Curtis Middle School Talent Show. You brought the house down. You used to make beautiful music together."

He pulled out a photograph from his desk drawer—a faded snapshot of two boys at age thirteen, arms around each other, both holding guitars. Smiling ear to ear. Innocent. With all of life's possibilities in front of them.

"Look at this," Wentworth said, holding the photograph up so both boys could see it. "This is what you were capable of when you weren't trying to prove anything to anyone."

Nick and Jackie stared at the photograph, and for a moment it seemed like they might remember who they used to be, might remember the friendship that had once been the most important thing in both their lives.

But then Nick looked away, and Jackie looked away, and the moment passed.

"That was then," Nick said, his voice carrying the particular hardness that comes from learning that the world is not a safe place. "This is now."

"This is about proving that working-class kids can make music that matters," Nick continued, his voice rising with the particular passion that comes from having something to prove. "This is about showing that you don't need expensive instruments or fancy training to create something real."

"This is about proving that excellence isn't negotiable," Jackie countered, his voice carrying that prep school confidence that turned statements into declarations. "This is about showing that rock and roll can be more than garage band noise."

Wentworth watched them, understanding that he was witnessing the death of something beautiful, the transformation of friendship into rivalry, the moment when music stopped being about connection and started being about competition.

"What happened next," Wentworth would later tell anyone who would listen, "spread through that high school like flood water finding every possible crack."

By lunch, the entire school knew about the challenge. It wasn't just Nick Johnson versus Jackie Marsh anymore. It was working class versus privilege, garage rock versus conservatory training, Aerosmith versus Queen. Kids who'd never cared about music suddenly had opinions about production values and authenticity. The cafeteria became a battlefield of musical taste, with kids arguing about whether "Bohemian Rhapsody" was overproduced or whether "Dream On" was too simplistic.

The Lincoln kids—the ones from the even smaller town lined up behind Nick like they were forming a musical militia. The Sudbury kids split along economic lines: the Hop Brook divide made visible in cafeteria seating charts, with kids literally moving tables to sit with their chosen side. The few students from Boston who got bused in watched from the sidelines, smart enough to recognize a civil war when they saw one brewing and wise enough to stay out of it.

Even the teachers found subtle ways to show where their loyalties lay. Mr. Ogden, the physics teacher, adjusted his lesson examples to include references to sound waves and harmonics from Queen songs. Mrs. Williams, who taught home economics, could be heard quietly humming Aerosmith tunes as she demonstrated how to make a perfect pie crust. The janitor, who'd been working at the school since before rock and roll was invented, just shook his head and muttered something about "kids these days" while mopping up the cafeteria.

But almost no one knew what Wentworth knew. In 1975, Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh had won the middle school talent show as a duo. At thirteen, they had stood together on that gymnasium stage, fearless, their guitars having a conversation so moving it left not a dry eye in the house.

The poster hung on that bulletin board for six weeks, a professional WBCN creation that had somehow managed to turn an entire high school into a battlefield. Kids who'd never spoken to each other suddenly had opinions about musical authenticity. Teachers who'd never cared about rock and roll found themselves taking sides in arguments about production values and artistic integrity. The school newspaper ran a poll: "Queen or Aerosmith?" (Queen won by a landslide, but Nick's supporters claimed voter fraud.)

And somewhere in the middle of it all, two boys who had once been best friends prepared to go to war, each one convinced that victory would prove something fundamental about who they were and what they believed in. Nick practiced "Dream On" until his fingers bled, determined to prove that working-class kids could make music that mattered. Jackie rehearsed "Somebody to Love" until his voice was hoarse, equally determined to prove that excellence wasn't negotiable.

The Battle of the Bands was six weeks away, and everyone was already choosing sides. The only question was: would music bring them together again, or would it tear them apart forever?

Spoiler alert: it was going to be complicated.
