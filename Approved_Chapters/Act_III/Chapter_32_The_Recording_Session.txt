# CHAPTER 32: THE RECORDING SESSION

---

## CHAPTER 32: THE RECORDING SESSION

March 16th, 1979. Midnight.

The Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School parking lot was empty except for two cars—Grace Johnson's battered Ford Pinto and Dr. Richard Marsh's Mercedes-Benz sedan, both waiting in the shadows like predators stalking the same prey. Inside the school, the Battle of the Bands had ended three hours earlier, but the real battle was just beginning.

Nick Johnson sat on the hood of his friend Stephen Murphy's pickup truck, staring at the stars through the windshield of his own exhaustion. The Battle of the Bands had been everything he'd expected and nothing he'd wanted. Walking the Line had performed with raw authenticity, had channeled Dan's memory into every note of "Walk This Way," had even written an original song about the town's divisions that made half the audience cry and the other half uncomfortable.

But Patty had won. Not him, not Jackie. Patty, who'd reminded everyone that music wasn't about competition but about connection, about finding the resonance where all voices mattered.

And now Nick was sitting in an empty parking lot at midnight, wondering what the hell he'd been fighting for.

"Hey."

Jackie's voice came from the darkness, soft and uncertain. Nick looked up to see him approaching from the direction of the Mercedes, hands in his pockets, looking like someone who'd just realized he'd been playing the wrong game.

"Hey," Nick replied, and the word carried the weight of four years of silence.

They stood there for a moment, two seventeen-year-old boys who'd once been inseparable, now separated by everything that mattered and nothing that should have.

"I think I've been doing this for the wrong reasons," Nick said finally, the words coming out like a confession he'd been carrying too long.

Jackie nodded, not looking at him. "Me too. I forgot why I love music."

The silence stretched between them, filled with everything they'd lost and everything they'd never had the chance to find.

"Remember the garage?" Jackie asked, and his voice carried the particular ache of someone remembering something precious that was gone forever.

Nick smiled, the first real smile he'd felt in months. "The Queen/Aerosmith mashup. We were thirteen and we thought we could change the world."

"We were good together," Jackie said, and it wasn't a question.

"Yeah," Nick replied. "We were."

And then, because sometimes the most important moments happen in the spaces between words, they both started walking toward Nick's garage.

---

## THE GARAGE

The garage looked exactly the same as it had four years ago—Dan's tools still hanging on pegboards, the concrete floor still stained with oil and possibility, the same milk crates still stacked in the corner where they'd once sat and made music that felt like truth.

Nick pulled out two crates and set them facing each other, just like they'd done when they were thirteen. Jackie sat down across from him, and for a moment, they were just two kids who'd discovered that music could be a language when words failed.

"You still have your guitar?" Jackie asked.

Nick nodded toward the corner, where his Fender Stratocaster leaned against the wall like an old friend waiting to be remembered. "You?"

Jackie pulled out a small acoustic guitar from a case he'd been carrying. "Elizabeth's. She won't miss it for one night."

They sat there for a moment, two boys with guitars, remembering what it felt like to make music together.

"Remember how we used to argue about Queen versus Aerosmith?" Nick asked, tuning his guitar.

Jackie smiled, tuning his own. "Raw power versus technical precision."

"Different wavelengths of the same truth," Nick said, and the words felt like something Patty might have said.

They started playing tentatively, like two people learning to dance again after years apart. Nick began with the opening riff to "Sweet Emotion," and Jackie joined in with the melody from "Bohemian Rhapsody," and somehow, impossibly, it worked.

The garage filled with the sound of two voices finding each other again, of raw power meeting technical precision, of two boys who'd forgotten they were supposed to be enemies remembering they'd once been friends.

They played for an hour, maybe two, losing track of time in the way that only happens when you're making music that matters. They played "Dream On" and "Somebody to Love," they played "Walk This Way" and "Killer Queen," and they played the Queen/Aerosmith mashup that had won them the middle school talent show four years ago.

And when they finished, when the last chord had faded into the night, they sat there in the silence, two boys who'd just remembered what it felt like to be themselves.

"That's what we could have been," Jackie said quietly.

"Yeah," Nick replied. "That's what we could have been."

---

## THE RECORDING SESSION

The call came at 2 AM.

Maxanne Sartori's voice crackled through the phone line like a lifeline thrown across years of silence. "Boys, I've been listening to your story all night. I think it's time we made it official."

"Official?" Nick asked, holding the phone between them so Jackie could hear.

"Studio C at WBCN. Right now. I've got the night shift engineer here, and he's willing to stay late for a story that matters. You record that Queen/Aerosmith mashup, and we'll have proof that the resonance still exists."

Nick looked at Jackie, and Jackie looked at Nick, and in that moment, they both understood that this was their last chance to be who they'd once been.

"Okay," Nick said.

"Okay," Jackie echoed.

---

Studio C at WBCN was smaller than Nick had imagined, but it felt like a cathedral. The soundproof walls were covered with gold and platinum records from bands that had started here, that had believed in the power of authentic music before the industry turned it into product.

Maxanne was waiting for them, looking exactly like Nick remembered from the Battle of the Bands—seventy-three years old, bourbon in hand, eyes that had seen too much and remembered everything.

"Gentlemen," she said, "welcome to where dreams get recorded."

The engineer was a kid named Mike, maybe twenty-five, with the kind of enthusiasm that comes from working at a station that's about to die. He'd set up two microphones, positioned them so Nick and Jackie could face each other while they played, could see each other's eyes while they sang.

"This is it," Maxanne said, settling into the producer's chair. "One take. No overdubs. Just you two, making music the way it was meant to be made."

She paused, looking at the boys with the particular wisdom that comes from watching too many dreams die in translation. "Some music can't survive being made professional. Some wavelengths get lost when you try to clean them up. But tonight, we're going to try anyway."

Nick and Jackie sat down across from each other, guitars in hand, and for a moment, they were just two thirteen-year-old boys who'd discovered that music could be magic.

"Rolling," Mike said, and the red light came on.

They played the Queen/Aerosmith mashup—"Sweet Emotion" meeting "Bohemian Rhapsody," raw power harmonizing with technical precision, two voices finding the resonance where all differences dissolved into something beautiful.

And it was perfect.

Not technically perfect—there were moments when Nick's voice cracked with emotion, when Jackie's fingers stumbled over chords he'd played a thousand times. But it was emotionally perfect, authentically perfect, the kind of performance that happens when two people stop trying to be better than each other and start trying to be better together.

When they finished, when the last note had faded into the silence of the studio, they sat there looking at each other, two boys who'd just proven that the resonance still existed.

"Play it back," Maxanne said quietly.

Mike hit play, and the sound that came through the speakers was like hearing your own voice for the first time. Nick and Jackie listened to themselves, to the way their voices had blended, to the way they'd found each other again in the space between the notes.

"This is what we could have been," Jackie whispered.

"Yeah," Nick replied, and his voice was thick with tears. "This is what we could have been."

---

## THE FINAL FRACTURE

The door to Studio C opened, and Dr. Richard Marsh stepped inside like a man who'd been waiting for this moment.

"Jackie," he said, and his voice carried the particular authority of someone who'd been planning this intervention for months. "It's time to go home."

Behind him, Grace Johnson appeared, looking tired and worried and proud all at once. "Nick, honey, it's late. We should go."

The spell was broken. The resonance was jammed by the static of adult reality.

Nick and Jackie looked at each other across the studio, and in that moment, they both understood that this had been their last chance to be who they'd once been.

"Dad," Jackie started, but Dr. Marsh was already moving, already claiming his son with the kind of possessive love that destroys everything it touches.

"Jackie, we've discussed this. You have responsibilities. Commitments. A future that doesn't involve..." He gestured vaguely at the studio, at Nick, at everything that represented the path not taken. "This."

Grace stepped forward, her voice gentle but firm. "Nick, honey, we need to go. It's late, and you have school tomorrow."

They stood there for a moment, two boys caught between the music they'd just made and the futures that had been chosen for them.

"Can we..." Nick started, but Jackie was already shaking his head.

"Different worlds," Jackie said quietly, and the words felt like a death sentence.

"Yeah," Nick replied. "Different worlds."

Dr. Marsh put his hand on Jackie's shoulder—not comforting, but claiming. "Come on, son. Your mother's waiting in the car."

Grace took Nick's arm, gentle but insistent. "Let's go, honey. Your father would have been proud of what you did tonight."

They walked out of Studio C in separate directions, two boys who'd just proven that the resonance still existed, but who'd also proven that sometimes knowing something exists isn't enough to save it.

---

## THE DEMO TAPE

Maxanne sat alone in Studio C, holding the master tape in her hands like it was evidence of a crime that had never been committed.

"What do we do with this?" Mike asked, looking at the tape with the particular reverence that comes from witnessing something that shouldn't have been possible.

Maxanne stared at the tape, at the proof that two boys had once found the resonance where all voices mattered, and she made a decision that would haunt her for thirty years.

"We preserve it," she said quietly. "We preserve it because someone needs to remember that this was possible."

She slipped the tape into her purse, and for the next three decades, it would sit in her personal collection, a secret she carried with her like a promise she'd made to two boys who'd never had the chance to keep it.

---

## THE SEPARATION

Two months later, Jackie Marsh stood in Maxanne's office at WBCN, looking like someone who'd been carved down to his essential components.

"I wanted to tell you I'm leaving," he said, and his voice carried the particular weight of someone who'd been given a choice that wasn't really a choice. "Juilliard accepted me for senior year. It's an opportunity I can't refuse."

Maxanne looked at him, at the way his rebellion had been sanded off, leaving something smooth and acceptable and utterly lifeless. "Nick?" she asked.

"We don't talk," Jackie replied, and the words felt like a confession. "Different worlds, you know? It was a nice dream, but..."

"But America doesn't believe in nice dreams," Maxanne finished.

Jackie laughed, but it sounded like surrender. "Patty was right, though. About the resonance. Sometimes late at night, I can still hear it. That sound we made when we were thirteen. Before we knew we weren't supposed to harmonize."

He left that day, and Maxanne never saw him again.

---

## THE ARMY

April 1980. Grace Johnson called Maxanne, crying.

"He's joining the Army," she said, and her voice carried the particular pain of a mother who'd watched her son trade his dreams for the only opportunity America had given him. "For the GI Bill. For the chance at college that Hop Brook denied him."

Maxanne sat in her office, listening to Grace cry, and she understood that this was how America worked. Nick Johnson would trade his body for education, the American working-class bargain that never gets discussed at Tanglewood.

"He's a good boy," Grace said. "He deserves better than this."

"Yeah," Maxanne replied. "He does."

---

## THE FINAL BROADCAST

August 2009. WBCN's final night.

Maxanne Sartori sat in Studio B, holding the demo tape that had been in her personal collection for thirty years. The tape that proved two boys had once found the resonance where all voices mattered, before America convinced them they were supposed to be enemies.

"Ladies and gentlemen," she said into the microphone, her voice carrying the weight of fifty-six years of breaking bands and watching dreams die, "this is Maxanne Sartori, and for the next five minutes, you're listening to the final broadcast of WBCN, 104.1 on your FM dial."

She looked at the demo tape in her hands, at the proof that authentic music had once been possible, and she made a decision that would define the rest of her life.

"Before we go," she said, "I want to play you something. Something that was never supposed to be heard. Something that proves that the resonance still exists, if we're brave enough to tune in."

She paused, thinking about how radio was being replaced by algorithms that could predict your taste but couldn't recognize when a song might save your life. About how artificial intelligences could analyze your preferences but couldn't understand the difference between what you wanted to hear and what you needed to hear.

"But roads leave traces," she continued, her voice growing stronger. "Even when they're paved over, built around, forgotten. The old paths persist under the new ones. The resonances remain, waiting for someone to find them again."

She slipped the tape into the player, and for the next four minutes and forty-three seconds, the sound of Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh singing their Queen/Aerosmith mashup filled the airwaves for the last time.

The sound of two boys who'd once been inseparable, who'd once believed that music could bridge any divide, who'd once found the resonance where all voices mattered.

The sound of what could have been.

When the song ended, when the last note had faded into the silence of the studio, Maxanne looked at the clock.

11:59:30 PM.

"Ladies and gentlemen," she said, her voice thick with tears, "this is Maxanne Sartori, signing off for the last time. The resonance still exists. The resonance still exists. The resonance still exists."

11:59:59 PM.

The ON AIR goes dark. The booth falls silent. One last time. WBCN is dead. But somewhere in Sudbury even in a town divided by water and waste, you can still hear two boys harmonizing in the garage of memory. Still proving that anything is possible when you are a kid. Still waiting for the rest of us to catch up.

The silence stretches out like an old New England highway, but if you listen close enough you can still hear them singing. Rocking to Queen and Aerosmith.  

You can always hear them singing if you remember how to listen.  

Walk this Way, I think to myself, yeah, WALK THIS WAY.

THE END.
