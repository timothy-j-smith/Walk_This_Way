# WALK THIS WAY
## A Novel by Timothy J. Smith

---

## Novel Overview

**Genre:** Literary Fiction / Music Fiction  
**Setting:** Boston/Sudbury, Massachusetts (1975-1979) with 2024 frame story  
**Word Count Target:** ~75,000-90,000 words  
**Status:** In revision

### Logline

On the final night of WBCN radio's 56-year run, legendary DJ Maxanne Sartori tells the story of two teenage musicians—one working-class, one privileged—whose friendship and rivalry in the 1970s Boston revealed the moment when American rock and roll chose division over harmony.

### Themes

- Class division in America
- Authenticity vs. commercialization in music
- The death of local radio and community
- Friendship across social boundaries
- Nostalgia and cultural loss
- The price of selling out / staying true

---

## Story Structure

**Frame Story (2024):**  
Maxanne Sartori, 59, hosts the final 4-hour broadcast of WBCN before the station converts to automated Christian contemporary format. She uses this time to tell a story she's carried for 45 years.

**Main Story (1975-1979):**  
Nick Johnson (working-class) and Jackie Marsh (upper-class) meet in middle school, bond over music, win a talent show together. Their friendship fractures over the next four years, culminating in a Battle of the Bands that becomes a metaphor for larger American divisions.

---

## Main Characters

- **Maxanne Sartori** - WBCN DJ, narrator, keeper of the story
- **Nick Johnson** - Working-class rocker, raw talent, Aerosmith-influenced
- **Jackie Marsh** - Upper-class pianist, classically trained, Queen-influenced  
- **Grace Johnson** - Nick's mother, widow, Friendly's waitress
- **Dr. Richard Marsh** - Jackie's father, BU economics professor
- **Dan Johnson** (deceased) - Nick's father, construction worker, ghost presence

## Writing Style Notes

**Voice:**
- Maxanne: Conversational, intimate radio DJ voice with bourbon and vinyl
- Nostalgic but not saccharine - melancholic, slightly bitter
- Specific details over generic descriptions
- Show don't tell
- Avoid "was/were" constructions when possible

**Influences:**
- Stephen King's conversational accessibility
- James Dickey's poetic imagery  
- Marilynne Robinson's contemplative depth
