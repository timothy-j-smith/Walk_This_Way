08/31/25 Walk This Way Act II

ACT II: BATTLE 

Chapter 9: The Weight of Representation (V1)

"March twelfth, 1979," I announce, cueing up Bob Dylan's "The Times They Are A-Changin'" because sometimes you need the prophet of American folk to explain what happens when private pain becomes public statement. "Forty-eight hours before the Battle of the Bands, and both boys discovered that music had stopped being about music."
Two days after Grace Johnson's controlled explosion at Friendly's, and Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School felt like a community holding its breath, waiting to see whether Saturday night would validate her challenge to their comfortable assumptions or dismiss it as the emotional outburst of a working mother who'd forgotten her place in the social order.
"The thing about being chosen to represent something larger than yourself," I tell my audience, lighting another cigarette because some stories require continuous combustion, "is that you lose the right to fail on your own terms. Every mistake becomes evidence of your people's inadequacy. Every success becomes proof that your side was right all along. And every seventeen-year-old boy carries that weight differently."
Nick hadn't slept since Saturday. Not really slept—he'd closed his eyes, lay in his narrow bed listening to the house settle around him, but actual rest required a kind of peace that had been impossible since he'd heard about his mother's speech from Tommy Morrison, who'd heard it from his older sister, who'd been working the Saturday dinner shift at Friendly's when the story was still spreading through the restaurant like spilled syrup, sticky and impossible to clean up completely.
Monday morning, walking through the Glass Corridors where the Rats gathered between classes, Nick had been treated like something between a celebrity and a martyr. Kids who'd never spoken to him directly were suddenly asking about the Battle of the Bands, about whether he was ready, about whether he understood what was riding on Friday night's performance.
"Your mom's got balls, Johnson," Steve Kowalski had said, his voice carrying the particular respect that working-class kids reserve for adults who'd risked something real for something important. "Taking on those college assholes like that. Making them see what they've been pretending not to notice."
But respect felt heavier than indifference. At least when people ignored you, you could fail without disappointing anyone except yourself. Now Nick was carrying the hopes of every kid whose parents worked doubles, every family that counted pennies between paychecks, every teenage dream that had been dismissed as unrealistic by people who'd never had to choose between realistic and possible.
The garage on Dutton Road had become a pilgrimage site. Not literally—Grace wouldn't allow that, had made it clear that reporters and curiosity seekers weren't welcome—but kids from the northeast neighborhoods kept finding reasons to walk past, to listen to the music bleeding through the walls, to witness the sound of their own hopes being rehearsed in three-chord progressions and four-four time.
"Tuesday evening," I continue, stubbing out the cigarette and immediately lighting another, "Nick stood in that garage surrounded by his father's tools and his mother's expectations and his community's faith, trying to remember why he'd started playing music in the first place."
The Lincoln boys were there—Stephen, David, and Mark, the inseparable trio who'd found in Nick's friendship the fourth corner of a musical square they hadn't known they were building. They'd been practicing for six hours, running through their set list until their fingers bled and their voices cracked, transforming Aerosmith covers into something rawer, more urgent, more honest than the originals.
But something was wrong. The music that had felt like salvation was starting to sound like obligation. The garage that had been sanctuary was feeling like a pressure cooker. The songs that had once been about escape were becoming about proving something to people who'd already decided what they believed about kids from places like this.
"What if we're not good enough?" Mark asked during a break, his usually steady hands shaking as he adjusted the settings on his borrowed keyboard. "What if all this talk about representing working-class kids just makes it worse when we lose?"
"We're not going to lose," Nick said, but his voice carried the hollow certainty of someone trying to convince himself more than his bandmates.
"But what if we do?" Stephen pressed, his bass guitar leaning against his shoulder like a weapon he was too tired to carry. "What happens to your mom then? To all the people who believed in us?"
Nick didn't have an answer for that. Couldn't admit that he'd been asking himself the same question every night since Saturday, lying awake calculating the cost of failure when failure meant more than just losing a high school competition.
Meanwhile, across town on Concord Road, Jackie Marsh was experiencing a different kind of pressure—the weight of representing advantages he'd never asked for but couldn't escape, of proving that privilege and talent weren't mutually exclusive, that his musical education enhanced rather than replaced authentic feeling.
The Marsh basement had been transformed into something resembling a professional recording studio: proper acoustics, high-end equipment, microphones that cost more than most families' monthly grocery budgets. Crown Jewels rehearsed there with the precision of a military operation, every note planned, every harmony arranged, every moment choreographed to demonstrate that technical excellence and emotional truth could coexist in the same performance.
But Jackie had heard about Grace Johnson's speech too—everyone in Sudbury had heard about it—and the knowledge sat in his stomach like a stone, weighing down every song, making every perfectly executed passage sound like evidence of the very inequality his mother's outburst had challenged.
"Wednesday after school," I tell my dwindling audience, "Jackie found himself alone in that basement, playing 'Bohemian Rhapsody' on the Steinway grand, trying to find in Mercury's operatic complexity some answer to questions he didn't know how to ask."
Dr. Marsh had been increasingly present during rehearsals, offering suggestions that felt more like instructions, guidance that sounded more like management. The Battle of the Bands had become his son's audition for a future that had already been planned, a demonstration that proper preparation and family investment produced superior results.
"The key is maintaining artistic integrity while showcasing technical superiority," Dr. Marsh had said during Tuesday's practice session, his voice carrying the measured authority of someone who'd spent his career analyzing other people's work rather than creating his own. "The competition isn't really with other bands—it's with the assumption that authentic music requires authentic struggle. We're proving that excellence can be cultivated, that talent improves with proper support."
But Jackie was seventeen, not a thesis statement. And sitting alone in that perfect basement studio, playing songs that had once felt like rebellion, he couldn't shake the feeling that he was preparing to win an argument that missed the point entirely, to prove something that didn't need proving to people who wouldn't be convinced anyway.
The phone rang at 8:47 PM. Jackie almost didn't answer—he was working on the bridge section of "We Are the Champions," trying to find a way to make Mercury's anthem sound earned rather than assumed—but something about the persistence of the ringing made him pick up.
"Jackie?" The voice was tentative, familiar, impossible.
"Nick?"
Silence stretched between them like the years since they'd been friends, like the geographic distance that was measured in more than miles, like everything that had made this phone call inadvisable, dangerous, potentially devastating to both their carefully constructed positions in the war that was consuming their community.
"I heard about what your mom said at Friendly's," Jackie finally continued, his voice careful, controlled, carrying none of the classical projection he'd been trained to use when speaking mattered. "About the things people were saying about you. About us. About this whole competition."
More silence. Nick could hear classical music in the background—not Queen, something older, more formal, the sound of a house where culture was furniture rather than feeling.
"It's gotten weird, hasn't it?" Nick said finally. "This whole thing. Started as a music competition, turned into... I don't even know what to call it."
"A symbol," Jackie answered immediately, like he'd been thinking about this for weeks, which he had. "We've become symbols of things we never chose to represent. You're authentic struggle, I'm privileged excellence. Two different versions of the American dream, battling it out in a high school cafeteria."
"Is that what we are?" Nick asked, and his voice carried something Jackie hadn't heard since they were thirteen: genuine curiosity about what his former best friend actually thought, rather than what he was supposed to think.
"I don't know what we are anymore," Jackie admitted. "I know what everyone else thinks we are. I know what my dad wants me to be, what your mom needs you to prove, what the whole town is expecting from Friday night. But I don't know what we actually are to each other."
The admission hung between them like a bridge neither was sure they should cross.
"Do you remember," Nick said slowly, "when we wrote that song about the railroad crossing? About trains having to stop and wait for each other to pass?"
"Diamond Crossing," Jackie said immediately. "Yeah, I remember."
"Feel like that's what we're doing now. Sitting at the same intersection, but we can't both move forward. One of us has to win, which means one of us has to lose, and either way the trains don't actually connect. They just... wait for permission to continue in different directions."
Jackie was quiet for a long moment, long enough that Nick wondered if he'd hung up, if the metaphor had been too much, if admitting uncertainty was a luxury neither of them could afford with forty-eight hours left before they had to perform their assigned roles in other people's expectations.
"What if we're both wrong?" Jackie said finally. "What if this whole competition is just... another way to make sure we stay in our designated corners? What if winning means proving we can beat each other instead of proving we can make music that matters?"
"Then we're fucked either way," Nick said, and for the first time in months, Jackie laughed—not the careful, polite sound he'd learned to make at dinner parties and faculty gatherings, but the genuine amusement of someone who recognized truth when he heard it, even when the truth was uncomfortable.
"Yeah," Jackie agreed. "We're fucked either way."
They talked for seventeen more minutes. Not about strategy or set lists or the technical aspects of performing under pressure, but about the weight of carrying other people's dreams, about the impossible mathematics of representing entire communities through three-minute songs, about the loneliness of being chosen to prove things they weren't sure they believed themselves.
When they finally hung up, both boys sat in their respective practice spaces—garage and basement, concrete and carpet, necessity and privilege—and understood that Friday night would determine more than which band won a high school competition. It would decide whether two former best friends could find a way back to each other, or whether America's talent for division was stronger than their shared history of harmony.
"Thursday, March fourteenth," I tell my audience, checking the time because some stories need to breathe between their acts. "Twenty-four hours before the Battle of the Bands, and both boys spent their final day of preparation discovering that the hardest part of competition isn't playing better than your opponent. It's playing well enough to justify the faith that other people have invested in your success."
Nick practiced until his fingers bled, until his voice cracked, until the Lincoln boys finally went home to their own nervous preparations, leaving him alone with his father's tools and his mother's courage and the impossible responsibility of proving that working-class kids deserved more than sympathy.
Jackie played until the neighbors complained about the noise, until his parents finally suggested he get some rest, until the perfect basement studio felt like a beautiful prison where every song sounded like evidence in a trial he'd never asked to attend.
"Twenty-four hours," I repeat, stubbing out another cigarette and reaching for water because my throat is raw from smoke and story. "Twenty-four hours for a community to finish choosing sides, for two teenagers to prepare for a competition that had stopped being about music and started being about which version of America deserved to survive."
The ON AIR sign continues its steady pulse, counting down toward midnight, toward silence, toward the end of WBCN and everything it had once represented about the democratic possibility of radio, about voices that mattered more than the demographics that determined whose voices got heard.
"Coming up," I tell the darkness beyond the booth, "the Battle of the Bands itself. The night when Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School became a battleground for every assumption Americans make about class, talent, and what becomes possible when we stop pretending that geography doesn't determine destiny."
But first, one more song. Not from the battle itself—that comes later, when the stakes are highest and the irony is complete. This is from Thursday night, March fourteenth, 1979. A recording that nobody was supposed to make, that I discovered years later in WBCN's archives, labeled simply "Phone Call - Not for Broadcast."
Because sometimes the most important conversations happen when nobody's supposed to be listening, when two former friends finally admit they're more afraid of winning than losing, more terrified of representing their communities than disappointing them, more worried about justifying other people's faith than surviving their own doubt.
The tape is scratchy, incomplete, but through the static you can hear it: two seventeen-year-old boys trying to remember who they were before America taught them who they were supposed to be, before competition became more important than collaboration, before the frequency that had once connected them became another casualty of the static we create to keep ourselves properly divided.

Chapter 10: The Battle Begins (V1)

"March fifteenth, 1979," I announce, cueing up The Who's "Baba O'Riley" because sometimes you need the sound of teenage wasteland to capture the moment when everything changes. "Seven PM at Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School, and the cafeteria had been transformed into something that looked like democracy and felt like war."
The lunch tables were gone, replaced by folding chairs arranged in careful rows that would be ignored the moment the music started. The serving line had been converted into a makeshift backstage area, hidden behind sheets that the drama department had donated to the cause. The stage was just risers from the choir room, but under the rented lights it looked like Madison Square Garden to kids who'd never been farther than Worcester.
Eight hundred people crammed into a space designed for six hundred, and the fire marshal had already made two visits, the second time staying to watch because even municipal officials understood that something significant was happening in the suburban heart of Massachusetts.
"I arrived at six-thirty," I tell my audience, lighting another cigarette because witness requires ceremony. "Chuck Morrison was already there, testing microphones with his signature 'Testing, one-two, one-two, is this thing on?' that had become a Boston catchphrase. Carter Alan was setting up his judging materials with the precision of someone who'd learned to take teenage music seriously. Bob Walker from Strawberries Records sat at the judges' table looking like a man who understood that careers could be made or broken in high school cafeterias if you knew how to listen."
The crowd had sorted itself according to the invisible boundaries that had defined the school since September: Rampers claiming the seats closest to the stage, their elevated status translating to elevated seating. Rats gathering near the exits, ready to escape if things got too civilized for their taste. Regs scattered throughout the middle territories, trying to remain neutral in a conflict that had made neutrality impossible. Rollers clustering near the back, where they could keep an eye on their vehicles in the parking lot and make quick exits if the music got too soft for their mechanical sensibilities.
But something was different tonight. The careful segregation was blurring at the edges, kids sitting with friends who happened to like the same music regardless of which quadrant they called home. The brief musical democracy of the previous week had been officially discouraged, but democracy has a way of reasserting itself when something important is at stake.
Grace Johnson sat in the third row, still wearing her Friendly's uniform because she'd come straight from work, her defiance of Saturday afternoon transformed into quiet dignity that made everyone around her sit a little straighter. Dr. and Mrs. Marsh occupied seats that had been reserved for faculty families, their presence carrying the weight of institutional approval and parental expectation.
And scattered throughout the crowd, I spotted faces that told the real story: kids who'd driven over from Concord and Wayland and Framingham, word having spread that this wasn't just another high school talent show but something larger, more significant, worth witnessing even if you didn't have a dog in the fight.
"Seven-fifteen," I continue, stubbing out the cigarette and immediately lighting another. "Chuck Morrison took the stage to announce the rules, the prizes, and the order of performance. But his opening words carried weight that nobody had expected."
"Ladies and gentlemen," Chuck said, his voice carrying the boundless energy of someone who'd done enough cocaine to power a small city but still believed in the democratic possibility of rock and roll, "welcome to what might be the most important Battle of the Bands in New England history."
The crowd stirred, recognizing that they were being invited to witness something significant rather than endure something obligatory.
"Before we begin," Chuck continued, "I want to acknowledge something that's been building in this community over the past week. This competition started as a way to celebrate musical talent. But music, real music, has always been about more than entertainment. It's been about giving voice to experiences that don't get heard, about proving that excellence can emerge from any neighborhood, any background, any set of circumstances."
He paused, and in that pause was everything that had led to this moment: Grace Johnson's controlled explosion at Friendly's, the brief scrambling of social boundaries, the recognition that this small-town competition had become a referendum on larger American questions about class, opportunity, and what becomes possible when we stop pretending that geography doesn't determine destiny.
"Tonight, eight bands will take this stage. Some of you will hear technical excellence. Others will hear authentic emotion. All of you will hear kids who understand that music matters more than the markets that try to contain it. May the best band win—and may we all remember that 'best' means different things depending on what you're listening for.”

7:22 PM - The Jesters

Four freshmen from Lincoln took the stage like they were heading to their own execution, instruments held like shields against whatever was about to happen. Tommy Morrison—no relation to the judge's family—carried his bass with the particular care of someone who'd borrowed it from his cousin and couldn't afford to break it. Billy Chen sat behind a drum kit that had been assembled from three different garage sales, none of the pieces matching but all of them thunderous when played with conviction. Mickey Kowalski approached the microphone like it might bite him.
They opened with The Cars' "My Best Friend's Girl," and for three minutes and forty seconds, eight hundred teenagers remembered why they'd fallen in love with music in the first place. Not because it was sophisticated or meaningful or culturally significant, but because it was fun, because it made you move, because four kids who barely needed to shave could make eight hundred people forget their differences long enough to sing along with a chorus everyone knew.
The applause was genuine, enthusiastic, the kind that said 'you exceeded our expectations and reminded us why we're here.' The Jesters cleared their equipment with the efficiency born of practicing the breakdown as much as the performance, and the real competition could begin.

7:31 PM - Mechanical Breakdown

Detroit Rock City from Wayland took the stage in full KISS regalia—face paint applied with mathematical precision, platform boots that added six inches and subtracted mobility, the complete theatrical costume that proved rock and roll was as much about image as sound.
They launched into "Rock and Roll All Nite" with enthusiasm that made up for what they lacked in precision. The lead singer attempted Gene Simmons' trademark tongue-wagging while delivering vocals that were more theatrical than musical. The guitarist wore Ace Frehley's spaceman silver, though it was already starting to run under the hot stage lights.
Two minutes into their set, catastrophe struck in the form of amplifier failure.
The lead guitar cut out mid-solo, leaving the rhythm section and vocals carrying a song that suddenly sounded naked, incomplete. The guitarist frantically checked cables while maintaining his rock star pose, trying to troubleshoot technical problems while wearing makeup that was becoming more abstract by the minute.
"Keep playing!" Chuck Morrison shouted from the judges' table, understanding that equipment failure was part of rock and roll's authentic experience, that sometimes the show going on was more important than the show going right.
What happened next transformed disaster into legend. The crowd, recognizing that these kids needed help, started slow-clapping in rhythm, eight hundred people providing the beat while Detroit Rock City figured out their technical difficulties. Not mocking applause, not ironic appreciation, but genuine support that turned equipment failure into community collaboration.
When the amp finally roared back to life, the guitarist launched into a solo that was more enthusiastic than skilled but carried so much gratitude that it sounded like revelation. The crowd erupted in appreciation that had nothing to do with musical excellence and everything to do with recognizing courage under pressure.
The makeup was streaming down their faces by the final chorus, black and silver creating abstract art that made them look like they'd survived something significant. Which, in a way, they had.

7:47 PM - Controlled Chaos

The intermission was supposed to last ten minutes. Long enough for equipment changes, bathroom breaks, and the kind of nervous energy release that keeps high school events from combusting under their own pressure.
Instead, it became performance art.
Four kids nobody recognized simply walked onto the stage like they belonged there. No announcement, no introduction, no acknowledgment that they weren't on the official program. They just appeared—angular, awkward, wearing matching gray suits that looked like they'd been stolen from a funeral home.
The crowd buzzed with confusion. Parents checked their programs. Faculty members looked around for guidance. Administrators huddled near the stage, trying to decide whether this was authorized chaos or unauthorized disruption.
The lead singer approached the microphone with David Byrne's particular brand of neurotic precision, adjusted the stand with movements that were simultaneously mechanical and organic, cleared his throat like he was about to deliver a lecture on the anthropological significance of suburban anxiety.
"Psycho Killer," he announced in a voice that was completely flat, completely committed, and completely terrifying in its casual delivery of casual menace.
The opening bass line started, and suddenly everyone understood they were witnessing something unprecedented: art school pretension executed with such confidence that it became authentic, performance art that was simultaneously parody and the real thing.
"Psycho killer, qu'est-ce que c'est," the singer delivered in perfect monotone, his body moving with Byrne's signature awkward precision, "fa-fa-fa-fa-fa-fa-fa-fa-fa-far better..."
The crowd was transfixed. This wasn't rock and roll as they understood it—wasn't about power chords or emotional catharsis or the kind of musical rebellion that suburban teenagers could safely consume. This was something weirder, more dangerous, more genuinely subversive than anything they'd expected to encounter in their high school cafeteria.
Principal Sargent finally reached the stage, ready to shut down this unauthorized addition to the evening's entertainment. But when he looked out at the crowd—eight hundred teenagers paying more attention than they had all night, completely absorbed by something they'd never seen before—he made the kind of administrative decision that either gets you promoted or fired.
He stepped back. Let it happen. Trusted that sometimes the best education occurs outside the curriculum.
The song ended to confused but enthusiastic applause. The four art students bowed with perfect synchronization, gathered their instruments, and walked off stage like they'd just completed a successful scientific experiment. No words, no explanation, no attempt to capitalize on their moment of unauthorized brilliance.
"Ladies and gentlemen," Chuck Morrison announced, his voice carrying the bewildered admiration of someone who'd just witnessed something that defied categorization, "that was... well, that was something we'll all remember.”

8:03 PM - Building Momentum

The next two scheduled bands—a folk duo from Concord-Carlisle called Wind & Rain, and a prog rock ensemble from Framingham North called Temporal Shift—performed with the professional competence of kids who'd been properly prepared for musical competition. Good songs, solid execution, the kind of performances that would have won most high school talent shows but somehow felt insufficient after what had come before.
The crowd was getting restless, energized by the chaos and humor but ready for something that mattered, something that would justify the emotional investment that had brought eight hundred people to a school cafeteria on a Friday night in March.
"Eight twenty-four PM," I tell my audience, reaching for my water glass because my throat is getting rough from chain-smoking through this story. "Time for the main event. Time for three kids who'd once made beautiful music together to discover whether America would let them find that frequency again, or whether the static had become too loud to overcome."
The stage lights dimmed. Equipment was moved with the precision of roadies who understood that what happened next would determine more than who won a high school competition. In the wings, Jackie Marsh adjusted his shirt, checked his guitar tuning, and prepared to carry his family's expectations and his community's assumptions about what privilege was supposed to accomplish.
In the former serving area, Nick Johnson stood with the Lincoln boys—Stephen, David, and Mark—knowing that they were about to represent not just themselves but everyone who'd ever been told their dreams were too big for their circumstances, everyone whose mother had ever served coffee to people discussing their child's limited prospects, everyone who understood that authenticity wasn't something you could buy but something you had to earn.
And somewhere in the crowd, Patty Lally waited for her turn to remind everyone what harmony sounded like before America taught them that competition mattered more than collaboration, that winning was more important than making music that mattered.
"The real battle," I tell the darkness beyond the booth, "was about to begin.”

Chapter 11: The Prince Under Pressure (V1)

"Eight twenty-seven PM," I announce, cueing up Queen's "Under Pressure" because sometimes the obvious choice is the only choice that makes sense. "Jackie Marsh and Crown Jewels took the stage carrying the weight of every assumption about privilege, talent, and what excellence looks like when it's been carefully cultivated rather than desperately earned."
The crowd shifted as Jackie's band set up their equipment—professional gear that gleamed under the stage lights, instruments that had never known the indignity of pawn shop price tags or garage sale negotiations. Elizabeth Marsh, Jackie's thirteen-year-old sister, positioned herself behind a violin that cost more than most families' cars. Arthur "Ace" Callahan approached his drum kit with athletic precision, every movement calculated for maximum efficiency. Max Wesslen stood with his bass, trying to project confidence through sheer force of will.
And Jackie himself moved to center stage like he'd been born for spotlights, carrying his Martin D-28 with the casual care of someone who'd never had to worry about replacing what he broke. But something was different tonight. The easy confidence that had carried him through two years of high school performances was cracking under pressure that had nothing to do with music and everything to do with representing advantages he'd never asked for but couldn't escape.
"The thing about Jackie's situation," I tell my audience, lighting another cigarette, "was that he couldn't win. Technical perfection would be dismissed as purchased rather than earned. Emotional authenticity would be suspect because what did rich kids have to be authentic about? He was trapped in a box constructed by other people's assumptions about what privilege meant and what it cost."
The northwest section of the cafeteria—his natural constituency—sat with the kind of nervous energy that comes from having invested too heavily in an outcome you can't control. Sarah Morrison gripped her program with hands that showed the stress of someone whose social position was somehow tied to Jackie's musical performance. The faculty families exchanged meaningful glances that said they understood this was about more than high school competition.
But it was the other three quarters that Jackie was really playing for. The Rats in their Glass Corridor exile, arms crossed, waiting for evidence that classical training was just expensive fakery. The Regs scattered throughout the middle territories, genuinely curious about whether technical excellence could coexist with emotional truth. The Rollers near the back, evaluating everything through their mechanical understanding of power, precision, and what it took to make something actually work.
"Ladies and gentlemen," Jackie said into the microphone, his voice carrying that prep school pronunciation that turned statements into declarations, "we are Crown Jewels. And we'd like to take you on a journey."
The word "journey" landed wrong. Too precious, too programmatic, too much like something his father would say when discussing educational philosophy at faculty dinners. A few kids in the Rat section actually groaned audibly.
But then Elizabeth's violin began the opening notes of "Killer Queen," and everything changed.
The arrangement was audacious in its complexity and ambition. What Mercury had created as camp rock opera, Crown Jewels transformed into something that sounded like it had been written by Bach if Bach had understood electricity, amplification, and the particular loneliness of being seventeen in suburban Massachusetts.
Jackie's voice found notes that shouldn't have been possible, classically trained precision applied to rock and roll attitude in ways that made both more interesting. "She's a Killer Queen, gunpowder, gelatine, dynamite with a laser beam..."
The crowd was quiet, not because they were bored but because they were processing something they'd never heard before: sophistication that wasn't condescending, technical excellence that served emotional expression rather than replacing it, classical training applied to popular music in ways that enhanced rather than diminished both traditions.
Even the Rats were listening now, grudgingly impressed despite themselves. Tommy Morrison, leather jacket and Aerosmith patches, found himself nodding along to violin lines that transformed rock anthems into chamber music that still rocked.
"But here's what killed me," I continue, stubbing out the cigarette with unnecessary force, "watching Jackie's face during that first song. He was technically perfect, emotionally controlled, professionally competent. And absolutely terrified that none of it mattered, that all his training and preparation and family investment would be dismissed as purchased excellence, artificial authenticity, the musical equivalent of a trust fund."
They moved into "Somebody to Love," and this was where the cracks started showing. The song is about desperation, about being so lonely you'd pray to any god who'd listen, about the kind of spiritual hunger that money can't fill and education can't cure. It requires vulnerability, emotional nakedness, the willingness to admit you need something you can't provide for yourself.
Jackie sang every note perfectly. Hit every harmony, nailed every dynamic change, executed the arrangement with the precision his classical training had drilled into him since childhood. But precision wasn't what the song demanded. "Somebody to Love" needed mess, needed imperfection, needed the kind of raw human hunger that proper musical education was designed to contain rather than express.
The crowd felt it too. Not conscious rejection, but subconscious recognition that something essential was missing. They appreciated the technical accomplishment while remaining emotionally unmoved, impressed by the performance while remaining untouched by the performer.
Danny Kowalski, the Roller mechanic who evaluated everything through his understanding of functional engineering, whispered to his girlfriend: "It's like a perfectly restored car that runs great but has no soul. All the parts work, but it doesn't feel like it was ever actually driven."
"Song three," I tell my audience, "was where Jackie decided to risk everything. Where he chose emotional truth over technical safety, authenticity over approval, the possibility of connection over the certainty of competent performance."
Instead of the Queen anthem everyone expected, Jackie moved to the piano—the same Steinway they'd somehow convinced the school to transport from the music room. He sat alone at the bench, his band stepping back to give him space, the stage suddenly feeling larger and more dangerous.
"This next song," he said into the microphone, his voice carrying something it hadn't carried before: uncertainty, vulnerability, the recognition that what happened next might determine more than a high school competition, "isn't a cover. It's something I wrote. About pressure. About expectations. About what it costs to be perfect when perfect isn't what anyone actually wants."
The silence was complete now, eight hundred teenagers understanding they were about to witness something unprecedented: original music from someone who was supposed to represent purchased rather than created art, preparation rather than inspiration, safety rather than risk.
Jackie's hands found the keys, and the opening notes were simple, honest, nothing like the complex arrangements Crown Jewels had been delivering. Just melody, rhythm, and whatever truth a seventeen-year-old boy could find when he stopped trying to justify his advantages and started admitting his disadvantages.
"Under pressure from the day I learned to speak," he sang, his classically trained voice stripped of everything except honesty, "Every word I say is weighed and measured, Every note I play is judged and found wanting or approved..."
The lyrics were autobiographical in ways that made everyone uncomfortable: the son of privilege admitting that privilege was its own kind of prison, that expectations could be as crushing as limitations, that being told you were destined for success was its own form of pressure.
"They say I've got advantages, and maybe that's true, But advantages don't teach you how to feel, Don't show you how to bleed music instead of playing notes, Don't help you find the frequency where your voice matters..."
The crowd was riveted. This wasn't what they'd expected from the prep school prince, wasn't the polished performance that would validate or challenge their assumptions about class and talent. This was just a teenage boy admitting he was scared, confused, trapped by other people's investments in his success.
"I practice in a basement that costs more than your house," Jackie continued, the brutal honesty making everyone flinch, "But I'd trade it all for your garage, For the freedom to fail without disappointing investors, For the right to be imperfect without betraying trust funds..."
Grace Johnson, still in her Friendly's uniform, started crying. Not sad tears, but recognition tears, the kind that come when someone articulates something you felt but couldn't express: that different kinds of pressure could produce the same kind of pain, that privilege and limitation could both trap you in boxes you didn't choose, that seventeen was too young to carry anyone's dreams but your own.
Even the Rats were silent now, leather jackets and concert t-shirts forgotten as they listened to someone they'd written off as a rich kid admit that being rich didn't mean being free, that having everything didn't mean having choices, that sometimes the kids who seemed to have it all were carrying weight that was invisible but crushing.
"When this song ends, I'll go back to being what you expect," Jackie sang, building toward a conclusion that felt inevitable and heartbreaking, "The classical kid, the prep school prince, the symbol of purchased excellence... But for these three minutes, I'm just seventeen, I'm just scared, I'm just hoping someone hears the music instead of calculating the cost..."
He finished to silence that lasted long enough for everyone to understand they'd witnessed something that mattered. Then the applause started—not polite appreciation, but genuine recognition. The Rampers applauding someone who'd transcended their expectations. The Rats acknowledging courage they hadn't expected to see. The Regs and Rollers responding to honesty that cut across all artificial boundaries.
But Jackie wasn't finished. He'd risked everything with vulnerability; now he was going to risk everything with joy.
Crown Jewels rejoined him for "We Are the Champions," but not the triumphant anthem everyone expected. Instead, they played it like a question, like a hope, like kids who understood that winning and being champions might be different things entirely.
"We are the champions, my friends," Jackie sang, but his voice carried doubt instead of certainty, question marks instead of exclamation points, "And we'll keep on fighting till the end..."
The crowd erupted.
