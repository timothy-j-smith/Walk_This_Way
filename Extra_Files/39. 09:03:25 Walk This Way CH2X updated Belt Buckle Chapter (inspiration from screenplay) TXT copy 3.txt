Chapter 2X: The Belt Buckles That Started Everything (V2)
"But first," I say, reaching for the cart I've been saving, "I need to tell you about two belt buckles and how they almost changed the world."
September 1975. Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh, both thirteen, both starting eighth grade at Ephraim Curtis Middle School—named after Ephraim Curtis, born in Sudbury in 1642, son of a carpenter who'd migrated from England during the Great Puritan Migration. Like his father, he would become a carpenter, but also a trading post owner, a military officer during King Philip's War, a scout and negotiator who understood that survival sometimes required building bridges between communities that preferred to remain separate.
But Curtis Middle School in 1975 wasn't concerned with historical bridge-building. It was concerned with cafeteria politics, social geography, and the eternal middle school question of which table you belonged at during lunch period.
The cafeteria buzzed with typical institutional chaos that September afternoon. Brown bag lunches from mothers who worked at home, cafeteria trays from families who qualified for subsidized meals, individual milk cartons that kids mixed with Jello like science experiments nobody wanted to grade. But cutting through the ordinary acoustic confusion of twelve and thirteen-year-old social navigation came something that would define the next four years of American music: disco.
"Dancing Queen" poured from someone's transistor radio, the kind of synthesized Euro-pop that made rock and roll feel endangered, that suggested American music was surrendering to production values over authentic expression. Most kids accepted it as background noise, the soundtrack to their surrender to social pressures that required choosing sides, choosing tables, choosing identities that would follow them through high school and beyond.
At a corner table, Jackie Marsh sat alone, clearly the new kid trying to figure out where he fit in geography that made no sense to someone whose family had just moved from Boston proper to suburban Massachusetts. He was smaller than he'd become but already carried himself with the careful precision that comes from understanding that every movement was being evaluated by kids who'd known each other since kindergarten.
He proudly wore a brown leather belt with a gold script "QUEEN" buckle as if it were an Olympic Gold Medal—a declaration of independence, a statement of musical allegiance that suggested he'd already chosen sides in cultural wars most thirteen-year-olds didn't know existed.
Nick Johnson entered the cafeteria scanning for a place to sit, wiry and already showing the restless energy that would define his relationship with authority, his approach to music, his eventual understanding that authenticity couldn't be taught but could be recognized when it appeared. His black leather belt sported a chrome buckle with "AEROSMITH" in gothic letters—not fashion statement but musical manifesto, proof that working-class kids could claim rock royalty that spoke their language.
The sun streaming through cafeteria windows caught Nick's buckle like a spotlight, and for a brief instant the reflection blinded Jackie. He covered his eyes with his forearm until the metal stopped broadcasting light across institutional linoleum. As Nick walked closer, searching for empty space at tables that seemed designed to exclude rather than welcome, their eyes met.
Not because they recognized each other—they were strangers navigating social territory neither fully understood. They met because they both noticed each other's belt buckles at exactly the same moment, both registering musical allegiances that suggested they might understand each other despite coming from different quadrants, different economic realities, different assumptions about what constituted normal family life.
Nick approached with confidence masking nervousness, the social calculation that required working-class kids to project strength even when uncertain about reception.
"Queen?" he said, pointing at Jackie's buckle with the directness that comes from understanding that subtlety was luxury his background couldn't afford. "Seriously?"
"What's wrong with Queen?" Jackie replied, defensive but curious, his voice carrying genuine interest rather than automatic hostility, suggesting he'd been waiting for someone to engage with his musical choices rather than dismiss them as pretentious foreign nonsense.
"Nothing wrong," Nick continued, grinning while displaying his own buckle with the pride that comes from representing something larger than individual preference. "Just... Aerosmith's better."
Jackie stood, energy building like electrical charge before thunderstorm, understanding that this conversation mattered more than typical cafeteria small talk. "Better? Have you heard 'Bohemian Rhapsody'?"
"Have you heard 'Dream On'?" Nick matched his energy, both boys recognizing that they'd stumbled into genuine debate rather than social posturing, that musical preference was revealing something more significant than taste.
They stared at each other while "Le Freak" blasted from someone's radio, the disco rhythm providing ironic soundtrack to confrontation that had nothing to do with synthesized dance music and everything to do with authentic expression versus manufactured entertainment.
"Can we agree that disco sucks?" Jackie said, gesturing toward the radio with disgust that transcended their Aerosmith-versus-Queen disagreement, that suggested common ground might exist beneath surface opposition.
"Oh my God, finally someone who gets it!" Nick replied, immediate bonding replacing musical competition. "It's like... music without soul. Music designed by committees who never understood why rock and roll mattered in the first place."
"Exactly!" Jackie's excitement carried the relief that comes from finding someone who understood that music was more than entertainment, more than background noise, more than social soundtrack. "It's everything that's wrong with American culture—artificial, manufactured, designed to make people dance rather than think."
And in that moment of shared disgust for disco's commercial manipulation, two boys from different worlds discovered they might have more in common than their belt buckles suggested. Not just musical preferences, but musical values. Not just favorite bands, but understanding that rock and roll represented something worth defending against cultural forces that prioritized sales over authenticity.
I take a sip of water, understanding that some stories require proper hydration to tell accurately, that the throat needs care when carrying voices across decades.
"December 1975," I continue, "their friendship had moved beyond cafeteria debates to garage sessions that proved Queen's precision and Aerosmith's raw power weren't musical opposites but complementary forces that could create something neither band had achieved alone."
They'd spend afternoons in Dan Johnson's converted garage, trying to crack the code of what made great rock and roll transcendent rather than merely entertaining. Taking Queen songs and making them more American, taking Aerosmith songs and making them more sophisticated, finding the place where British theatricality met Massachusetts authenticity.
The middle school announced its annual talent show: May 15th, 1976. The same competition where Karen Bowers had won first place for three consecutive years with flawless renditions of Broadway standards that showcased technical proficiency while revealing nothing about the person performing them.
"But Nick and Jackie had other ideas," I tell the darkness beyond Studio C, cleaning another record with reverent attention because some stories require proper ceremony. "They'd been working on something. A secret project that would prove their argument was actually harmony disguised as disagreement."
They chose "Under Pressure"—the David Bowie and Queen collaboration that had just been released—and reimagined it as acoustic proof that different approaches to the same human longing could enhance rather than threaten each other. They practiced in secret, after official garage sessions, when Dr. Marsh thought Jackie was at the library and Grace Johnson assumed Nick was doing homework.
May 15th, 1976. The night everything changed.
Two thirteen-year-old boys took the stage with acoustic guitars and voices that hadn't finished changing, performing music that proved working-class authenticity and classical training could collaborate rather than compete. For three minutes and twenty-one seconds, they provided proof that Queen and Aerosmith weren't opposites but different aspects of the same musical truth: that authentic expression transcends economic background, that harmony emerges when different voices decide to support rather than overshadow each other.
The recording that survived—captured by the school's basic audio equipment that documented events for posterity—became the most important music never commercially released. Three minutes and twenty-one seconds of what American rock and roll could have become if friendship had proved stronger than social machinery designed to keep different kinds of people separate.
"June 1976," I continue, reaching for my bourbon because the story gets more complex from here, "their talent show victory earned them something that would change everything: a week at Interlochen Music Camp in Michigan. Not the full summer program that rich kids could afford, but a scholarship week that recognized exceptional talent regardless of economic background."
That demo tape sits in my personal collection now, waiting for the right moment. Not yet. First, Boston needs to understand what connected them, what pulled them apart, why their story matters more than the rock and roll history books that never recorded their names.
But that recording—that three minutes and twenty-one seconds when Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh proved that chrome Aerosmith and gold Queen weren't enemies but different voices in the same song—that's the proof that everything I'm about to tell you actually mattered, actually happened, actually represented possibility rather than just teenage dreams that died when real life demanded choosing sides.
The belt buckles that started everything: working-class authenticity meeting privileged rebellion, the beginning of a friendship that should have changed music history but instead became casualty of American class warfare disguised as high school competition.
