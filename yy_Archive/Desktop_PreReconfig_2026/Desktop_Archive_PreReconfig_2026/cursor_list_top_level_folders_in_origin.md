# CHAPTER 26: THE BATTLE BEGINS

May 15th, 1979. Seven o'clock sharp and the Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School auditorium smelled like old wood, floor polish, and the particular kind of anxiety that comes when eight hundred people cram into a space meant for six. The Rampers took the front rows—jocks and student council, kids who'd never missed a meal or a guidance counselor meeting. The Rats claimed the wings and back corners, leather jackets and boots up on seats, making their own rules. The Regs filled the middle sections, studying their programs like there'd be a quiz later. The Rollers stayed in the back rows, grease still under their fingernails from after-school shop class, trading stories that wouldn't make it home to parents until summer.

These weren't tribes. They were just kids trying on identities, most of them temporary, some of them permanent. A kid might wear a letterman jacket on Friday night and a leather jacket on Saturday night and nobody thought twice about it. That's how high school worked—you had more freedom to be inconsistent than you'd ever get again in life.

Nick Johnson stood in the narrow space behind the stage curtain, watching the crowd through the gap where the velvet didn't quite meet the floor. His guitar hung from his shoulder by a strap Dan had bought him three years ago, back when three years felt like a lifetime. The strap was worn smooth where Nick's thumb rested when he wasn't playing, the way a piece of leather gets soft and dark after years of being touched.

"Dream On" came out of the PA system between bands—Aerosmith, a song Nick had played a thousand times in Dan's garage. He closed his eyes and saw his father's hands on the guitar, showing him the solo that started simple and built to something that felt like flying. That was eighteen months ago, before November 14th, 1977, before the cable snapped and four steel I-beams came down on a construction trailer in Back Bay.

The song ended and Nick opened his eyes. The crowd was clapping, waiting for the next band. Walking the Line was fifth on the program, which meant three more bands before they went on. Three more chances for something to go wrong, three more minutes of his stomach trying to climb up his throat.

"What are you thinking about?"

Stephen Murphy stood next to him, bass guitar already slung and ready. Stephen had been Nick's friend since third grade, back when friendship was just about who you sat with at lunch. He'd been there the night Grace came home from the hospital and told Nick his father was gone. Nick shook his head. "Nothing. Just trying to remember the set list."

He was thinking about Grace, sitting somewhere in that crowd, wearing her best dress, which was still just her work dress from Friendly's, only she'd ironed it. He was thinking about how easy it had been, before Dan died, to just play music because music was fun. What if he stood up there with Dan's solo running through his fingers and all anyone saw was a working-class kid trying too hard? What if the grief that had turned into obsession, that had made him practice until his fingers bled, was just noise to everyone else?

He'd written papers on adolescent development, on the psychology of achievement, on the relationship between parental expectations and student performance. "You ready?" Grace asked. She was trying to smile, but Nick could see the worry in her eyes. She worried that he was disappearing into the music, that he wasn't sleeping enough, that he was too thin, that he didn't talk to anyone except his band.

"Ready enough," Nick said.

Grace reached out and touched his arm, just briefly, the way she did when she wasn't sure what to say. "Your father would be proud of you. You know that, right?"

Nick nodded. But he wasn't sure. "One minute," someone called from the stage area.

Nick hugged his mother quickly, the way he did when he was in a hurry to get somewhere else. "I'll see you after."

"I'll be right here," Grace said. "I'll always be right here."

In a few minutes, he'd be standing on that stage with Dan's solo running through his head and eighteen months of grief trying to find its way out through his fingers.

He'd play "Dream On" because that was the song Dan had taught him. He'd play it the way his father had shown him, building from simple to something that felt like flying. And maybe, if he got it right, someone in that crowd would hear what he was trying to say—that grief wasn't just something you got over, it was something you carried, and sometimes the only way to carry it was to turn it into music.

But that was the thing about grief—you couldn't control what other people heard. You could only play the notes and hope.

"Walking the Line!" someone called from the stage. "You're up!"

Nick looked at Stephen, at Mark, at David. They were just kids, really. But they were his kids, his band, and for the next fifteen minutes, they were all he had.

He took a deep breath and walked toward the curtain, toward the stage, toward whatever happened next.

Jackie would go on after Walking the Line. He'd play perfectly, because that's what he did. He'd make his father proud and his mother smile and everyone in the crowd would see exactly who Jackie Marsh was supposed to be.

But the person he was supposed to be wasn't the person he was, and that was the problem. That had always been the problem.

The crowd roared as Walking the Line took the stage. Jackie pressed his fingers against the piano keys and tried to remember what music had felt like when it was just about the music, when it wasn't about expectations or fathers or proving anything to anyone.

But that had been a long time ago. Before high school. Before everything got complicated.

Before he'd needed Nick for all the wrong reasons.

The Rollers were scattered, like they'd forgotten there was supposed to be a system.

Bob Walker from Strawberries Records and Tapes, whose thick arms rested atop a clipboard like he was selecting the draft. And Chuck Morrison, WBCN's morning show MC, tall and frayed at the edges, exactly the sort of local star who could turn a town's ordinary into something worth missing your curfew for.

"Good evening, Lincoln-Sudbury!" Chuck boomed into the battered mic, sending feedback twining around the rafters. "Welcome to the first ever WBCN Battle of the Bands! Sponsored by WBCN, the Rock of Boston, and Strawberries Records and Tapes, the place to get your albums, cassettes, eight-tracks—and if you're lucky, immortality."

Walking the Line—Nick Johnson's crew, Aerosmith loud and full of challenge. Crown Jewels—Jackie Marsh's outfit, Queen covers built from both sweat and inheritance. Rumours—Patty Lally's Concord-Carlisle dreamers, conjuring Fleetwood harmonies. The Regulators—Framingham's Zep-worshippers, promising to break the windows with volume.

The Jesters opened the night with "Just What I Needed." It was careful, earnest, almost sweet—a reminder of late summer swings and Friday night sleepovers, but nothing to rearrange your understanding of the universe. Detroit Rock City stomped in next, everything sprayed silver, platform heels wobbling, blood packets spent by the second verse. There were mishaps: fire-breathing that mostly steamed, cake makeup drooping into the eyes. Still the crowd cheered, partly for the spectacle, partly in hope their own embarrassing attempts at cool weren't quite so obvious.

The Dead Heads rolled slow and warm through "Truckin'," crowd hypnotized at first, then gradually fidgeting as minutes stretched into what felt like months. His guitar hung from a strap his father had bought him three years earlier. The strap was worn smooth where Nick's thumb rested when he wasn't playing. A piece of leather gets soft and dark after years of being touched.

A random song came out of the PA speakers between acts—something Nick didn't recognize. He closed his eyes and heard his father's voice saying *Don't rush the notes, let them breathe*. That was eighteen months ago, before the cable snapped and four steel I-beams came down. Before Nick stopped being a kid who played guitar for fun and started being a kid who played guitar because it was the only thing that made the silence bearable.

The song ended. Nick opened his eyes. One more band before Walking the Line went on.

Through the curtain gap, he saw his mother in the third row. Grace looked small and tired and proud all at once, like she'd been carrying something heavy for a long time and tonight was the first night she might get to put it down.

Dr. Richard Marsh sat three rows behind her. Jackie's father. He taught psychology at Boston University, which meant he wrote papers about why people did what they did. He'd written papers about adolescent development, about the psychology of achievement, about the relationship between parental expectations and student performance.

He'd never written a paper about his own son, but Jackie suspected he'd thought about it.

Across the backstage area, separated by extension cords and amplifiers, Jackie Marsh stood at the school's Steinway piano, running his fingers across the keys without making any sound. The Crown Jewels would go on sixth, after Walking the Line. Jackie had time to run through the parts that still felt uncertain. Not the notes themselves—those were perfect. He'd played "Bohemian Rhapsody" so many times he could do it with his eyes closed. The uncertainty was in the space between the notes, the places where he was supposed to feel something and instead felt nothing at all.

Elizabeth tuned her violin. Ace Callahan spun his drumsticks between his fingers. Max Wesslen adjusted the bass amp. The Crown Jewels. Four kids with professional equipment and the kind of technical precision that comes from expensive lessons.

"Jackie."

Dr. Marsh stood in the doorway. "Everything all right?"

"Fine," Jackie said. "Just nervous."

"Good. Nerves mean you're taking it seriously." Dr. Marsh adjusted his tie. "Remember, this isn't just about music. It's about showing people who you are."

Jackie nodded. That was the problem. He wasn't sure who he was when he wasn't trying to be who everyone else needed him to be.

The guitar had been Nick's thing. The piano was supposed to be Jackie's thing. But somewhere along the way, the piano had stopped being about what Jackie wanted and started being about what Jackie thought everyone else wanted. Classical earned his father's approval. Rock earned his breath. He couldn't have both.

"You'll do fine," Dr. Marsh said. "You always do."

Jackie watched his father walk back toward the auditorium. The Crown Jewels would play Queen songs, and everyone would see exactly who Jackie Marsh was supposed to be—the kid with perfect technique, the kid who made difficult things look easy.

But the person he was supposed to be wasn't the person he was, and that was the problem. That had always been the problem.

Backstage, Nick heard the crowd react to whatever band was playing now. One more act before Walking the Line. In a few minutes, he'd be standing on that stage with Dan's solo running through his head and eighteen months of grief trying to find its way out through his fingers.

The guitar had become an obsession after Dan died. Nick practiced in the garage late into the evening, sometimes past midnight, until his mother came out in her bathrobe and asked him to stop. The neighbors might complain. Grace was tired. But Nick kept playing because it was the only thing that made the silence bearable, the only thing that made him feel connected to his father in a way that words couldn't touch.

"Walking the Line!" someone called from the stage. "You're up!"

Nick looked at Stephen, at Mark, at David. They were just kids. But they were his kids, his band, and for the next fifteen minutes, they were all he had.

He took a deep breath and walked toward the curtain, toward the stage, toward whatever happened next.

Behind him, Jackie watched Nick disappear through the curtain. The Crown Jewels would go on after Walking the Line. Jackie would play perfectly, because that's what he did. He'd make his father proud and his mother smile and everyone in the crowd would see exactly who Jackie Marsh was supposed to be.

But the person he was supposed to be wasn't the person he was, and that was the problem. That had always been the problem.

The crowd roared as Walking the Line took the stage. Jackie pressed his fingers against the piano keys and tried to remember what music had felt like when it was just about the music, when it wasn't about expectations or fathers or proving anything to anyone.

But that had been a long time ago. Before high school. Before everything got complicated.

Before he'd needed Nick for all the wrong reasons.
