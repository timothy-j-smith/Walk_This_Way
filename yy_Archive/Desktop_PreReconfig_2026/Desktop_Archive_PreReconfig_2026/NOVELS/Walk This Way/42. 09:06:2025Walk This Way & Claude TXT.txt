WALK THIS WAY
On October 15, 2024, Maxanne Sartori, legendary DJ who discovered Aerosmith and launched Boston rock, prepares for WBCN's final broadcast before the station dies at midnight. At 73, she has four hours to tell one last story—not about the bands who made it, but about two boys whose musical partnership could have changed everything if America hadn't perfected the art of division.
The story unfolds in two timelines. In 2024, Maxanne broadcasts from WBCN's dying booth, weaving between on-air monologues and private revelations, counting down to midnight when the station will flip to Christian radio. In 1979, we witness the story she's telling: Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh, once inseparable friends in middle school, now seniors at Lincoln-Sudbury High, divided by Hop Brook—the stream that separates Sudbury's working class from its upper-middle elite.
Nick lives with his widowed mother Grace, who works double shifts at the hospital since his father died in a construction accident. His garage band, Walking the Line, practices among his dead father's tools, making authentic rock from grief and class rage. Jackie, son of a Harvard Medical professor and a Wellesley poet, has been trained for excellence since birth. His band, Crown Jewels, practices in a basement worth more than Nick's house, performing Queen covers with classical precision but no soul.
The novel's first act establishes Sudbury's geography of division: four quadrants separated by Hop Brook and the town dump (with its adjacent Cavicchio pig farm), by railroad lines that cross but don't connect, by parents who preach tolerance while practicing segregation. Through flashbacks, we learn Nick and Jackie once harmonized perfectly at a 1974 music camp with Patty Lally from Concord, before the world explained why working-class authenticity and upper-class education couldn't mix.
When Lincoln-Sudbury announces its Battle of the Bands for March 15, 1979, what begins as a school competition becomes a class war. WBCN's coverage, led by Maxanne herself, transforms it into a regional event. Eight bands enter, each representing different aspects of Sudbury's divisions: The Jesters (working-class Lincoln kids), Cherry Bomb (all-girl punk challenging male rock supremacy), The Establishment (rich kids' sterile precision), Dead Heads (stoner transcendence), Crown Jewels, Walking the Line, and unexpectedly, Rumours—Patty Lally's Concord band that refuses to choose sides.
The battle itself occupies the novel's entire second act, with each band receiving a full chapter for their performance. The evening progresses from segregated seating to gradual mixing as music temporarily dissolves boundaries. Jackie's technical perfection cracks when he breaks into Elton John's "Your Song," revealing the emotion his parents tried to train out of him. Patty's original songs explicitly name the shared past: "Three kids found a frequency in 1974 / Before the world explained what harmony was for." The parking lot aftermath reveals all divisions immediately reasserting themselves, what Maxanne calls "both revolutionary and completely futile."

The novel's examination of how art fails to transcend class barriers despite our myths about its democratizing power connects to Colson Whitehead's "Sag Harbor" and Tom Perrotta's "The Leftovers".
Pulitzer Potential
The novel has several elements that appeal to Pulitzer committees: exploration of American class divisions through an unexpected lens, innovative structure using radio broadcast as narrative frame, and deep regionalism that speaks to universal themes. The music-as-democracy metaphor and examination of how the 1970s' promise failed could resonate with contemporary concerns about inequality.
However, Pulitzer committees typically favor either experimental literary fiction (like "The Netanyahus" by Joshua Cohen) or sweeping social novels (like "The Overstory" by Richard Powers). "Walk This Way" would need exceptional prose execution and perhaps a more ambitious scope to compete. The ending's insistence that "frequencies persist" might be seen as too hopeful for current literary tastes that favor bleaker views of American division.
The novel's strength lies in its specific regional detail and unique narrative voice. With the expanded performance chapters and deeper exploration of the aftermath, it could achieve the scope needed for major recognition.
