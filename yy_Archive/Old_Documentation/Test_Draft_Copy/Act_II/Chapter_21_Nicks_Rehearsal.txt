CHAPTER 21: NICK'S REHEARSAL
**Third Person - May 8, 1979 (Day Before the Battle)**
**Word Count Target:** 1,200-1,500 words

---

The garage on Dutton Road smelled like old concrete and machine grease—the kind that soaks into floors and never leaves. Dan Johnson's tools hung on the pegboard, organized with the precision of a man who'd made do with what he had.

Nick stood in the center of the space, surrounded by his father's memory and the weight of what tomorrow meant. Stephen Murphy, David Cassel, and Mark Pianka were there—the Lincoln boys, his band. Walking the Line. They'd been practicing for four hours straight.

"What if we're not good enough?" Mark asked during a break, adjusting his borrowed keyboard.

Nick picked up his guitar. The $25 acoustic Dan had bought him three years ago, worn smooth by practice. "We are."

"But what if we lose?" Stephen pressed.

Nick didn't answer. He'd been asking himself the same question every night since his mother's speech at Friendly's. Kids stopping him in hallways, telling him they were rooting for him. Parents asking Grace how he was doing. Like he was carrying something bigger than his own dreams.

And maybe he was.

"Let's run 'Dream On' again," Nick said.

---

The opening riff filled the garage. Raw. Powerful.

Stephen's bass locked in. David's drums provided the heartbeat. Mark's keyboard added texture. Together, they created something more than the sum of borrowed equipment and work-worn hands.

Nick had always loved "Dream On." The way it built from quiet desperation to full-throated defiance. Tyler's raw vocals speaking to something deep he couldn't name.

But tonight it felt different. Heavier.

When they finished, Stephen nodded. "That's the best we've ever played it."

"'Sweet Emotion,'" Nick said.

The iconic bass line. Stephen locked into it perfectly, fingers finding the groove. They worked through the set: "Dream On," "Sweet Emotion," "Walk This Way," "Toys in the Attic."

Each song carrying weight now. Not just notes and chords.

"Walk This Way" was their closer. The song that gave everything its name. Simple guitar line building into something unstoppable.

When they finished, the garage was silent except for breathing.

"That's what we've got," Nick said.

Stephen set down his bass. "Nick. Whatever happens tomorrow—we're with you. Not because of your mom's speech or the town or any of that shit. Because you're the reason we started playing in the first place."

Mark nodded. "Freshman year, you were the only kid who didn't laugh when I showed up with my grandmother's old Casio. You said, 'Can you play it?' Not 'Is that a real keyboard?'"

David twirled a drumstick. "You taught me how to hold the sticks properly. Spent three lunches in a row just showing me the grip. You didn't have to do that."

"We're not here because you're carrying some working-class flag," Stephen said. "We're here because you made us believe we could actually be a band. So tomorrow? We play for that. Screw everything else."

Nick looked at the three of them. The Lincoln boys. His brothers in borrowed equipment and split-knuckled determination. "Let's run it one more time."

They did.

---

Outside, Grace stood in the driveway, listening to her son's music drift through the garage walls.

She'd never meant for it to happen this way. The speech at Friendly's had turned into something she hadn't anticipated. She was surprised how many people in town had heard about the Battle of the Bands, how many knew about Nick, how many had opinions about which way it should go.

But what surprised her more was how much Nick had changed since Dan died.

Dan had taught Nick the guitar—that had been their bond, that connection words couldn't describe. But after the accident, the playing had become something else entirely. Grace had encouraged it. What else could she do? She couldn't bring Dan back. She couldn't fix what was broken. But she could let Nick play.

Except somewhere along the way, the playing had become something else. An obsession. A mission. The garage wasn't just where Nick practiced anymore—it was where he disappeared for hours, chasing something she couldn't see, reaching for something she couldn't name.

She was afraid of what would happen if he lost tomorrow. Would it destroy him the way Dan's death had almost destroyed her? Would he see it as proof that the gift Dan had seen wasn't real?

But she was also afraid of what would happen if he won. Would music take him away from her too? Would he chase that dream until it killed him, just like construction had killed Dan?

Grace pushed away from the house and walked back inside. She'd made her choice when she'd stood up to Dr. Taub. There was no turning back now.

---

Nick practiced until the Lincoln boys finally went home. He heard their car pull away, heard the silence settle.

A few minutes later, the side door opened. Grace stood there, still in her Friendly's uniform.

"You should be asleep," Nick said.

"So should you." She came in, leaned against the workbench. "It sounded like your father was in here with you. All of you."

"Mom—"

"Your father would be proud, you know."

Nick looked down at his guitar. "What if I'm not good enough?"

Grace was quiet for a moment. Outside, a car passed on Dutton Road. Inside, the garage smelled like machine oil and memory—the kind that gets into your clothes and won't wash out, no matter how many times you try.

"You already are," she said. "You've been good enough since the day you picked up that guitar. Tomorrow's not about proving anything to anyone. It's about showing them what you already know."

She kissed the top of his head. "Don't stay up too late."

After she left, Nick sat on the workbench, guitar across his lap. The wood warm. The strings familiar.

This was where he'd learned to play. Where Dan had taught him the "Dream On" solo one Sunday afternoon, both of them hunched over the guitar, Dan's rough hands guiding his until he got it right.

"You got the gift, Nicky," Dan had said. "Don't waste it."

Nick looked at the Craftsman toolbox in the corner, latches rusted shut. Three years and Grace still couldn't bring herself to open it. Three years of her picking up double shifts whenever they needed the money. Three years of him in this garage, pretending the guitar was just a hobby and not the last thread connecting him to his father.

Tomorrow wasn't just about beating Jackie Marsh.

Nick knew what he was really afraid of. Not losing to Jackie. Not the town's disappointment. Not even proving Dr. Taub right about "realistic expectations."

He was afraid of letting her down. Of proving that every risk she'd taken—standing up at that PTA meeting, confronting Dr. Taub at Friendly's, putting herself on the line—had been for nothing.

When he thought about it, he felt something tighten in his chest. Not quite panic. Not quite dread. Something heavier, like carrying a weight you can't put down but aren't sure you can hold.

What if she was wrong? What if tomorrow proved that the world worked exactly the way Dr. Taub said it did?

Nick pressed his palm against the guitar's body, feeling the wood vibrate under his hand.

He wasn't playing for Dan's memory anymore. He was playing for the woman who'd stood up to the world and said her son mattered.

Nick ran through "Dream On" one more time. Alone now. Just him and the ghost of his father's voice saying *you got the gift.*

The Battle was eighteen hours away.
