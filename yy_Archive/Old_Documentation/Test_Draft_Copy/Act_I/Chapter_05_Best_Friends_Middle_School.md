CHAPTER 5: BEST FRIENDS (MIDDLE SCHOOL)

The friendship that began with belt buckles in September 1974 would deepen over the next year in ways that neither Jackie Marsh nor Nick Johnson could have predicted. They were twelve years old when they met, thirteen when they became inseparable, and by the time they reached eighth grade, they'd discovered something that most adults had forgotten: that music could be a bridge instead of a barrier, that friendship could cross any divide, that sometimes the most important battles were fought not with weapons but with songs.

But before the magic happened, before the garage sessions that would change everything, there were the small moments that built a friendship brick by brick, like kids constructing a fort in the woods, not knowing they were building something that would last forever.

It started with cassette tapes. Jackie would bring Queen albums recorded from his father's stereo system—bootleg copies of "A Night at the Opera" and "News of the World" that he'd dubbed onto TDK cassettes, the kind that cost more than Nick's family spent on rent in a month. Nick would bring Aerosmith tapes borrowed from older kids, handwritten lyrics scrawled on notebook paper in ballpoint pen, the words to "Dream On" and "Sweet Emotion" copied down during study hall when he was supposed to be learning algebra.

They'd meet in the school library during lunch, headphones split between them like a secret handshake, listening to music that made the other kids look at them funny. The librarian, Mrs. Stephens, would shush them constantly, but she never kicked them out. Maybe she understood that some things were more important than quiet.

"Listen to this harmony," Jackie would say, rewinding "Bohemian Rhapsody" for the hundredth time. "Freddie Mercury is doing things with his voice that shouldn't be possible."

"Maybe," Nick would counter, "but listen to Steven Tyler's scream in 'Dream On.' That's not just singing—that's bleeding your soul into a microphone."

They'd argue for hours, but it wasn't the kind of arguing that tears people apart. It was the kind that builds bridges, the kind that says "I care enough about this to fight for it, and I care enough about you to listen when you fight back."

By spring of 1975, their friendship had expanded beyond music. They discovered the conservation land that ran behind both their neighborhoods—hundreds of acres of pine and oak where middle school kids could escape adult supervision and build their own world.

They'd ride their bikes through trails that wound deeper into the woods than either of them had ever gone alone. Jackie's bike was a ten-speed Raleigh that his father had bought him for his birthday, shiny and new and completely wrong for the mud and rocks of the conservation trails. Nick's bike was a hand-me-down Schwinn that someone had left at The Dump as a reclamation project, painted black with silver spray paint, the chain always threatening to fall off but somehow holding together through every adventure.

They'd find swimming holes that weren't on any map, places where the water was cold and clear and deep enough to dive into without hitting bottom. They'd climb trees that seemed to touch the sky, sitting in branches that swayed with their weight, looking down at a world that seemed smaller and more manageable from above. The boys could sit in comfortable silence, just two boys who'd found each other in the chaos of middle school, building a friendship that didn't need words to exist.

The first time Jackie came to dinner at Nick's house, Grace Johnson was suspicious. Not hostile, but cautious, like someone who'd learned that good things usually came with hidden costs. She'd heard about the Marsh family from other mothers at the diner where she worked—the professor who taught at Boston University, the house on the good side of Hop Brook, the kind of people who didn't usually socialize with families who counted coupons and prayed the car would start.

But Jackie showed up with a genuine smile and an appetite that made Grace's heart soften. He ate peanut butter sandwiches with the same enthusiasm he'd shown for her meatloaf, and when he helped clear the dishes without being asked, Grace realized he understood something that most kids his age didn't: that helping out was just what you did.

"He's a good kid," Grace told Nick later, after Jackie had gone home. "Different from the other boys around here, but different in a good way."

The first time Nick visited Jackie's house, he was intimidated by everything—the size of the rooms, the books on the shelves, the way Dr. Marsh spoke in complete sentences without using contractions. But Mrs. Marsh welcomed him with the same warmth she showed her own children, and when she asked about his music, Nick found himself talking about Aerosmith with the same passion he'd always felt, but now with someone who actually listened.

"He's got real talent," Mrs. Marsh told Jackie after Nick had gone home. "There's something genuine about him, something that can't be taught."

The test came in October, when the leaves were turning and the air smelled like wood smoke and possibility. Doug Malone was the kind of kid who made up for academic failure with physical intimidation and the kind of cruelty that middle school students perfected through daily practice.

He found Jackie alone in the hallway between third and fourth periods, cornered near the lockers where the fluorescent lights buzzed overhead and the linoleum floor reflected the institutional green of the walls. Doug Malone had been watching Jackie for weeks, waiting for the right moment to enforce the social hierarchy that middle school required.

"Hey, rich boy," Doug said, his voice carrying the particular menace that only thirteen-year-old boys could muster. "You lost? Your people hang out by the windows."

Jackie looked up, and for a moment, he considered avoiding any conflict, but knew from experience that this only delayed the inevitable, and he'd learned that middle school hierarchies required public enforcement. If he didn't face things now, Doug Malone would just find him later, and the humiliation would be worse.

"I'm not lost," Jackie said, his voice steady despite the way his hands felt moist.

"Sure looks like it to me," Doug said, moving closer. "You got a professor for a daddy and a house with a name. What are you doing hanging around here?"

Before Jackie could answer, Nick appeared beside him, stepping into the confrontation with the kind of casual confidence that comes from having already lost everything that mattered.

"Problem here?" Nick asked, his voice carrying across the hallway.

Doug Malone looked at Nick, sizing him up. Nick was smaller than Doug, wiry where Doug was solid, but there was something in Nick's eyes that suggested he'd been in fights before and wasn't afraid to be in another one.

"No problem for you, Johnson," Doug said, assuming working-class solidarity. "This is between me and the rich kid."

"Seems like a problem for me if you're bothering my friend," Nick said, and the word "friend" hung in the air like a challenge.

"Your friend?" Doug laughed, but it was the kind of laugh that didn't reach his eyes. "Since when do you hang out with kids from the good side of town?"

"Since always," Nick said, stepping closer to Jackie. "And people who mess with my friends have problems with me."

The hallway had gone quiet, other kids stopping to watch the confrontation. Doug Malone looked from Nick to Jackie and back again, trying to understand what he was seeing. Two kids from different worlds, standing together against him, united by something he couldn't understand.

"Whatever," Doug said finally, backing down. "Come on, guys. Not worth it."

He walked away, but the moment hung in the air like smoke after a fire. Three boys had negotiated the terms of social survival and the meaning of genuine friendship, and everyone who'd been watching knew that something important had just happened.

After Doug Malone left, Jackie and Nick stood in the empty hallway, the silence between them filled with everything that didn't need to be said.

"You didn't have to do that," Jackie said finally.

"Yeah, I did," Nick replied, and they both knew it was true.

They walked to their next class together, two boys who'd just discovered that friendship wasn't just about sharing music or riding bikes or eating dinner at each other's houses. It was about standing up for each other when the world tried to tear them apart, about being willing to fight battles that weren't your own because the person you were fighting for mattered more than the consequences.

They were best friends in the way that only thirteen-year-old boys could be—completely committed to each other's success, willing to defend each other against social pressure, the kind of friendship that made other kids jealous and adults nostalgic for their own lost innocence.

That weekend, they met in Nick's garage, the same space where Dan Johnson's tools still hung on pegboards, waiting for hands that would never come back to use them. The garage was cluttered but welcoming, filled with the kind of organized chaos that suggested someone had once cared about keeping things in their proper place.

They sat on milk crates, guitars in hand, the "versus" debate having evolved into something deeper. Nick strummed the opening to "Sweet Emotion," and the garage seemed to pulse with the rhythm, the concrete floor vibrating with the bass notes.

"That's half-decent," Jackie said, impressed despite himself. "Check out what I've been working on."

He played the piano part to "Bohemian Rhapsody" on his guitar, and the sound bounced off the walls and ceiling, filling the space.

"Holy shit, Jackie," Nick said, awed. "That's like... fucking operatic."

"Language, Nicholas," Jackie said, but he was smiling. "But thank you."

Nick looked at his guitar, then at Jackie, then back at his guitar. "You know what? We should start a band."

Jackie laughed. "What would we call it? 'Queen Smith'?"

"Aero Queen," Nick said, deadpan.

They both burst into laughter, the sound echoing in the garage and creating natural harmonies that neither of them had planned.

"What if we did both?" Jackie asked, picking up Nick's rhythm. "Like... what if we took the best of both worlds?"

"Raw power meets technical precision?" Nick said, joining Jackie's melody.

They began to play together—Nick's "Sweet Emotion" riff under Jackie's "Bohemian Rhapsody" melody. It shouldn't have worked, but it did. The sound filled the garage, tools vibrating on their hooks, dust motes swirling in the sunlight streaming through the windows.

They sang together, their voices blending:

"Sweet emotion... is this the real life..."

The music filled the garage, bigger than the two boys creating it.

When they finished, they were both breathing hard, sweat beading on their foreheads despite the October chill.

"We could do this," Jackie said, his voice filled with wonder.

"We could change everything," Nick replied, and they both knew it was true.

This was the golden time, before the world told them they couldn't be friends, before class differences became more important than musical harmony, before the Battle of the Bands would turn their friendship into a battlefield. This was the moment when two boys from different sides of Hop Brook discovered that music could be a bridge instead of a barrier, that friendship could cross any divide.

And it all started with a belt buckle catching the light.
