# CHAPTER 24: PATTY RETURNS - THE THIRD VOICE

---

May 9th, 1979, and Patty Lally was sitting in St. Michael's Church in Concord, Massachusetts, trying to figure out why the music that had once brought her so much joy now felt like a burden she wasn't sure she wanted to carry.

The church was quiet except for the sound of her voice and Robin Bosshard's guitar, working through Fleetwood Mac's "Don't Stop Thinking About Tomorrow" with the kind of careful precision that came from musicians who knew they were good but weren't entirely sure what they were good for.

Patty was seventeen, a senior at Concord-Carlisle High School, and she was supposed to be figuring out her future. But the future felt like a question she didn't know how to answer, a destination she wasn't sure she wanted to reach.

"Music shouldn't be about war," she said, stopping in the middle of the song, her voice carrying the kind of frustration that came from watching something beautiful get turned into something ugly.

Robin looked up from his guitar, his fingers still moving through the chord progression, his expression one of confusion and concern. "What do you mean?"

"I mean this whole Battle of the Bands thing," Patty said, gesturing toward the window that looked out over Concord's Main Street. "I heard about it from some kids at school. Lincoln-Sudbury Regional is having this huge competition, and it's turned into this whole class warfare thing. Working-class kids versus privileged kids, authentic music versus technical excellence. It's like they've forgotten that music is supposed to bring people together, not tear them apart."

## **THE CONCORD PERSPECTIVE**

Concord was different from Sudbury. It was smaller, more insular, the kind of town where everyone knew everyone else's business and nobody pretended they didn't. It was the kind of place where the Battle of the Bands would have been just that—a battle of the bands, not a referendum on class and privilege and the American dream.

But Patty had heard about what was happening in Sudbury, and it bothered her. Not because she had a stake in the outcome, but because she understood what it meant when music became a weapon instead of a bridge.

"Remember that summer camp?" she said, looking at Robin with the kind of intensity that came from remembering something important. "Camp Winaukee, the summer of '75? Those two boys from Sudbury—Nick and Jackie? They were so good together. They had this thing, this connection, this way of making music that felt like magic."

Robin nodded, his fingers still moving through the chord progression, his expression one of recognition. "Yeah, I remember. They won the talent show that year, didn't they?"

"They did," Patty said, her voice carrying the kind of sadness that came from watching something beautiful die. "And now they're enemies. Now they're on opposite sides of some stupid competition that's turned into a war. And I keep thinking—what happened? What went wrong? How do you go from making music together to hating each other?"

## **THE SUMMER CAMP MEMORY**

Patty had been thinking about that summer a lot lately. The three of them—Nick, Jackie, and Patty—had been inseparable for that one week at Camp Winaukee. They'd practiced together, performed together, dreamed together about what their musical futures might hold.

Nick had been the raw talent, the kid who could make a guitar sing with emotions he couldn't put into words. Jackie had been the technical perfectionist, the kid who could arrange harmonies that made your heart stop. And Patty had been the bridge between them, the kid who could find the melody that connected their different approaches to music.

Together, they'd created something that was more than the sum of its parts. They'd written songs, arranged covers, discovered that music could be a language that transcended the differences that divided other people.

But that had been four years ago. They were seventeen now, seniors in high school, and the future that had seemed so full of possibilities had narrowed into a series of choices that felt more like obligations than opportunities.

"I wrote them letters," Patty said, her voice carrying the kind of honesty that came from admitting something you'd been trying not to think about. "After camp, I wrote them both letters. Told them about my band, asked them about theirs, tried to keep the connection alive. But they never wrote back. Not to me, not to each other. It's like that summer never happened."

Robin was quiet for a moment, his fingers still moving through the chord progression, his expression one of understanding. "Maybe they're just busy," he said finally. "Senior year, college applications, all that stuff."

"Maybe," Patty said. "But I don't think that's it. I think something happened between them, something that made them forget how good they were together. And now they're enemies, and I keep thinking—what if I could help them remember? What if I could remind them that music isn't supposed to be about winning or losing? It's supposed to be about connection."

## **THE DECISION**

The idea had been forming in Patty's mind for days, ever since she'd first heard about the Battle of the Bands. It was crazy, probably impossible, definitely inadvisable. But it was also the kind of thing that felt right in a way that logic couldn't explain.

"What if we entered?" she said, looking at Robin with the kind of intensity that came from having made a decision you weren't sure you could carry out. "What if Rumours entered the Battle of the Bands?"

Robin's fingers stopped moving, his expression one of surprise and confusion. "Patty, we're from Concord. We don't go to Lincoln-Sudbury. We can't enter their competition."

"I know," Patty said. "But what if we could? What if we found a way? What if we showed up and reminded them that music isn't supposed to be about class warfare or technical superiority or any of that stuff? What if we reminded them that music is supposed to be about connection, about bringing people together, about finding the melody that connects different voices?"

Robin was quiet for a long moment, his guitar resting against his chest, his expression one of someone trying to process an idea that was equal parts brilliant and insane.

"Patty," he said finally, "that's crazy. Even if we could find a way to enter, even if we could get permission, what would we do? We'd be outsiders in a competition that's already divided along lines we don't understand. We'd be walking into a war zone with nothing but a guitar and a dream."

"Exactly," Patty said, her voice carrying the kind of conviction that came from understanding something important. "We'd be walking into a war zone with nothing but a guitar and a dream. And maybe that's exactly what they need. Maybe they need someone to remind them that music isn't supposed to be about winning or losing. It's supposed to be about connection."

"We can do this," she said, her voice carrying the kind of conviction that came from understanding something important. "We can find a way to enter. We can remind them that music isn't supposed to be about war."

Robin was quiet for a long moment, his expression one of someone trying to process an idea that was equal parts brilliant and insane.

"Patty," he said finally, "are you sure about this? Because once we enter, there's no going back. We'll be part of this whole thing, whatever it turns into."

"I'm sure," Patty said, her voice carrying the kind of conviction that came from understanding something important. "I think it's the right thing to do. I think they need someone to remind them that music is supposed to be about connection, not competition."

## **THE FINAL PREPARATION**

The Battle of the Bands was twenty-four hours away, and Patty Lally was about to do something that was equal parts brilliant and insane. She was going to enter a competition she had no business entering, in a town she didn't live in, to remind two boys she hadn't seen in four years that music was supposed to bring people together, not tear them apart.

It was crazy, probably impossible, definitely inadvisable. But it was also the kind of thing that felt right in a way that logic couldn't explain.

"Music shouldn't be about war," she said again, looking out the window of St. Michael's Church at the quiet streets of Concord, Massachusetts. "It should be about connection. And maybe someone needs to remind them of that."

Robin nodded, his fingers moving through the chord progression of "Don't Stop Thinking About Tomorrow," his expression one of someone who'd decided to follow a dream that was equal parts brilliant and insane.

"Maybe," he said finally. "Maybe someone does."
