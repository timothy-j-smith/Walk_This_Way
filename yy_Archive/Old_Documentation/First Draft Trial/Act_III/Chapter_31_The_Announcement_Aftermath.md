## CHAPTER 31: THE ANNOUNCEMENT & AFTERMATH

### **The Judges' Deliberation**

As Walking the Line left the stage, the judges huddled around their score sheets, trying to make sense of what they'd just witnessed. This wasn't supposed to happen—a high school talent show wasn't supposed to transform an entire community, wasn't supposed to remind everyone of what music was supposed to be about.

Maxanne Sartori looked at her score sheet, at the numbers she'd written down, at the technical ratings she'd assigned to each performance. But the numbers felt meaningless now. How do you score a transformation? How do you rate a reminder? How do you judge something that transcends competition?

Carter Alan was doodling on his score sheet, drawing little musical notes that spiraled into infinity. He'd been listening to the future of rock and roll for years, but he'd never heard anything like this. This wasn't just music—this was something else entirely.

Bob Walker from Strawberries Records was checking his watch, but not because he had somewhere better to be. He was checking his watch because he was trying to figure out how to explain what he'd just witnessed to his colleagues, how to describe something that defied description.

Chuck Morrison was pacing behind the judges' table, his cocaine-thin frame vibrating with energy that had nothing to do with stimulants and everything to do with witnessing something historic.

"This is impossible," Maxanne said finally, looking up from her score sheet. "How do you judge something like this?"

### **The Impossible Decision**

The judges looked at each other, understanding that they were about to make a decision that would define not just the Battle of the Bands, but the entire community. They'd come here to judge music, but they'd ended up judging something much more important.

"How do you choose between authenticity and excellence?" Carter Alan asked, his voice carrying the particular confusion that comes from trying to apply old rules to new situations.

"How do you choose between technical skill and emotional truth?" Bob Walker added, looking at his score sheet like it was written in a language he didn't understand.

"How do you choose between what's perfect and what's real?" Chuck Morrison asked, pacing faster now, his energy building to something that felt like revelation.

Maxanne looked at her score sheet, at the numbers that felt meaningless now, at the technical ratings that couldn't capture what they'd just witnessed. And then she made a decision that would change everything.

"We don't choose," she said quietly. "We acknowledge."

### **The Announcement**

Chuck Morrison took the microphone and walked to the center of the stage, his cocaine-thin frame vibrating with energy that had nothing to do with stimulants and everything to do with witnessing something historic.

"Ladies and gentlemen," he announced, his voice carrying the authority of someone who'd spent years perfecting the art of commanding attention, "after careful deliberation, our distinguished panel of judges has reached a decision."

The auditorium was silent, waiting to hear who had won the Battle of the Bands, who had proven that their approach to music was superior, who had earned the right to represent the future of rock and roll in suburban Massachusetts.

"But first," Chuck continued, "I want to acknowledge something that happened here tonight. Something that none of us expected, but all of us needed to see."

He paused, looking out at the audience, at the kids who'd stopped competing and started connecting, at the parents who'd stopped judging and started understanding, at the community that had stopped dividing and started harmonizing.

"Tonight, we witnessed something that transcended competition. We witnessed something that reminded us of what music is supposed to be about—connection, not competition. We witnessed something that proved that the divisions we thought were real might not be as real as we thought."

The audience was silent, understanding that they were about to hear something important, something that mattered more than winning or losing.

"And so," Chuck continued, "our distinguished panel of judges has decided that the winner of the first annual WBCN Battle of the Bands is..."

He paused, milking the suspense, but this time it felt different. This time, the suspense wasn't about who would win, but about what the announcement would mean.

"...Rumours!"

The auditorium erupted in applause, but it wasn't about Patty's victory. It was about the truth she'd spoken, the reminder she'd given them, the possibility she'd shown them.

### **The Aftermath**

As Patty walked onto the stage to accept her prize, something began to happen in the auditorium. The social boundaries that had defined Lincoln-Sudbury for years began to dissolve completely. Kids from the northeast section started talking to kids from the southwest section. The divisions that had separated them—quarters and comics, advantages and disadvantages, privilege and struggle—began to feel artificial, temporary, like costumes they'd been wearing without realizing it.

Grace Johnson stood up and walked over to Dr. Marsh, who was sitting in the front row with Elizabeth and Ace. They'd never spoken before, but now they were talking about their sons, about the music they'd just heard, about the possibility that maybe the divisions weren't as real as they'd thought.

Sarah Morrison, who'd been sitting in the northeast section with the other kids from the wrong side of Hop Brook, stood up and walked over to Tommy Morrison, who'd been sitting in the southwest section with the other kids from the right side of Hop Brook. They were cousins, but they'd never talked at school because they'd been assigned to different sections, different worlds, different versions of reality.

Lisa Henderson, who'd been sitting in the northwest section with the other kids who didn't know which side they were supposed to be on, stood up and walked over to Danny Kowalski, who'd been sitting in the southeast section with the other kids who were trying to figure out where they belonged. They'd never met before, but now they were talking about music, about the possibility that maybe the divisions weren't as real as they'd thought.

The auditorium was transforming. The Battle of the Bands was becoming something else entirely—a reminder of what was possible when people stopped competing and started connecting.

### **The Recognition**

As the applause died down, Nick and Jackie looked at each other across the auditorium. They'd both lost the Battle of the Bands, but they'd both won something much more important. They'd both been reminded of what music was supposed to be about—connection, not competition. They'd both been reminded of what they'd once had, what they'd lost, what might still be possible.

"She remembers," Nick whispered, looking at Patty, who was standing on stage with her prize, looking out at the audience with the kind of understanding that only comes from someone who'd been there when it all began.

"She remembers," Jackie whispered back, looking at Nick, understanding that they'd both been wrong about what the other represented, that they'd both been wrong about what the divisions meant, that they'd both been wrong about what was possible.

The frequency still existed. They just had to be brave enough to find it.
