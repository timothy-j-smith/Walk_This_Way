# CHAPTER 26: EVE OF THE BATTLE

---

May 10th, 1979. The night before the Battle of the Bands, and Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School hummed with the kind of electricity that made teachers reach for their coffee and administrators check their insurance policies.

The parking lot told the story better than any words could: on one side, cars with "Aerosmith" spray-painted on windows in blue and white. On the other, neat "Queen" bumper stickers that looked like they'd been applied with a ruler. Some brave souls had tried to remain neutral, but neutrality had become its own statement—and neutrality, it turned out, was no longer an option.

Inside the school, three separate rehearsals were happening simultaneously, each one representing a different approach to the same impossible question: what does it mean to make music that matters?

## **AUTO SHOP - BAY THREE**

Nick Johnson stood in the center of the auto shop, surrounded by the smell of motor oil and the sound of four kids who'd learned to make do with what they had and make it work. The shop teacher, Carl Dukowski, was a Vietnam vet who understood what it meant to need noise to drown out other noise. He'd cleared bay three, the one with the best acoustics, and told them to make it count.

Walking the Line had gathered their army: Stephen Murphy on bass, looking like a young John Entwistle if Entwistle had grown up poor in Massachusetts. David Cassel behind a drum kit they'd cobbled together from three different sets, none matching but all thunderous. Mark Pianka on keyboards, the quiet kid who let his fingers do the screaming. And Nick, center stage, channeling every moment of working-class rage into six strings and a voice that could strip paint.

They ran through their set list one final time:

"Walk This Way" - their anthem, their declaration that raw power could beat polished precision every time.

"Dream On" - slowed down, turned into something that sounded like a prayer for kids who'd stopped believing in miracles but still believed in each other.

"Sweet Emotion" - sped up, aggressive, less seduction and more accusation against a world that had already decided their fate.

"Train Kept A-Rollin'" - their secret weapon, nine minutes of pure sonic assault that would either bring the house down or bring the house down on them.

Nick stood in bay three, surrounded by his brothers-in-arms, and felt the weight of representing everyone who'd ever been told they weren't good enough. Every kid who'd watched their parents struggle, every family that knew the exact price of milk because every penny mattered, every dream deferred because rent came first—they were all counting on him to prove that authentic meant more than educated.

"The thing about Nick's version of Aerosmith," Carl Dukowski would later tell anyone who'd listen, "was that he understood something most cover bands miss. He wasn't trying to be Steven Tyler. He was trying to tell Steven Tyler's story through his own scars."

## **AUDITORIUM**

While Nick's band turned the auto shop into a temple of noise, Jackie Marsh had transformed the school auditorium into something else entirely. His band—"Crown Jewels," because subtlety had died somewhere between the bulletin board and the bridge—had the advantage of proper acoustics and actual music stands and the kind of equipment that most high schools couldn't afford.

His recruited ensemble read like a who's who of suburban achievement: Elizabeth Marsh on violin, Jackie's thirteen-year-old sister who played with the precision of someone who'd been taking lessons since she could hold a bow. Arthur "Ace" Callahan on drums, the freshman soccer star who approached rhythm like athletics—all discipline and explosive power. Max Wesslen on bass, the gangly kid who'd gotten good because his parents believed in lessons even when it meant skipping vacations.

And Jackie himself, moving between piano and guitar and vocals like a maestro conducting his own fever dream, his voice trained to find notes that didn't exist in nature.

Their set list was ambitious to the point of arrogance:

"Bohemian Rhapsody" - all six minutes, every movement, arranged for their instruments with the kind of precision that made other bands sound like they were playing with oven mitts.

"Somebody to Love" - gospel influences intact but somehow made whiter, more suburban, less dangerous.

"We Will Rock You" - reimagined as symphony, every note perfectly placed, every harmony mathematically precise.

"We Are the Champions" - their closer, their declaration of war against anyone who thought that working-class authenticity was the only kind that mattered.

But here's what the gathering crowds didn't know, what even the most dedicated supporters couldn't see: both bands were scared shitless.

Jackie sat at the Steinway they'd moved into the auditorium, surrounded by kids who'd never wondered if the lights would stay on, and felt the weight of representing everyone who'd been told they were only good enough because of their advantages. Every kid who'd been accused of buying their talent, every achievement dismissed as privilege, every genuine emotion reduced to performance—they were all counting on him to prove that excellence could exist alongside soul.

## **ST. MICHAEL'S CHURCH - CONCORD**

While Nick and Jackie prepared for war, Patty Lally sat in St. Michael's Church in Concord, practicing with Robin Bosshard and trying to figure out how to make the impossible possible.

Rumours had been working on Fleetwood Mac covers, "Dreams" and "Go Your Own Way," but Patty kept thinking about something else. Something that had nothing to do with competition and everything to do with connection.

"Music shouldn't be about war," she said to Robin as they packed up their instruments.

Robin looked at her, confused. "What do you mean?"

"I mean this whole thing—the Battle of the Bands, the way everyone's chosen sides, the way Nick and Jackie have become symbols instead of people." Patty shook her head. "They used to make music together. They used to create something beautiful. Now they're just performing other people's expectations."

Robin nodded slowly. "So what are we going to do?"

Patty smiled, and there was something in that smile that reminded Robin of why they'd decided to enter this competition in the first place. "We're going to remind them what music is supposed to be about. We're going to show them that harmony doesn't have to be a battle. Sometimes it's just two voices finding the same frequency."

## **MR. WENTWORTH'S OFFICE**

In his office, Mr. Wentworth sat at his desk, surrounded by sheet music and lesson plans and the weight of knowing that he'd started something he couldn't control. The Battle of the Bands had become bigger than anyone had anticipated, bigger than a simple competition, bigger than the school itself.

He'd wanted to drive up interest in music, to fight against the new computers that were showing up in high schools for the first time. He'd wanted to prove that music still mattered, that it could still bring people together instead of tearing them apart.

But looking at the way the school had divided itself, at the way two former best friends had become symbols of everything that was wrong with America, he wondered if he'd made a mistake.

"Maybe I should cancel it," he said to the empty room.

But he knew he couldn't. The poster was up, the bands were ready, the town was invested. The Battle of the Bands would happen tomorrow night, and there was nothing anyone could do to stop it.

## **GRACE JOHNSON'S KITCHEN**

Grace Johnson sat at her kitchen table, drinking coffee and trying not to think about tomorrow night. Nick had been practicing in Dan's garage for weeks, working until his hands ached, until his voice grew hoarse, until she'd had to go outside and listen from the driveway because the sound was too much to bear.

She was proud of him, of course she was proud of him. But she was also worried. Worried about the weight he was carrying, worried about the way this competition had become about more than just music, worried about what would happen when it was over.

"Dan would have loved this," she said to the empty kitchen. "He would have been right there in the front row, cheering louder than anyone else."

But Dan wasn't there. Dan was gone, and Nick was trying to fill shoes that were too big for anyone to fill, and Grace was trying to be strong for both of them.

## **DR. MARSH'S STUDY**

Across town, Dr. Marsh sat in his study, surrounded by medical journals and the weight of knowing that his son was about to perform in front of the entire town. Jackie had been practicing for weeks, perfecting every note, every harmony, every moment of technical excellence.

But Dr. Marsh had heard something in Jackie's voice lately, something that worried him. Not the sound of someone who was confident in their abilities, but the sound of someone who was trying to prove something they weren't sure they believed in.

"Maybe I pushed him too hard," he said to Elizabeth, who was sitting across from him, her violin case at her feet.

Elizabeth looked at her father, and there was something in her eyes that reminded him of Jackie when he was younger, before the pressure of expectations had started to weigh him down.

"Maybe," she said. "But maybe he needs to figure this out for himself. Maybe he needs to remember why he loves music in the first place."

Dr. Marsh nodded slowly. "And what if he doesn't remember?"

Elizabeth smiled, and there was something in that smile that gave him hope. "Then maybe someone else will remind him."

## **THE PARKING LOT**

As the rehearsals wound down and the school emptied out, three separate cars pulled out of the parking lot, each one carrying a different kind of hope, a different kind of fear, a different kind of dream.

Nick's car, an old Chevy that had seen better days, headed toward the northeast neighborhoods, carrying the weight of representing everyone who'd ever been told they weren't good enough.

Jackie's car, a newer model that his parents had bought him for his sixteenth birthday, headed toward the southwest neighborhoods, carrying the weight of representing everyone who'd been told they were only good enough because of their advantages.

And Patty's car, a borrowed station wagon from her parents, headed toward Concord, carrying the weight of trying to remind everyone that music was supposed to bring people together, not tear them apart.

Tomorrow night, they would all return to this same parking lot. Tomorrow night, they would all walk into the same auditorium. Tomorrow night, they would all discover whether music could still do what it was supposed to do—heal what politics had broken, bridge what class had divided, bring people together instead of tearing them apart.

But tonight, they were just three kids who loved music, trying to figure out how to make sense of a world that had turned their passion into a weapon in a war they'd never asked to fight.

The Battle of the Bands was tomorrow night. And the fate of two former best friends hung in the balance of songs that were never supposed to be anything more than the sound of two voices finding the same frequency.
