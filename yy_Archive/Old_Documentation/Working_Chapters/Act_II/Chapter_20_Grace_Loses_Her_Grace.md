# CHAPTER 20: GRACE LOSES HER GRACE - THE FRIENDLY'S CONFRONTATION

---

May 1st, 1979, and Grace Johnson had been working at Friendly's for twenty-three years. Twenty-three years of polyester uniforms that made everyone look like they were melting under fluorescent lights, of name tags that reduced her identity to first name and corporate cheerfulness, of aprons that had absorbed two decades of other people's messes and other people's problems and other people's opinions about what was realistic for other people's children to hope for.

She'd started there in 1956, when she was nineteen and newly married to Dan Johnson, when the idea of working in a restaurant had seemed like a temporary solution to a temporary problem. But temporary had a way of becoming permanent, and problems had a way of multiplying, and here she was, forty-two years old, still wearing the same uniform, still serving the same kinds of people, still listening to the same kinds of conversations about what was realistic for other people's children to hope for.

The difference was, now she had a son who was about to graduate from high school. Now she had a son who had won the middle school talent show and was about to compete in the Battle of the Bands. Now she had a son whose future seemed to hang in the balance of other people's opinions about what was realistic for other people's children to hope for.

And she was tired of it.

## **THE CONVERSATION**

It started, as these things often do, with a simple question. Dr. Patterson was sitting in booth twelve, nursing a cup of coffee and reading the Sudbury Weekly, which had been running front-page stories about the Battle of the Bands for the past two weeks. Dr. Patterson was one of those customers who liked to make conversation with the waitstaff, who seemed to think that his medical degree gave him insight into every aspect of human experience, including the proper way to serve coffee and the proper way to raise children.

"Grace," he said, looking up from his newspaper, "I've been reading about this Battle of the Bands situation. Quite the controversy, isn't it?"

Grace was refilling his coffee cup, a task she'd performed approximately ten thousand times in her career at Friendly's. "Yes, Dr. Patterson, it's been quite the topic of conversation."

"I have to say," Dr. Patterson continued, "I'm concerned about the way this competition is dividing our community. It seems to be bringing out the worst in people, don't you think?"

Grace set the coffee pot down on the table, careful not to spill any on the newspaper. "How do you mean, Dr. Patterson?"

"Well, it's become so political, hasn't it? All this talk about class and privilege and what's 'authentic' versus what's 'trained.' It's as if people are using this competition to air grievances that have nothing to do with music."

Grace nodded, but something in her expression must have changed, because Dr. Patterson leaned forward, his voice taking on the tone he probably used when he was explaining a diagnosis to a patient who didn't understand why they couldn't just take a pill and make everything better.

"I mean, take your son, for example. Nick, isn't it? I've heard he's quite talented. But this competition—it's setting up unrealistic expectations, don't you think? These kids are going to be disappointed when they realize that talent alone isn't enough to succeed in the music industry."

Grace's hand tightened around the coffee pot handle. "Dr. Patterson, I'm not sure I understand what you're getting at."

"I'm just saying that it's important to be realistic about these things. Your son is a bright boy, Grace, but the music industry is very competitive. It takes more than talent to succeed—it takes connections, it takes resources, it takes the kind of training that costs money. I'm just concerned that this competition is giving these kids false hope."

Grace set the coffee pot down on the table with a thunk that was just a little too loud. "Dr. Patterson, are you suggesting that my son shouldn't enter this competition because he doesn't have the right kind of training?"

"No, no, of course not. I'm just saying that it's important to be realistic about expectations. Your son has talent, but talent alone isn't enough. These competitions can be very discouraging for kids who don't have the proper background."

"Proper background," Grace repeated, and her voice had taken on a quality that Dr. Patterson had never heard before—not in twenty-three years of eating at Friendly's, not in all the times he'd watched Grace serve him coffee and pie and listen to his opinions about everything from the weather to the Red Sox to the proper way to raise children.

"Dr. Patterson," Grace said, and now her voice was steady and clear and completely unlike the voice she usually used when talking to customers, "my son has been playing guitar since he was eight years old. He's been practicing in our garage every day for the past four years. He's won every talent show he's ever entered. He's been invited to perform at the Battle of the Bands by WBCN, one of the most respected radio stations in Boston. And you're telling me that he doesn't have the 'proper background'?"

Dr. Patterson shifted in his seat, suddenly aware that the conversation had taken a turn he hadn't anticipated. "Grace, I didn't mean to suggest—"

"What exactly are you suggesting, Dr. Patterson? Are you suggesting that my son shouldn't enter this competition because he's not from the right kind of family? Because he doesn't have the right kind of education? Because he doesn't have the right kind of connections?"

"Grace, I think you're misunderstanding—"

"No, Dr. Patterson, I think I understand perfectly. You're suggesting that my son shouldn't enter this competition because he's working class. Because he's the son of a waitress and a construction worker who died in an accident. Because he doesn't have the advantages that other kids have."

Dr. Patterson's face was turning red, and he was looking around the restaurant as if hoping someone would come to his rescue. But the other customers were all listening now, and none of them seemed inclined to interrupt.

"Grace, I really think you're overreacting—"

"Overreacting?" Grace's voice was still steady, but now it had an edge that could have cut glass. "Dr. Patterson, you've been coming to this restaurant for twenty-three years. You've watched me serve you coffee and pie and listen to your opinions about everything from the weather to the Red Sox to the proper way to raise children. And in all that time, you've never once asked me about my son's dreams. You've never once asked me about his talent. You've never once asked me about his future. But now that he's about to enter a competition that might give him a chance to pursue those dreams, you're suddenly concerned about 'realistic expectations'?"

Dr. Patterson was looking at his coffee cup as if it might contain the answer to how he'd gotten himself into this situation. "Grace, I was just trying to be helpful—"

"Helpful? Dr. Patterson, you're not being helpful. You're being condescending. You're telling me that my son shouldn't enter this competition because he doesn't have the 'proper background,' and you're calling that helpful?"

"Grace, I really think you're misinterpreting what I said—"

"No, Dr. Patterson, I'm not misinterpreting anything. You're telling me that my son shouldn't enter this competition because he's working class. You're telling me that talent alone isn't enough, that he needs connections and resources and the kind of training that costs money. You're telling me that this competition is giving him false hope. And you're calling that helpful?"

Dr. Patterson was looking around the restaurant again, but this time he was looking for an escape route. "Grace, I think we should end this conversation—"

"No, Dr. Patterson, I think we should finish this conversation. You've been telling me for twenty-three years that I should be realistic about my son's future. You've been telling me that working-class kids shouldn't have big dreams. You've been telling me that talent alone isn't enough. And I've been listening, Dr. Patterson. I've been listening for twenty-three years. But you know what? My son has talent. Real talent. And he's worked hard to develop that talent. And he deserves a chance to see where that talent can take him. And if you don't think that's realistic, then maybe you should think about what you're really saying."

Grace straightened her uniform—the polyester that made everyone look like they were melting under fluorescent lights, the name tag that reduced her identity to first name and corporate cheerfulness, the apron that had absorbed twenty-three years of other people's messes and other people's problems and other people's opinions about what was realistic for other people's children to hope for.

"Enjoy your pie, Dr. Patterson. It's made from apples grown in this town, served by people who live in this town, paid for by families who've spent generations making your comfort possible. Maybe think about that the next time you're discussing what's 'realistic' for other people's children to hope for."

She walked away then, not to the kitchen or the counter or anywhere that would suggest she was running from what she'd said. She walked to the next table—booth seven, where a young couple was sharing dessert—and asked if they needed anything else, her voice back to professional courtesy as if nothing had happened, as if she hadn't just demolished the polite fiction that had allowed Friendly's to function as neutral territory in a town divided by every kind of boundary except honesty.

## **THE AFTERMATH**

The manager came out of the office eventually. Tom Andrews, fifteen years managing Friendly's, never seen anything like it. He took Grace into the back room, had the conversation that managers have with employees who've violated the cardinal rule of customer service: never make the customers uncomfortable with your humanity.

But Tom Andrews had grown up in Sudbury too. Had worked construction with Dan Johnson, had been to the funeral, had watched Grace carry herself with dignity through circumstances that would have broken most people. He gave her a lecture about professionalism and public relations and the importance of maintaining appropriate boundaries between staff and customers.

Then he gave her a raise.

"Fifteen cents an hour," Grace told me that June afternoon, laughing the way people laugh when the irony is too perfect to be tragic. "Fifteen cents for finally saying what everyone in this town knows but pretends they don't. For proving that the help has opinions, feelings, children whose futures matter as much as anyone else's."

The story spread through Sudbury like spilled coffee, staining everything it touched. By Monday, everyone had heard some version of what had happened at Friendly's, though the details varied depending on who was telling it and what they needed it to mean.

Some people said Grace had been rude to a customer. Some people said Dr. Patterson had been condescending to a waitress. Some people said it was a misunderstanding that had gotten out of hand. Some people said it was about time someone stood up to the kind of attitude that made working-class people feel like they didn't belong in their own town.

But everyone agreed on one thing: Grace Johnson had finally lost her grace. And in losing it, she'd found something else—something that had been there all along, buried under twenty-three years of polyester uniforms and professional courtesy and the kind of patience that comes from knowing that your job depends on your ability to make other people comfortable with their privilege.

She'd found her voice. And once you find your voice, you can't lose it again.
