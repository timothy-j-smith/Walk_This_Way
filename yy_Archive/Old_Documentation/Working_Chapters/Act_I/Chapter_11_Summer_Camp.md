## CHAPTER 11: SUMMER CAMP

The bus to Camp Winnipesaukee smelled like diesel fuel and adolescent anticipation, a combination that made Nick Johnson's stomach flutter with the kind of nervous excitement that comes from stepping into the unknown. He sat next to Jackie Marsh, both of them clutching their acoustic guitars like talismans against whatever social hierarchies awaited them in the New Hampshire woods.

"Think anyone else will know about the talent show?" Nick asked, his voice carrying the particular uncertainty that comes from being thirteen and not quite sure where you fit in the world.

Jackie shrugged, but Nick could see the same nervousness in his friend's eyes. "Probably not. It's not like we're famous or anything."

The bus wound through mountain roads that seemed to climb away from everything familiar—away from Sudbury's invisible class lines, away from the expectations that came with last names and addresses, away from the subtle pressures that had begun to shape their friendship into something more complicated than it had been in the garage.

Camp Winnipesaukee looked exactly like the brochures had promised: rustic cabins that smelled like pine sap and possibility, a dining hall where food fights were democracy in action, and most importantly for their story, a music building where kids who understood that music mattered more than fitting in could find their tribe.

The camp director, a wiry man named Mr. Freedman who looked like he'd been teaching music since before rock and roll was invented, greeted them with the kind of enthusiasm that suggested he'd seen hundreds of kids arrive with guitars and dreams, and he still believed every one of them mattered.

"Welcome to Cabin 7," he said, leading them toward a wooden structure that looked like it had been built by someone who understood that summer magic required four walls and a roof, but not much else. "Music intensive. You'll be sharing with six other kids who think music is more important than sleep."

The cabin smelled like wood shavings and teenage boy—a combination that would have been overwhelming if it hadn't been for the sense of possibility that seemed to hang in the air like morning mist. Nick and Jackie claimed bunks near each other, their guitars resting against the wall like old friends who'd traveled a long way to be here.

That first evening, as the sun set over the lake and the sounds of other kids settling into their own cabins drifted through the open windows, Nick found himself talking about things he'd never discussed with anyone outside his family.

"My dad used to say that music was the one thing that didn't care where you came from," he said, his voice carrying the particular weight that comes from speaking about someone who's gone. "He'd come home from work covered in sawdust and sweat, and he'd pick up his guitar and suddenly he was anywhere he wanted to be."

Jackie was quiet for a moment, and Nick could see him processing this information about Dan Johnson, about the kind of man who could find freedom in six strings and a few chords.

"My dad says music is a luxury," Jackie finally said, and there was something in his voice that suggested he'd been thinking about this for a while. "He says it's fine as a hobby, but real success comes from understanding economics, from knowing how the world actually works."

Nick looked at his friend, really looked at him, and for the first time he began to understand that Jackie's world was different from his own in ways that went beyond the obvious differences in their houses and their parents' jobs.

"What do you think?" Nick asked.

Jackie was quiet for so long that Nick thought he might not answer. Then: "I think your dad was right. I think music doesn't care where you come from. But I think the world does."

The conversation hung in the air between them like smoke from a campfire, and Nick felt something shift in their friendship—not a crack, not yet, but the recognition that there were forces at work in their lives that they were only beginning to understand.

The next morning, they met Patty Lally.

She arrived during breakfast, a last-minute addition when someone from Vermont had to cancel due to a family emergency. Fourteen years old, wheat-colored hair that caught sunlight like it was storing it for later use, and a voice that made everyone else sound like they were trying too hard.

She wasn't the prettiest girl at camp, but she was the most magnetic. There was something in the way she moved through the world like she belonged wherever she happened to be standing, like confidence was her natural state and self-doubt was a foreign language she'd never bothered to learn.

"Hi," she said, sliding into the seat across from Nick and Jackie with the kind of ease that suggested she'd been eating breakfast with strangers her whole life. "I'm Patty. I play piano and I sing, and I'm here because my grandmother left me some money with the instruction to use it for 'something beautiful.'"

Nick and Jackie exchanged glances, and Nick felt something flutter in his chest that he couldn't quite name.

"I'm Nick," he said. "I play guitar."

"Jackie," Jackie added. "Guitar and piano."

Patty smiled, and Nick felt like the sun had just come out from behind a cloud. "Perfect. I've been looking for people to make music with. The other kids here seem more interested in showing off than actually creating something together."

Over the next few days, the three of them became inseparable. They spent their mornings in the music building, working on arrangements that blended Nick's raw talent with Jackie's technical skill and Patty's ability to find the frequency where both could coexist.

"Listen to this," Patty said one afternoon, sitting at the piano and playing a chord progression that seemed to capture something essential about being thirteen and not quite sure where you belonged in the world. "This is what happens when you stop worrying about being perfect and start caring about being real."

Nick picked up his guitar and found a melody that wove through Patty's chords like a conversation between old friends. Jackie added harmonies that made the whole thing sound like something that had always existed, waiting for the right three people to discover it.

"This is it," Patty said, her voice carrying the particular excitement that comes from recognizing something important. "This is what music should be. Not competition, not showing off, just... this."

They worked on Elton John's "Your Song," Patty's idea to blend Queen's harmonies with Aerosmith's emotion. Nick added the raw power that came from growing up in a house where dreams were luxuries you couldn't afford. Jackie added the technical precision that came from growing up in a house where excellence was not just expected but required. Patty added the heart that came from growing up with a grandmother who believed that beauty was worth investing in.

The result was something that transcended the sum of its parts—a performance that proved different approaches to the same emotions could enhance rather than compete with each other.

When they performed it at the camp talent night, the other kids fell silent in a way that suggested they were witnessing something they hadn't known was possible. This wasn't the kind of performance that made you want to clap politely and move on. This was the kind of performance that made you want to lean forward and listen harder, to understand what you were hearing.

Afterward, as they sat by the lake watching the stars appear one by one in the darkening sky, Patty said something that would haunt all three of them for years to come.

"The frequency is still there," she said, her voice carrying the particular wisdom that comes from understanding something important about the world. "Don't forget that. No matter what happens when we go back to our real lives, the frequency is still there."

Nick and Jackie exchanged glances, and Nick felt something tighten in his chest—the recognition that this moment, this perfect harmony, couldn't last forever.

"What do you mean?" Jackie asked.

Patty was quiet for a moment, and Nick could see her choosing her words carefully. "I mean that what we found here—this way of making music together, this way of being friends—it's real. But the world we're going back to, it's not set up for this kind of thing. It's set up to separate people, to put them in categories, to make them compete instead of collaborate."

Nick felt a chill that had nothing to do with the evening air. "So what do we do?"

Patty smiled, but there was something sad in her eyes. "We remember. We remember that this is possible. And maybe, if we're lucky, we find ways to make it possible in the real world too."

The last night of camp, they made promises that felt both sacred and impossible. They would stay in touch, they would form a band together, they would change the world with their music. They exchanged addresses and phone numbers, and for a moment it seemed like nothing could break the bond they'd formed.

But as Nick lay in his bunk that final night, listening to the sounds of other kids packing their bags and saying their goodbyes, he felt something that he couldn't quite name—a sense that this perfect week had been a gift, but also a preview of something that might never be possible again.

The next morning, as their parents arrived to pick them up, Nick watched Jackie climb into Dr. Marsh's car—a vehicle that spoke of success and stability and a future that was already mapped out. He climbed into Grace's car—a vehicle that spoke of hard work and determination and a future that would have to be earned one day at a time.

As they drove away in different directions, Nick looked back at Camp Winnipesaukee and felt something that would become familiar over the years—the recognition that he'd just experienced something beautiful and temporary, something that couldn't survive the return to the real world.

But he also felt something else, something that would sustain him through the difficult years ahead: the memory of what was possible when three people decided to make music together instead of competing with each other.

The frequency, as Patty had called it, was still there. The question was whether it would be strong enough to survive everything that was coming.
