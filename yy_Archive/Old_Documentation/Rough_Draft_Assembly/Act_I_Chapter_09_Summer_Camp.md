**From Extra_Files/15 (lines 248-256):**

The camp looked exactly like the brochures had promised: rustic cabins that smelled like pine sap and possibility, a dining hall where food fights were democracy in action, and most importantly for our story, a music building where kids who understood that music mattered more than fitting in could find their tribe.

"The thing about summer camp," I tell my dwindling audience, "is that it's America's laboratory for social experimentation. Take kids away from their familiar hierarchies, their established roles, their predetermined futures, and see what happens when they're free to reinvent themselves for two weeks."

Nick and Jackie were assigned to Cabin 7 again, the music intensive cabin, but this time they arrived as celebrities. Word had gotten around about their talent show victory, about two kids who'd found a way to make Queen and Aerosmith sound like they belonged in the same song, the same friendship, the same world.

For the first week, they were inseparable. Best friends exploring their expanded world, discovering that there were kids from all over New England who shared their obsession, their belief that music was salvation disguised as entertainment.

"But summer camp," I continue, lighting another cigarette, "has a way of introducing variables that even the best friendships aren't prepared for."

Her name was Patty Lally, and she arrived during the second week—a last-minute addition when someone from Vermont had to cancel due to a family emergency. Fourteen years old, wheat-colored hair that caught sunlight like it was storing it for later use, and a voice that made everyone else sound like they were trying too hard.

She wasn't the prettiest girl at camp, but she was the most magnetic. There was something in the way she moved through the world like she belonged wherever she happened to be standing, like confidence was her natural state and self-doubt was a foreign language she'd never bothered to learn.

**From Extra_Files/53 (lines 461-464):**

In the summer of 1974 [CHANGE TO 1975], back when they were all twelve and thirteen, back before high school taught them who they were supposed to hate, there had been a regional summer music camp. Two weeks at a place called Camp Winnipesaukee, where kids from different towns learned that music was bigger than zip codes.

Nick had been there on scholarship—one of those programs designed to make rich donors feel good about themselves. Jackie had been there because his parents thought it would look good on future applications. Patty had been there because her grandmother had left her a small inheritance with the stipulation that it be used for "something beautiful."

"For two weeks," I tell the darkness beyond the booth, "those three kids made music together. Nick's raw talent, Jackie's technical skill, and Patty's ability to find the frequency where both could coexist. They wrote songs, performed together, made promises about staying in touch and forming a band that would change the world."

Then camp ended. They went back to their respective towns, their respective lives, their respective futures. The promises faded like summer tans.
