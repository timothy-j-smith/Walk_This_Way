## CHAPTER 19: JACKIE'S REHEARSALS

The Marsh basement had been transformed into something resembling a professional recording studio: proper acoustics, high-end equipment, microphones that cost more than most families' monthly grocery budgets. Crown Jewels rehearsed there with the precision of a military operation, every note planned, every harmony arranged, every moment choreographed to demonstrate that technical excellence and emotional truth could coexist in the same performance.

But Jackie had heard about Grace Johnson's speech too—everyone in Sudbury had heard about it—and the knowledge sat in his stomach like a stone, weighing down every song, making every perfectly executed passage sound like evidence of the very inequality his mother's outburst had challenged.

Wednesday after school, forty-eight hours before the Battle of the Bands, Jackie found himself alone in that basement, playing "Bohemian Rhapsody" on the Steinway grand, trying to find in Mercury's operatic complexity some answer to questions he didn't know how to ask.

Dr. Marsh had been increasingly present during rehearsals, offering suggestions that felt more like instructions, guidance that sounded more like management. The Battle of the Bands had become his son's audition for a future that had already been planned, a demonstration that proper preparation and family investment produced superior results.

"The key is maintaining artistic integrity while showcasing technical superiority," Dr. Marsh had said during Tuesday's practice session, his voice carrying the measured authority of someone who'd spent his career analyzing other people's work rather than creating his own. "The competition isn't really with other bands—it's with the assumption that authentic music requires authentic struggle. We're proving that excellence can be cultivated, that talent improves with proper support."

But Jackie was seventeen, not a thesis statement. And sitting alone in that perfect basement studio, playing songs that had once felt like rebellion, he couldn't shake the feeling that he was preparing to win an argument that missed the point entirely, to prove something that didn't need proving to people who wouldn't be convinced anyway.

The Steinway grand piano dominated the room, its polished surface reflecting the expensive lighting that had been installed to create the perfect recording environment. Jackie's fingers moved across the keys with the mechanical precision of someone who'd been practicing since before memory, but the music that emerged felt hollow, technically perfect but emotionally vacant.

He tried "We Are the Champions," working on the bridge section, trying to find a way to make Mercury's anthem sound earned rather than assumed. But every note felt like evidence of the very privilege that Grace Johnson had challenged in her speech at Friendly's.

The phone rang at 8:47 PM. Jackie almost didn't answer—he was deep in concentration, trying to find the emotional core of a song that had once felt like a declaration of victory—but something about the persistence of the ringing made him pick up.

"Jackie?" The voice was tentative, familiar, impossible.

"Nick?"

Silence stretched between them like the years since they'd been friends, like the geographic distance that was measured in more than miles, like everything that had made this phone call inadvisable, dangerous, potentially devastating to both their carefully constructed positions in the war that was consuming their community.

"I heard about what your mom said at Friendly's," Jackie finally continued, his voice careful, controlled, carrying none of the classical projection he'd been trained to use when speaking mattered. "About the things people were saying about you. About us. About this whole competition."

More silence. Nick could hear classical music in the background—not Queen, something older, more formal, the sound of a house where culture was furniture rather than feeling.

"It's gotten weird, hasn't it?" Nick said finally. "This whole thing. Started as a music competition, turned into... I don't even know what to call it."

"A symbol," Jackie answered immediately, like he'd been thinking about this for weeks, which he had. "We've become symbols of things we never chose to represent. You're authentic struggle, I'm privileged excellence. Two different versions of the American dream, battling it out in a high school cafeteria."

"Is that what we are?" Nick asked, and his voice carried something Jackie hadn't heard since they were thirteen: genuine curiosity about what his former best friend actually thought, rather than what he was supposed to think.

"I don't know what we are anymore," Jackie admitted. "I know what everyone else thinks we are. I know what my dad wants me to be, what your mom needs you to prove, what the whole town is expecting from Friday night. But I don't know what we actually are to each other."

The admission hung between them like a bridge neither was sure they should cross.

"Do you remember," Nick said slowly, "when we wrote that song about the railroad crossing? About trains having to stop and wait for each other to pass?"

"Diamond Crossing," Jackie said immediately. "Yeah, I remember."

"Feel like that's what we're doing now. Sitting at the same intersection, but we can't both move forward. One of us has to win, which means one of us has to lose, and either way the trains don't actually connect. They just... wait for permission to continue in different directions."

Jackie was quiet for a long moment, long enough that Nick wondered if he'd hung up, if the metaphor had been too much, if admitting uncertainty was a luxury neither of them could afford with forty-eight hours left before they had to perform their assigned roles in other people's expectations.

"What if we're both wrong?" Jackie said finally. "What if this whole competition is just... another way to make sure we stay in our designated corners? What if winning means proving we can beat each other instead of proving we can make music that matters?"

"Then we're fucked either way," Nick said, and for the first time in months, Jackie laughed—not the careful, polite sound he'd learned to make at dinner parties and faculty gatherings, but the genuine amusement of someone who recognized truth when he heard it, even when the truth was uncomfortable.

"Yeah," Jackie agreed. "We're fucked either way."

They talked for seventeen more minutes. Not about strategy or set lists or the technical aspects of performing under pressure, but about the weight of carrying other people's dreams, about the impossible mathematics of representing entire communities through three-minute songs, about the loneliness of being chosen to prove things they weren't sure they believed themselves.

When they finally hung up, Jackie sat alone in that perfect basement studio, surrounded by equipment that cost more than most families' monthly grocery budgets, and understood that Friday night would determine more than which band won a high school competition. It would decide whether two former best friends could find a way back to each other, or whether America's talent for division was stronger than their shared history of harmony.

The Battle of the Bands was twenty-four hours away, and Jackie Marsh was discovering that the hardest part of competition wasn't playing better than your opponent. It was playing well enough to justify the faith that other people had invested in your success, while trying to remember why you'd started playing music in the first place.
