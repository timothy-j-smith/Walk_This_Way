## CHAPTER 1: THE LAST VOICE OF BOSTON ROCK

Four hours. That's what I've got left.

Four hours before WBCN becomes just another frequency in the graveyard of American radio. Four hours before the suits flip the switch and turn us into automated Christian contemporary—because apparently Jesus rocks harder than Steven Tyler ever did. Four hours before fifty-six years of breaking bands, of late-night conversations with musicians who poured their souls into three-minute songs, of watching Boston transform from a city of steel and sweat into whatever the hell it's become now—four hours before all of that gets erased like a bad demo tape.

I'm Maxanne Sartori. Seventy-three years old, sitting in Studio C with a bourbon that costs more than most people's car payments, watching the ON AIR sign blink red like a dying heartbeat. The same studio where I watched Steven Tyler's hair get caught in the mixing board back in '73. The same turntables that spun the first singles from bands who went on to fill stadiums. The same microphone that's carried my voice through five decades of chaos, rebellion, and the kind of music that made parents lock their daughters' bedroom doors.

"Maxanne?" The program director's voice crackles through the intercom like a bad phone connection. Kid's twenty-eight, wears sneakers that cost more than my first car—a '67 Mustang I bought with my first WBCN paycheck—and thinks rock history started with Nirvana. "You've got four hours left. Make them count."

Make them count. As if four hours could contain what WBCN meant to a city that lived and breathed rock and roll. As if I could summarize fifty-six years in a neat little package with a bow on top. As if the story I'm about to tell could be wrapped up with a happy ending and a lesson learned.

But here's the thing about endings—they make liars of us all. You promise to go out with dignity, say something profound, make those final moments matter. And then you find yourself sitting in a radio booth with a bottle of Pappy Van Winkle that Steven Tyler himself sent when he heard about the station closing. 'For the first lady who ever gave a damn,' the note said. Even after all these years, even after becoming the biggest rock star in the world, he still remembers the kid from WBCN who believed in four guys from New Hampshire before anyone else did.

I adjust the headphones—the same Sony MDR-7506s I've worn since 1971, held together now with electrical tape and pure stubbornness. The console lights pulse expectantly like a heartbeat on life support. Outside the booth windows, Boston glitters in the October darkness like broken glass in a parking lot—all glass towers and gentrification where dive bars used to breed bands, where the Rat used to host shows that would make your grandmother blush, where kids with nothing but dreams could still believe they had a shot at changing the world.

"This is Maxanne Sartori, and for the next four hours, you're listening to the final broadcast of WBCN, 104.1 on your FM dial. The Rock of Boston. The station that launched Aerosmith when they were just kids from New Hampshire with more attitude than money. The station that gave J. Geils his first shot, that broke The Cars when they were still just Ric Ocasek's garage band, that made Boston believe it could rock as hard as anywhere else."

I take a sip of bourbon—another violation of modern radio protocol, but who's going to fire me now?—and feel the warmth spread through my chest like liquid fire. The good kind that makes you remember why you started drinking in the first place. The kind that makes you tell stories you've been carrying too long.

"But tonight isn't about the bands that made it. Tonight is about the ones that didn't. Tonight is about two kids from Sudbury, Massachusetts, who should have changed everything but got caught in the crossfire of America's class war. Tonight is about the night I watched the future of rock and roll die in a high school auditorium, when two seventeen-year-old boys who should have been allies became enemies over a question that seems quaint now: What is authentic rock and roll?"

The silence stretches across the city like a held breath. In the engineering booth, the kid they've assigned to babysit my final show looks up from his phone. Maybe something in my voice warns him this won't be the nostalgia trip he expected.

"March, 1979. Disco was dying its overdue death. The Clash were about to teach America that punk had something to say. MTV was three years away from making image matter more than sound. We were living in the last moments when two kids with nothing but talent and rage could matter as much as marketing budgets."

I spin my chair to face the wall of albums, each one a memory, a story, a moment when music mattered more than metrics. The vinyl spines catch the booth's amber light like tombstones in a graveyard of sound. My fingers find the right cart without looking—muscle memory from five decades of curating sound, my hands knowing these grooves better than my own face in the mirror.

"Nick Johnson lived in a house where the mortgage was always late and dreams were luxuries you couldn't afford. His father had died in a construction accident two years earlier, crushed beneath a load of steel beams when a crane cable snapped—the kind of industrial tragedy that makes page three of the Metro section and gets forgotten by everyone except the families who eat silence for dinner afterward. His mother worked doubles at Friendly's, came home smelling like fryer oil and other people's happiness, and never once complained about her son spending grocery money on guitar strings."

The bourbon burns like liquid fire, the good kind that makes you remember why you started drinking. I can see the kid in the engineering booth scrolling through his phone, probably checking his social media, updating his status to "babysitting some old lady's final radio show." He has no idea what a garage means to rock and roll. He grew up with GarageBand, thinking music could be manufactured in a bedroom with the right plugins. He never felt the sacred geometry of four walls, concrete floor, and kids who had nothing to lose.

"Jackie Marsh lived less than a mile away as the crow flies, but between them stretched Hop Brook—named a century ago for its hops, the old mill long gone to modernization. The brook divided Sudbury like a scar, separating the haves from the have-nots. In winter, kids from both sides might skate the same frozen pond, but when the ice melted, they returned to their proper territories. Jackie's father taught economics at Boston University, the kind of professor who wrote papers about the working class without ever living in their neighborhoods, who could analyze the economic geography of Sudbury's four quadrants without understanding that those invisible lines actually shaped real lives, real friendships, real possibilities."

I pause, letting the silence breathe. Radio silence used to terrify program directors, but I learned long ago that the spaces between words can say more than the words themselves.

"These boys should never have met. America designs its suburbs specifically to prevent these collisions—invisible lines drawn by property values, school districts, the careful architecture of keeping the classes in their proper lanes. But music has always been democracy's wild card, the wavelength that cuts through all the bullshit boundaries we build."

Through the booth window, I can see the billboard across Boylston Street advertising luxury condos where The Rat used to host bands too raw for radio. Twenty-four hundred square feet starting at 2.2 million—you could buy the entire block of Lansdowne Street for that in '77. The Ramones played that club back when Joey was so tall he had to duck under the pipes backstage, and the sound system was held together with duct tape and prayers.

"I met them both the night of March fifteenth, 1979. The Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School Battle of the Bands. I was there because the station had sponsored it, back when we could afford to care about local music. Back when Clear Channel hadn't swallowed half the FM dial and algorithms hadn't replaced intuition."

Another sip. The bourbon is smooth, expensive—a gift from Steven Tyler himself. He still remembers, even after all these years, even after becoming the biggest rock star in the world, he still remembers the kid from WBCN who believed in four guys from New Hampshire before anyone else did.

"I need you to understand something about 1979. It wasn't just another year. It was the pivot point when rock and roll had to decide what it wanted to be. The corporations were circling, smelling money in what had been rebellion. We were living in the last moments when two kids with nothing but talent and rage could matter as much as marketing budgets."

I load the first song—not Aerosmith, not The Cars or J. Geils Band, but a scratchy demo that never made it to vinyl. The tape hisses with age, the left channel crackles if you push it past six, but that imperfection is the point. This isn't about perfect sound. This is about perfect truth.

"This is the only recording that exists of Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh playing together. Not from that night in 1979—no, this is from the night after the Battle of the Bands, when they came to this very studio and proved that harmony was still possible, even after everything that had happened. March sixteenth, 1979. Listen to it. Listen to what we lost."

The demo starts—two young voices finding harmony over acoustic guitars, neither Aerosmith nor Queen but something uniquely theirs. It lasts ninety-three seconds before cutting off mid-verse. That's all that survived.

"Ninety-three seconds. That's how much evidence I have that America chose division over music. The rest of the story? That's what the next four hours are for. So pour yourself something strong, turn off your phone, and let me tell you how the Battle of the Bands became the war that killed rock and roll's democratic promise."

The ON AIR sign pulses like a dying heartbeat. Four hours left to tell the truth, four hours left to make sense of forty-five years of watching America choose noise over harmony.

I crack my knuckles—arthritis now, too many decades of spinning vinyl—and lean into the microphone one more time. The bourbon's warmth spreads through my chest like liquid courage, the good kind that makes you tell stories you've been carrying too long.

"But first, we need to talk about a garage on Dutton Road, and why the ghost of a dead construction worker might have been the only adult who understood what was really at stake…"
