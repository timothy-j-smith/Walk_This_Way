## CHAPTER 17: TOWN INVESTMENT

The Battle of the Bands had stopped being a high school competition. It had become something else entirely—a referendum on class, a test of community values, a chance for Sudbury to prove to itself that it understood what mattered about music, about talent, about the American dream.

By the first week of March 1979, the entire town was invested.

**THE BARBER SHOP**

Frank's Barber Shop on Sudbury Road had been cutting hair and hosting debates for three decades. Every Tuesday morning, the same group of retired men gathered to discuss town politics, Red Sox prospects, and the weather. But for the past two weeks, they'd been arguing about something else entirely.

"Johnson's kid's got real talent," said Al Peterson, who'd worked construction with Dan Johnson before the accident. "That boy can make a guitar sing like it's got a soul."

"Talent don't pay the bills," countered Bob Mitchell, who owned the hardware store and had two kids at Harvard. "Marsh's boy's been trained properly. Classical background, proper technique. That's what wins competitions."

The debate had been going on for days, each man taking sides based on their own experiences, their own understanding of what success looked like, their own biases about where talent came from and where it belonged.

**THE DINER**

Sudbury Diner on Route 20 had become ground zero for the town's investment in the competition. Every booth held a different conversation about the Battle of the Bands, every table a different perspective on what was at stake.

Grace Johnson sat in her usual booth, nursing a cup of coffee and listening to the conversations around her. She'd become something of a celebrity since her speech at Friendly's, but celebrity felt heavier than anonymity.

"Your boy's got the whole town behind him," said Mary Kowalski, whose son Steve was in Nick's band. "Every working family in Sudbury's rooting for him."

But Grace could also hear the other conversations—the ones about how Nick was "just a garage band kid," about how Jackie had "real musical training," about how the competition would prove once and for all whether talent could overcome circumstance.

**THE LOCAL PAPER**

The Sudbury Weekly had never covered a high school event with this much enthusiasm. The editor, Tom Harrison, had assigned three reporters to cover the Battle of the Bands, treating it like a major sporting event.

"Battle of the Bands: Class Warfare or Musical Celebration?" read the headline on March 8th. The article quoted parents, teachers, and students, each offering their perspective on what the competition represented.

"The real question," wrote Harrison in his editorial, "is whether Sudbury can celebrate musical diversity or whether we'll use this competition to reinforce the divisions that already exist in our community."

**THE RADIO SPOTS**

WBCN had been promoting the Battle of the Bands for weeks. Maxanne Sartori's voice could be heard throughout the Boston area, building anticipation for what she called "the most important high school competition in Massachusetts history."

"This isn't just about two bands," she'd said in her most recent spot. "This is about two different ideas of what American music can be. This is about whether authenticity or excellence matters more. This is about whether we can find harmony in our differences or whether we'll let division destroy what could be beautiful."

The spots had been running every hour, building the competition into something larger than itself, something that transcended high school rivalries and touched on fundamental questions about class, talent, and the American dream.

**THE SCHOOL HALLWAYS**

Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School had become a battleground of its own. Students wore their allegiances like team colors—Aerosmith t-shirts for Nick's supporters, Queen buttons for Jackie's fans. The hallways buzzed with anticipation, with arguments about which band was better, which approach to music was more authentic.

"Walking the Line's got real soul," said Sarah Murphy, who'd been Nick's lab partner in chemistry. "They understand what music's supposed to feel like."

"Crown Jewels has technical excellence," countered Jennifer Walsh, who'd been in Jackie's music theory class. "They understand what music's supposed to sound like."

The debate had divided the school along lines that had nothing to do with geography and everything to do with philosophy—about what mattered in music, about what mattered in life, about what it meant to be authentic in a world that seemed determined to force people into categories.

**THE PARENTS' MEETINGS**

The PTA had scheduled an emergency meeting to discuss the "tension" that the Battle of the Bands had created in the school. Parents from both sides had been calling the principal's office, concerned about the "hostile environment" that the competition had created.

"This has gotten out of hand," said Dr. Richard Marsh, speaking on behalf of the parents who supported Jackie. "A simple music competition has become a referendum on class and privilege. That's not what education should be about."

"This is exactly what education should be about," countered Grace Johnson, speaking for the parents who supported Nick. "Our kids are learning that talent comes from everywhere, that excellence isn't limited to those who can afford the best training. That's a lesson worth learning."

**THE BUSINESS COMMUNITY**

Local businesses had started taking sides as well. The music store on Main Street had put up a poster supporting Nick's band. The bookstore had a display of Queen albums in their window. The pizza place was offering a "Battle of the Bands Special" with half-price slices for students wearing band t-shirts.

Even the town council had gotten involved, scheduling a meeting to discuss whether the competition should be moved to a larger venue to accommodate the expected crowd.

"This has become bigger than a high school event," said Mayor Patricia O'Brien. "This has become a community event, and we need to treat it as such."

**THE FINAL COUNTDOWN**

By March 14th, the day before the Battle of the Bands, the entire town of Sudbury was holding its breath. The competition had stopped being about two bands and had become about everything—about class and privilege, about talent and training, about authenticity and excellence, about whether America could find harmony in its differences or whether division would always win.

The Battle of the Bands was no longer just a high school competition. It was a referendum on the American dream itself.
