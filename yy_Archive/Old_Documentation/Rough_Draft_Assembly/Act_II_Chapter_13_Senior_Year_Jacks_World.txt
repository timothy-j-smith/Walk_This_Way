## CHAPTER 13: SENIOR YEAR - JACKIE'S WORLD

The Marsh house on Maple Street felt like a museum of expectations. Jackie sat at the grand piano in the living room, his fingers moving through Chopin etudes with the mechanical precision of someone who'd been practicing since before memory. The notes were perfect—every one struck with technical excellence that spoke of thousands of hours of lessons, of parents who considered anything less than excellence a character flaw.

But Jackie was dying in there.

Behind the technical perfection was a boy playing someone else's definition of music, speaking someone else's language with flawless pronunciation but no soul. The piano had become a prison, the living room a stage where he performed the role of the perfect son, the musical prodigy, the boy who would make his parents proud.

Dr. Richard Marsh appeared in the doorway, his Boston Brahmin accent turning 'car' into 'cah' but somehow making it sound aristocratic rather than working class. "Jackie, we need to discuss your college applications."

Jackie's hands stilled on the keys. "I thought we already submitted them."

"Early admission to Juilliard," Dr. Marsh said, his voice carrying that particular tone that turned statements into commands. "But I'm hearing concerns about your focus. This Battle of the Bands competition—it's a distraction from your real future."

"It's just a local competition," Jackie said, his voice carefully neutral.

"Local competitions don't lead to symphony halls," Dr. Marsh replied. "They lead to... well, they lead to places like that garage where your friend Nick practices."

The words hung in the air like an accusation. Jackie felt the familiar weight of his father's disappointment, the pressure to be everything his parents had invested in, the fear that he would never be good enough to justify the sacrifices they'd made.

Elizabeth appeared in the doorway, his younger sister who seemed to understand the pressure he was under without ever having to experience it herself. "Jackie, your band is here."

Jackie looked up, grateful for the interruption. "Thanks, Liz."

As he gathered his sheet music and prepared to leave, Dr. Marsh's voice followed him. "Remember, Jackie. Excellence isn't negotiable. It's expected."

The parking lot at Lincoln-Sudbury had become an unofficial staging area. Jackie's band loaded equipment with military precision—professional cases for every instrument, organized cables, backup equipment for every possible scenario. They worked with the efficiency of people who'd been trained to do things properly, who understood that preparation was the difference between success and failure.

Twenty feet away, Nick's band loaded gear with working-class efficiency—instruments in whatever cases they could find, cables wrapped around whatever was available, making do with what they had and making it work.

They pointedly ignored each other, but the tension was electric.

Jackie's boom box started playing "Somebody to Love," and his band members automatically began moving with theatrical precision—the Queen influence evident in every gesture. The music filled the parking lot, drawing attention from students who stopped to watch.

Jackie couldn't help himself. He began to sing with operatic power, his classically trained voice soaring through the afternoon air. "Somebody to love, somebody to love..."

Nick heard it and cranked his own boom box—"Mama Kin" blasting out. His band started moving with Steven Tyler's distinctive swagger, raw energy meeting theatrical precision.

"It ain't easy, livin' like you wanna live," Nick sang with raw emotion, his voice rougher but carrying something deeper.

The parking lot became a musical face-off. Students stopped to watch as precision met raw power, as classical training met garage authenticity, as two different definitions of excellence clashed in the afternoon sun.

Elizabeth approached Jackie, her voice carrying over the music. "This is stupid, Jackie. Nick was your best friend. You were inseparable."

Jackie kept performing, but his voice carried a note of pain. "I miss who he used to be."

The music stopped simultaneously. In the sudden silence, Jackie and Nick looked at each other across twenty feet of asphalt.

For a moment, it seemed like one of them might speak. They might break the barrier, might find the words to bridge the gap that had grown between them. But then Jackie's mother honked her horn for Elizabeth, and Nick's friends called for him to hurry.

The moment passed. The opportunity was lost.

As Jackie loaded the last of his equipment into his mother's station wagon, he thought about the cafeteria performance, about the way Nick had stood up and sung "Dream On" with such raw authenticity. He thought about the way their voices had once harmonized perfectly, about the way they'd once understood each other without words.

But that was before America convinced them they were supposed to be enemies. Before they'd learned that talent could be a weapon instead of a bridge. Before they'd discovered that the same music that had once united them could now divide them.

Jackie looked across the parking lot at Nick, who was loading his own equipment into an old pickup truck. For a moment, their eyes met.

Then they both looked away.

The Battle of the Bands was ten days away, and they were already choosing sides.
