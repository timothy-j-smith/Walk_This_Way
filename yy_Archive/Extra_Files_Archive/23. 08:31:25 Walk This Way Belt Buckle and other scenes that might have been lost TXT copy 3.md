Chapter 4A: The Belt Buckles That Started Everything
"But first," I say, stubbing out another cigarette and reaching for the cart I've been saving, "I need to tell you about two belt buckles and how they almost changed the world."
September 1976. Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh, both thirteen, both starting eighth grade at Lincoln-Sudbury Regional Middle School, both wearing their musical allegiances like armor into the battlefield of junior high cafeteria politics.
Nick's belt buckle was chrome-plated Aerosmith—the winged logo polished to mirror brightness, catching fluorescent light like a signal flare. He'd bought it with lawn-mowing money at Spencer's in the mall, the same weekend he'd spent his birthday cash on "Rocks," their latest album that proved rock and roll was still dangerous, still real, still the sound of America when it stopped pretending to be civilized.
Jackie's was pewter Queen—elegant, understated, the band's crest that looked like it belonged in medieval heraldry rather than on a thirteen-year-old's Levi's. A Christmas gift from his aunt who lived in London, who'd seen them at Hammersmith Odeon and understood that Freddie Mercury was redefining what rock stars could be, what music could accomplish when it stopped worrying about rules and started making its own.
"The thing about thirteen-year-old boys," I tell my audience, "is they'll fight about anything if it means avoiding the fight they're really having, which is usually with themselves."
They met in Mr. Patterson's—excuse me, Mr. Marshal's—social studies class, assigned seats alphabetically, Johnson and Marsh right next to each other by the accident of surnames and the inevitability of fate. For the first week, they were just two kids trying to navigate the particular hell that is eighth grade, where your voice cracks at random moments and your body grows in directions you didn't authorize and every day brings fresh evidence that childhood is ending whether you're ready or not.
Then came the Great Belt Buckle Confrontation.
It was a Tuesday morning in late September, Indian summer making the classrooms feel like saunas, windows open to let in air that smelled like dying leaves and new possibilities. Jackie had worn a Queen t-shirt—the "News of the World" tour shirt that his aunt had sent from England, soft cotton that announced his taste to anyone who cared to notice.
Nick noticed. Of course he did. He'd been wearing his Aerosmith shirt—"Draw the Line" tour, purchased at the Worcester Centrum where he'd seen them live, first concert ever, ears ringing for three days afterward like a religious conversion.
"Nice shirt," Nick had said, and even at thirteen he understood sarcasm as both weapon and peace offering.
"Thanks," Jackie replied, and his voice carried that careful courtesy that rich kids learn early, the ability to be polite while keeping all options open. "I like yours too."
They stared at each other for a moment, two kids taking inventory: Nick seeing the expensive clothes, the perfect teeth, the confidence that comes from never worrying about whether the lights will stay on. Jackie seeing the worn jeans, the callused hands, the particular pride that comes from earning everything you have.
"Queen's pretty good," Nick offered, which in thirteen-year-old diplomacy was practically a peace treaty.
"Aerosmith rocks," Jackie countered, which was his version of extending an olive branch while keeping his sword ready.
And so began the Great Debate of 1976, conducted in whispers during social studies, continued at lunch, resumed walking between classes, a rolling argument that covered every possible angle: technical skill versus raw power, British sophistication versus American authenticity, Freddie Mercury's four-octave range versus Steven Tyler's raw emotion.
"Mercury can hit notes that don't exist in nature," Jackie would argue, pulling out album covers like evidence in a court case. "Listen to 'Bohemian Rhapsody'—it's opera, it's rock, it's completely insane, and it works."
"Tyler doesn't need to hit high notes," Nick would counter, his own collection of vinyl providing ammunition for the war. "He makes you feel every note in your chest. Listen to 'Dream On'—that's real pain, man. That's what rock and roll is supposed to be."
They'd spend entire lunch periods arguing whether guitar solos should be melodic (Brian May) or raw (Joe Perry), whether rock stars should wear costumes (Mercury's theatrical outfits) or just get up there and play (Tyler's scarves and leather). They debated lyrics, production values, the proper role of keyboards in rock music, whether British bands could really understand the blues.
"Here's what killed me," I tell my listeners, lighting another cigarette because some memories require nicotine, "watching those two kids discover they were having the same argument from different sides. They both believed music mattered more than anything else. They just disagreed about what mattering looked like."
Because that's what it was, really: two kids who'd found something they cared about more than fitting in, more than being cool, more than surviving the daily humiliation that was junior high school. Music was their salvation, their escape, their proof that there was something in the world worth getting excited about.
The debates spread. Other kids chose sides, not because they particularly cared about Queen versus Aerosmith, but because taking sides is what junior high students do—it's practice for all the larger divisions they'll face later, training for a lifetime of choosing teams instead of finding common ground.
But Nick and Jackie? They were still friends. Best friends, even. The musical argument was foreplay, the warm-up act before the real show, which happened after school in Dan Johnson's garage.
"October 1976," I continue, checking the time not for countdown but for pacing, "Nick invited Jackie home. First time a Concord Road kid had ever set foot on Dutton Road, first time Jackie had seen how the other half lived."
The Johnson house looked smaller than Jackie had imagined, more fragile, like a strong wind might relocate it to somewhere less convenient. But the garage was revelation: twenty feet of pure possibility, Dan's tools hanging like promises of things that could be built, fixed, improved through the application of skill and stubbornness.
Nick's guitar was there—the pawn shop special with the warped neck—along with a bass he'd borrowed from his cousin and a drum kit assembled from three different garage sales. Not professional equipment, but equipment that wanted to be played, that had been waiting for kids brave enough to make noise.
"Want to try something?" Nick had asked, and in that question was everything: invitation, challenge, possibility that their argument might become collaboration.
They started with "We Will Rock You" because it was simple, because everyone knew it, because it was impossible to screw up unless you really tried. Jackie on vocals, Nick on guitar, both of them stomping on the concrete floor to keep time.
Then something magical happened. Jackie started improvising, turning Queen's anthem into something more complex, adding vocal runs that Mercury would have envied. Nick found guitar parts that weren't in the original, blues licks that transformed British pomp into American grit.
"Now try this," Nick said, launching into the opening riff of "Walk This Way," and suddenly they were in different territory, American rock and roll with all its swagger and attitude.
But Jackie didn't just follow along. He found harmonies, countermelodies, ways to make his classically trained voice dance around Tyler's blues shouting. He turned Aerosmith's raw power into something more sophisticated, more layered, more interesting than either band had achieved alone.
"November 1976," I say, "they were inseparable. Best friends who'd discovered that arguing about music was more fun than agreeing about everything else."
They'd spend every afternoon in that garage, trying to crack the code of what made great rock and roll great. Taking Queen songs and making them more American, taking Aerosmith songs and making them more sophisticated, finding the place where British theatricality met Massachusetts authenticity.
Dr. Marsh noticed, of course. Started asking questions about where Jackie was spending his time, who these new friends were, whether this musical experimentation was interfering with his classical training. The piano lessons continued, the theory classes, the carefully planned development of a young musician who was expected to achieve excellence in acceptable forms.
But Jackie had tasted something else now: the joy of making music that nobody had planned, that emerged from collaboration rather than instruction, that proved creativity was more interesting than perfection.
"December 1976," I continue, "the middle school announced its annual talent show. January 15th, 1977. The same competition where Karen Bowers had won first place for four consecutive years with her flawless renditions of Broadway standards."
Karen Bowers: the princess of Lincoln-Sudbury Middle School, daughter of the music teacher at the elementary school, blessed with perfect pitch, classical training, and the kind of blond hair that bounced when she moved. She sang like she was auditioning for roles she was destined to win, performed like she was doing everyone a favor by sharing her obvious talent.
Every year, the same result: Karen first place, some instrumental act second, everyone else grateful for participation ribbons and the chance to tell their parents they'd tried their best.
"But Nick and Jackie had other ideas," I tell the darkness beyond the booth. "They'd been working on something. A secret project. A song that would prove their argument was actually harmony in disguise."
It was Jackie's idea, really. Take "Under Pressure," the David Bowie and Queen collaboration that had just been released, and reimagine it as an acoustic duet. Strip away the synthesizers, the production, the studio tricks, and see what remained when it was just two voices and two guitars, finding the spaces where they could coexist.
They practiced in secret, after the official garage sessions, when Dr. Marsh thought Jackie was at the library and Grace Johnson thought Nick was doing homework. Two thirteen-year-olds teaching themselves how to harmonize, how to take turns with the melody, how to make two different musical philosophies into one song.
"January 15th, 1977," I announce, "the night everything changed."
The middle school auditorium was packed—parents with cameras, kids with attitudes, teachers hoping for the best and expecting chaos. Karen Bowers was scheduled to close the show, the traditional spot for the inevitable winner, performing "Tomorrow" from "Annie" with the kind of polish that made everyone else feel like amateurs.
Nick and Jackie were somewhere in the middle, two kids with acoustic guitars, listed in the program simply as "Johnson & Marsh." No band name, no elaborate costumes, no choreography—just music.
They took the stage looking nervous, which they were, and determined, which they were more of. Two boys who'd discovered that their musical argument had actually been a love song in disguise, a debate that had become a duet.
Jackie started it, his voice clear and controlled: "Pressure, pushing down on me..."
Then Nick joined, harmony underneath, blues inflection against classical precision: "Pressing down on you, no man ask for..."
And for three minutes and twenty-one seconds, they proved that Queen and Aerosmith weren't opposites. They were complementary colors, different frequencies of the same essential truth about what music could accomplish when it stopped worrying about categories and started caring about connection.
The audience went silent first, then restless, then completely absorbed. This wasn't what they'd expected from a middle school talent show. This was something else: two kids making music that mattered, that felt real, that proved collaboration could create something neither could achieve alone.
When they finished, the silence lasted long enough for everyone to understand they'd witnessed something important. Then the applause started—not polite appreciation, but genuine enthusiasm, recognition that the rules had just been rewritten.
Karen Bowers still performed "Tomorrow," and she was still technically perfect. But perfect wasn't enough anymore. Not after everyone had heard what possible sounded like when it wasn't worried about being proper.
"Johnson & Marsh: First Place," announced Principal Sargent, and in those three words was proof that sometimes the best arguments are the ones that end in agreement.
The prize was two weeks at Camp Winnipesaukee, the music intensive program in New Hampshire. All expenses paid, a chance to study with professional musicians, to meet other kids who understood that music was more than hobby, more than entertainment, more than the thing you did when you were bored.
"March 1977," I say, stubbing out the cigarette, "two best friends prepared for summer camp, still arguing about Queen versus Aerosmith, still discovering that argument was just another word for friendship when both sides cared about the same things."
They didn't know they were about to meet a girl who would complicate everything. They didn't know that high school was waiting like a patient predator, ready to teach them that best friends were luxuries they couldn't afford, that music was supposed to divide rather than unite, that America had very specific ideas about which kids were supposed to succeed and which were supposed to fail.
They were still thirteen. Still innocent. Still believing that winning talent shows meant something, that music could solve problems instead of creating them, that harmony was the natural state of the universe and discord was just what happened when people forgot how to listen.
"But first," I tell my audience, reaching for the next cart, "summer camp. Where they learned that even the best friendships can survive only so much possibility, only so much choice, only so much evidence that the world is bigger than the arguments that define us."
I slide the cart into the machine, the same demo I've been saving all night. But now you know what it is: two thirteen-year-olds making music that proved difference could be harmony, that arguments could be love songs, that the best collaborations start with the bravest disagreements.
"This is Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh, January 15th, 1977, Lincoln-Sudbury Middle School Talent Show. Listen to what we lost. Listen to what was possible before America reminded them it wasn't."
The music starts—two young voices finding perfect harmony, two guitars making one sound, three minutes and twenty-one seconds of proof that the best friendships are the ones that argue about everything except what matters most.
Which is music. Which is connection. Which is the frequency that exists between any two voices brave enough to sing together instead of alone.
