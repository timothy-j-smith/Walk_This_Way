WALK THIS WAY


Chapter 1: The Last Voice of Boston Rock

The thing about endings is they make liars of us all. We promise to go out with dignity, to say something profound, to make those final moments count. But here I am, Maxanne Sartori, seventy-three years old, sitting in the same Studio C where I discovered Aerosmith in 1973, and all I can think about is two kids who never made it onto my playlist.
WBCN dies at midnight. After fifty-six years of being the Rock of Boston, we're flipping to automated Christian contemporary. The equipment around me—turntables I fought to keep when everything went digital, cart machines that hold the DNA of Boston rock, mixing boards that have channeled more raw talent than any studio in America—will be sold for scrap or shipped to museums where teenagers will stare at them like archaeological artifacts, not understanding that these machines once made democracy possible, that radio could be more than background noise, that voices could matter more than the demographics that determined whose voices got heard.
"Maxanne?" The program director's voice crackles through the intercom. He's twenty-eight, wears sneakers that cost more than my first car, and thinks rock history started with Nirvana. "Four hours left. Make them count."
Make them count. As if four hours could contain what WBCN meant to this city, to rock and roll, to the kids who found their voices through our signal. As if I could compress fifty-six years of discovering music into a neat little package tied with nostalgic ribbon.
I adjust my headphones—the same model I've worn since 1971, held together now with electrical tape and pure stubbornness—and methodically arrange the vinyl records I've selected for tonight's final broadcast, each one placed with the reverent precision of someone who understands these artifacts will outlast the station that once gave them meaning. The ON AIR sign flashes red. One last time.
"Boston," I say into the microphone that's been my confessional, my pulpit, my lifeline since Nixon was president. "This is Maxanne, and we need to talk."
Outside Studio C, the Prudential Tower blinks its lonely beacon across a city that's forgotten what it meant to have a rock station that wasn't afraid to play something nobody had ever heard before. Boston glitters in the October darkness, all glass towers and gentrification where dive bars used to breed bands, where the Combat Zone once pulsed with the kind of danger that made rock and roll necessary.
I reach for my water glass—hydration matters when you're planning to talk for four straight hours. The bourbon will come later, when the stories get harder to tell. Some memories require liquid contemplation.
"I've been your guide through Boston rock since before some of you were born," I continue, my voice settling into the rhythm that's guided this city through decades of late nights and broken hearts. "I gave Springsteen his first radio interview in '73 when he was just another Jersey kid with a guitar and something to prove. He played 'Rosalita' live in this booth, just him and Clarence on sax, and said 'This is my first radio interview, my mother lives in California, HI MOM!' I knew—the same way I knew with Tyler—that something real was happening."
I carefully clean the needle on the vintage turntable with the anti-static cloth that's become as essential to my routine as breathing, understanding that some equipment deserves reverence rather than replacement. These machines have heartbeats, have memories embedded in their circuits and worn spots that tell stories no digital interface could carry.
"But tonight, I want to tell you about the night I watched the future of rock and roll die in a high school auditorium. March fifteenth, 1979. Two kids from opposite sides of a brook that might as well have been the Atlantic Ocean."
The console lights blink expectantly. I've got carts loaded with Aerosmith's "Dream On," Queen's "Bohemian Rhapsody," The Cars, The J. Geils Band, all the legends. But first, I need to take you back to when those legends were just beginning to cast shadows, when the real battles were fought not in recording studios but in the divided territories of suburban high schools.
I pull out a cart labeled in my own careful handwriting: "Nick & Jackie - Middle School Demo - 2:47 - NEVER AIRED." The tape that proves harmony was possible, recorded when two best friends thought music could solve everything, before America taught them they were supposed to hate each other.
"Let me tell you about two boys," I continue, organizing the next sequence of albums with obsessive attention to detail that's kept my broadcasts coherent for thirty years. "Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh. You've never heard their names because they never made it. Not together. Not separately. Not in any way that matters to the recording industry or the rock and roll history books or the people who decide which dreams get to survive."
The demo sits in the cart machine, waiting. Two minutes and forty-seven seconds of two thirteen-year-old boys harmonizing their way toward a future that never arrived, recorded at their middle school talent show in 1975 when they still believed music was stronger than the social machinery designed to keep them apart.
"But they should have made it. Christ, they should have been bigger than Aerosmith, more innovative than Queen, more authentic than any band that ever emerged from Boston's working-class dreams or its ivory-tower privilege. They had something that can't be taught, can't be manufactured, can't be replicated: they had the musical chemistry that turns two individual voices into one sound that nobody else could create."
I reach for the bourbon now—some stories require something stronger than water. The amber liquid catches the studio lights, promising warmth for the stories that hurt most to tell.
"This is the story of what happened when American class warfare decided that friendship was a luxury working-class kids and rich kids couldn't afford. It's about March 15, 1979. Disco was dying its overdue death. The Clash were about to teach America that punk had something to say. And in Sudbury, Massachusetts—that's about twenty miles west of here for you newcomers—two seventeen-year-old boys were about to go to war over a question that seems quaint now: What is authentic rock and roll?"
The ON AIR sign pulses like a heartbeat. Four hours left to tell the truth about what we lost, what we chose, what still matters if anybody's brave enough to listen.
"But first, you need to understand how we got there. You need to know what Nick and Jackie were before they became symbols, before they became casualties, before they became proof that some frequencies are stronger than the static we create to drown them out."




Chapter 2: The Geography of Division

"Before I tell you about the battle," I say, swirling the bourbon in my glass and watching how it catches the studio lights like liquid amber, "I need to tell you about the battlefield. Because Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School wasn't just divided by a brook. It was quartered like a medieval criminal, mapped by invisible lines that determined everything from where you sat at lunch to who you were allowed to love."
I cue up Pink Floyd's "Welcome to the Machine" because Roger Waters understood how systems digest people and excrete compliance, how institutions can turn human connection into processed product designed for efficient consumption.
"Picture Sudbury from above in 1979," I continue, my voice getting that late-night raspiness that used to drive program directors crazy. "A town carved into quarters like a medieval map of the humors—blood, phlegm, choler, and melancholy—each section containing its own species of American dream, its own relationship to disappointment."
My engineer looks up from his phone, possibly interested for the first time tonight. Even young digital natives understand geography. They've all lived somewhere that mattered, somewhere that shaped them whether they wanted it to or not.
"Hop Brook runs north to south through the middle of town like God had drawn a property line with His finger, separating those who worried about mortgage payments from those who worried about mortgage rates. The brook starts in Ashland, gathers momentum through Sudbury, and empties into the Sudbury River near the Wayside Inn where Henry Ford tried to preserve American authenticity by buying it wholesale. Even our water has a sense of irony."
I methodically organize my next set of vinyl, each album placed with the careful precision of someone who understands that order matters when everything else is chaos, that some rituals become more important as they become more rare.
"But Hop Brook is only half the story. Running east to west, bisecting the town at a perfect right angle, sits what everyone simply called 'The Dump'—twenty-three acres of American excess where Saturday mornings taught democracy and disposal in equal measure. The official name was Sudbury Sanitary Landfill, but nobody used official names for places that smelled like truth."
The turntable needle needs cleaning again. Some habits become meditation when the world gets too loud. I use the anti-static cloth with the same reverence my grandmother used for her rosary, understanding that repetition can be prayer when it serves something larger than itself.
"The Sudbury dump wasn't just where garbage went to die. It was where families went on Saturday mornings, station wagons loaded with a week's worth of American excess. Fathers and kids—mostly fathers and sons, let's be honest about 1979—would drive up to the landfill and participate in that most democratic of rituals: throwing your shit away and watching bulldozers bury it. The smell when the wind was wrong could peel paint off houses a mile away, a constant reminder that everyone's consumption eventually becomes someone else's problem."
I light what I promise myself is my last cigarette for this segment, understanding that some promises are meant to be broken when the story requires it.
"East of The Dump, right up against it like a joke God was telling about American efficiency, sprawled Cavicchio's Pig Farm. Three hundred acres of Yankee pragmatism—pigs eating garbage, garbage feeding pigs, the circle of life with a Boston accent. Giuseppe Cavicchio had refused to sell his water rights to Henry Ford in 1923, blocking Ford's plan for an auto parts factory. Ford called it 'Italian stubbornness.' Giuseppe called it 'understanding what land is worth.' The pigs understood both perspectives and kept eating."
These two lines—water and waste—created four distinct Sudburys, each with its own ZIP code anxiety, its own relationship to the American dream, its own soundtrack for surviving disappointment.
"Northeast: The working-class quarter that bordered Lincoln—a town so separate that most Sudbury residents would never set foot there in their lifetime, though it shared the regional high school. Lincoln existed like a ghost town just a football toss away, known to Sudburyites only through the hyphen in 'Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School.' Row houses and triple-deckers lined streets named after trees that had been cut down to build them—Maple, Oak, Elm. Streets where the lawn had surrendered to New England winter months ago, brown grass poking through dirty snow like stubble on a dead man's face."
I reach for my water glass, understanding that bourbon and cigarettes require balance if you want your voice to last four hours, if you want the story to matter more than the storyteller.
"This was where fathers worked construction and overtime, where mothers counted coupons and prayed the car would pass inspection, where kids learned guitar because instruments were cheaper than therapy, and both were cheaper than expecting college. Kids here understood something about time that most teenagers don't—how fast it steals what you love, how quickly dreams become luxuries you can't afford when you're the man of the house before you're even a man."
The studio lights reflect off the vinyl, creating small rainbows that dance across the album covers like promise, like possibility, like the brief moments when music makes everything else bearable.
"Southeast: The rural quarter, where the Cavicchio pig farm sprawled against the landfill in what seemed like cosmic joke but was actually cosmic justice. Dirt roads that turned to mud in spring, farmhouses that had been there since before anyone remembered why they were built, families who understood that land ownership was different from land stewardship, that some things couldn't be bought or sold because they belonged to something larger than individual ambition."
I clean the needle again, watching how the cloth picks up dust that could destroy sound, understanding how small things matter more than big things when quality depends on attention to detail.
"Southwest: The forest quarter, protected by the Sudbury Valley Trustees and careful zoning that preserved both trees and property values. Colonial homes with original wide-plank floors sat on lots measured in acres rather than feet, driveways that curved through woods where stone walls older than the Constitution marked boundaries between estates named after dead Indians and living WASPs. This was where professors lived, where mothers ran the PTA with military efficiency, where kids learned piano because musical literacy was assumed, not earned."
The bourbon is working now, warming my chest and loosening the stories that usually stay locked away, understanding that some truths require chemical courage to survive their own telling.
"Northwest: The money quarter, where the Revolutionary War seemed to have ended just yesterday and on favorable terms. Houses that had names instead of just addresses—'Whispering Pines,' 'Old Mill Farm,' 'Concord View'—mansions that sat behind gates designed to keep out everything unpleasant, including accountability. Stone walls that weren't just property markers but philosophical statements about who belonged where, who deserved what, who had the right to expect their dreams to survive contact with reality."
Between these four quarters ran the transportation lines that proved connection wasn't the same as unity, that proximity could increase division as easily as it could decrease it.
"The Massachusetts Central Railroad ran east to west, carrying freight that never stopped in Sudbury because Sudbury had nothing to export except tax revenue and the particular kind of passive-aggressive resentment that comes from having everything you're supposed to want without knowing why it doesn't feel like enough."
I check the clock: three hours and seventeen minutes left. Time enough to explain how four quadrants became two armies, how a brook became a battlefield, how two best friends became symbols of everything wrong with American democracy when it chooses efficiency over equity, order over justice, comfort over connection.
"Route 20—the old Boston Post Road—cut through all four quarters like a scar that wouldn't heal, carrying traffic that connected everywhere else while dividing everything here. Kids would stand at the bus stops watching cars go by, understanding that motion wasn't the same as progress, that movement could trap you as easily as staying still."
The ON AIR sign pulses. Outside, Boston sleeps fitfully, dreaming of what it was, forgetting what it's becoming. And somewhere in the darkness, two old men in their sixties are listening to their story being told, maybe remembering when geography was destiny, when zip codes determined dreams, when a brook was all it took to make best friends into strangers.
"When we come back," I say, "I'll tell you about a garage on Dutton Road, and why the ghost of a dead construction worker might have been the only adult who understood what was really at stake when two kids picked up guitars and tried to make sense of a world that insisted they were supposed to be enemies."

Chapter 3: The Garage That Held More Than Cars

"The Johnson family garage on Dutton Road," I say, carefully placing the needle on a well-worn copy of "Born to Run" because some stories require Springsteen's understanding of working-class dreams and their particular weight, "wasn't just where Dan Johnson had kept his tools. It was where his ghost still taught his son that dead men's wisdom could be louder than living men's advice."
The track plays softly under my voice, just background enough to remind everyone listening that music matters, that sound carries meaning beyond words, that some truths can only be transmitted through the frequency where heartbreak meets hope.
"Nick Johnson lived in a house that had been built for a different kind of family, a different kind of economy, a different relationship between work and reward. Two bedrooms upstairs, kitchen and living room downstairs, basement that smelled like motor oil and sawdust and the particular sadness that comes from dreams deferred until they become dreams denied."
I take a sip of bourbon, understanding that Grace Johnson's story requires respect, requires recognition of dignity under pressure, requires acknowledgment that some people carry more weight than they should have to because the alternative is watching everything fall apart.
"Grace Johnson worked double shifts at Friendly's, the only restaurant in Sudbury where kids from all four quadrants could afford to eat, where the red vinyl booths had absorbed forty years of whispered secrets and shouted arguments and the kind of honest conversation that happens when people stop pretending their problems are temporary. She served coffee to professors who discussed her son's 'limited prospects' without understanding that limitations aren't the same as ability, that circumstances don't determine capacity, that Nick's authenticity came from understanding what it cost to survive disappointment without becoming bitter."
I clean the needle again, understanding that this ritual has become more important as the night progresses, that maintaining quality requires constant attention when everything else is breaking down.
"The garage held Dan Johnson's ghost like a museum holds artifacts—everything exactly where he'd left it, tools arranged with the methodical precision of someone who understood that organization could be an act of love, that caring for equipment was the same as caring for the work it made possible. Nick would go there after school, after arguments with his mother about money, after seeing Jackie Marsh drive by in his father's BMW, after understanding that his dreams were supposed to be smaller because his circumstances were smaller."
The studio lights catch the bourbon in my glass, creating amber reflections that remind me of late autumn afternoons when everything beautiful is about to die, when beauty becomes more precious because it's temporary.
"But the garage was more than memorial. It was sanctuary. Nick had set up a practice space in the corner where his father's workbench used to be, amplifier and microphone surrounded by the tools that had built half of Sudbury's houses, that had put food on tables when construction work was steady, that had taught Nick what his hands could create when they worked in service of something larger than individual ambition."
I organize the next sequence of albums, understanding that the pacing of this story matters as much as its content, that rhythm can carry meaning when words become insufficient.
"The ghost of Dan Johnson wasn't supernatural. It was practical. It was the understanding that work matters, that skill matters, that creating something solid in a world designed to make everything temporary was its own form of rebellion. Nick learned guitar the same way his father had learned construction—through repetition, through attention to detail, through understanding that mastery comes from respecting the materials you're working with, whether they're wood and nails or words and melodies."
Grace Johnson appears in this story like dignity personified, like proof that class wasn't about money but about how you carried yourself when money wasn't enough, how you raised a son to believe in himself when society insisted he should lower his expectations.
"Grace still wore her Friendly's uniform when she came home from the late shift, still smelled like coffee and the particular exhaustion that comes from serving people who think service is submission, who mistake courtesy for subservience. But she never let that confusion become Nick's confusion. She taught him that working-class authenticity wasn't about accepting limitations—it was about understanding that real strength comes from creating beauty despite obstacles, not because of their absence."
I light another cigarette, understanding that some habits become more necessary as truths become harder to tell, that nicotine can be prayer when it helps you find the courage to say what needs saying.
"The garage where Nick practiced was thirty yards from the garage where Jackie Marsh kept his BMW. Thirty yards that might as well have been thirty miles, thirty years, thirty different planets. They could hear each other playing—sound carries in suburban neighborhoods designed for quiet, travels through the spaces between houses like secrets nobody wants to keep. Nick's Aerosmith covering Jackie's Queen, Jackie's classical training responding to Nick's emotional honesty, two boys making music that could have changed everything if geography hadn't made collaboration impossible."
The needle skips slightly—old vinyl, old stories, old wounds that haven't quite healed. I lift it carefully, clean both record and stylus, understanding that some maintenance becomes meditation when the alternative is silence.
"But they were listening to each other. Even when they couldn't admit it, even when their friends would have called it betrayal, even when the social mathematics of high school insisted that acknowledgment was weakness, that respect was surrender, that harmony was a luxury neither could afford because their audiences demanded conflict over connection."
Through the studio windows, Boston's skyline reminds me that cities survive by forgetting, that progress requires amnesia about what was sacrificed to create what is, that some stories only matter to the people who lived them.
"When we come back from the silence that lives between songs," I say, adjusting the microphone with the precision that comes from fifty years of understanding that sound is sacred, that transmission is responsibility, that some frequencies matter more than others, "I'll tell you about September 1976, about two belt buckles that started everything, about how thirteen-year-old boys can create friendship that seems stronger than geography until American social machinery teaches them geography is stronger than anything they could create together."
The bourbon is nearly gone. The cigarettes are half finished. The night is still young enough for truth, still dark enough for stories that require darkness to survive their own telling.
Outside, somewhere in the divided territories of greater Boston, two old men are listening to their young selves being described, maybe remembering when possibility felt infinite, when music seemed like the answer to every question, when friendship seemed like the one thing that could never be broken by anything as simple as a brook, a zip code, a difference in mortgage payments.
Maybe wondering if there's still time to find that frequency again.
Probably not. But maybe.

Chapter 2X: The Belt Buckles That Started Everything
"But first," I say, stubbing out another cigarette and reaching for the cart I've been saving, "I need to tell you about two belt buckles and how they almost changed the world."
Fall 1974. Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh, both thirteen, both starting eighth grade at Ephraim Curtis Middle School—named after Ephraim Curtis, born in Sudbury in 1642, son of a carpenter who'd migrated from England during the Great Puritan Migration. Like his father, he would become a carpenter, but also a trading post owner, a military officer during King Philip's War, a scout and negotiator who understood that survival sometimes required building bridges between communities that preferred to remain separate.
But Curtis Middle School in 1974 wasn't concerned with historical bridge-building. It was concerned with cafeteria politics, social geography, and the eternal middle school question of which table you belonged at during lunch period.
The cafeteria buzzed with typical institutional chaos that September afternoon. Brown bag lunches from mothers who worked at home, cafeteria trays from families who qualified for subsidized meals, individual milk cartons that kids mixed with Jello like science experiments nobody wanted to grade. But cutting through the ordinary acoustic confusion of twelve and thirteen-year-old social navigation came something that would define the next four years of American music: disco.
"Dancing Queen" poured from someone's transistor radio, the kind of synthesized Euro-pop that made rock and roll feel endangered, that suggested American music was surrendering to production values over authentic expression. Most kids accepted it as background noise, the soundtrack to their surrender to social pressures that required choosing sides, choosing tables, choosing identities that would follow them through high school and beyond.
At a corner table, Jackie Marsh sat alone, clearly the new kid trying to figure out where he fit in geography that made no sense to someone whose family had just moved from Ithaca, New York to suburban Massachusetts. His father's promotion had brought them from the university town where everything revolved around Cornell, where professors' kids mixed naturally regardless of their parents' bank accounts, where intellectual curiosity mattered more than ZIP codes. But Sudbury was different. Sudbury had rules he didn't understand yet.
He was smaller than he'd become but already carried himself with the careful precision that comes from understanding that every movement was being evaluated by kids who'd known each other since kindergarten. Nick Johnson entered the cafeteria scanning for a place to sit, wiry and already showing the restless energy that would define his relationship with authority, his approach to music, his eventual understanding that authenticity couldn't be taught but could be recognized when it appeared. His black leather belt sported a chrome buckle with "AEROSMITH" in gothic letters—polished to mirror brightness, catching fluorescent light like a signal flare. He'd bought it with lawn-mowing money at Spencer's in the mall, the same weekend he'd spent his birthday cash on "Rocks," their latest album that proved rock and roll was still dangerous, still real, still the sound of America when it stopped pretending to be civilized.

"What's wrong with Queen?" Jackie replied, defensive but curious, his voice carrying genuine interest rather than automatic hostility, suggesting he'd been waiting for someone to engage with his musical choices rather than dismiss them as pretentious foreign nonsense.
"Nothing wrong," Nick continued, grinning while displaying his own buckle with the pride that comes from representing something larger than individual preference. "Just... Aerosmith's better."
Jackie stood, energy building like electrical charge before thunderstorm, understanding that this conversation mattered more than typical cafeteria small talk. "Better? Have you heard 'Bohemian Rhapsody'?"
"Have you heard 'Dream On'?" Nick matched his energy, both boys recognizing that they'd stumbled into genuine debate rather than social posturing, that musical preference was revealing something more significant than taste.
They stared at each other while "Le Freak" blasted from someone's radio, the disco rhythm providing ironic soundtrack to confrontation that had nothing to do with synthesized dance music and everything to do with authentic expression versus manufactured entertainment.

Chapter 2A: The Geography of Division (V4)
I slide another cart into the machine and let the opening notes of "Peace of Mind" fill the booth—Boston's own contribution to the soundtrack of American anxiety. Outside the booth windows, Boston's skyline glitters with the cold beauty of digital prosperity, all glass towers and algorithmic efficiency where dive bars used to breed the kind of bands that understood geography as destiny.
