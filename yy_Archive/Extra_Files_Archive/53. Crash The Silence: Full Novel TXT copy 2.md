Chapter 1: The Last Voice of Boston Rock
The thing about endings is they make liars of us all. We promise to go out with dignity, to say something profound, to make those final moments count. But here I am, Maxanne Sartori, seventy-three years old, sitting in the same Studio C where I discovered Aerosmith in 1973, and all I can think about is two kids who never made it onto my playlist.
WBCN dies at midnight. After fifty-six years of being the Rock of Boston, we're flipping to automated Christian contemporary. The equipment around me—turntables I fought to keep relevant, cart machines that hold the DNA of Boston rock, mixing boards that have channeled more raw talent than any studio in America—will be sold for scrap or shipped to museums where teenagers will stare at them like archaeological artifacts.
"Maxanne?" The program director's voice crackles through the intercom. He's twenty-eight, wears sneakers that cost more than my first car, and thinks rock history started with Nirvana. "You've got four hours left. Make them count."
Make them count. As if four hours could contain what WBCN meant. As if I could summarize the moment when rock and roll was dangerous, when DJ voices mattered more than algorithms, when two kids with guitars could change the world if they could just stop hating each other long enough to plug in their amps.
I adjust the headphones—the same model I've worn since 1971, held together now with electrical tape and pure stubbornness. The ON AIR sign flashes red. One last time.
"This is Maxanne Sartori, and for the next four hours, you're listening to the final broadcast of WBCN, 104.1 on your FM dial. I've been your guide through the evolution of Boston rock since before some of you were born. I've spun the first singles from bands who went on to fill stadiums, and I've played demos from kids who never made it past their garage. But tonight, I want to tell you about the night I watched the future of rock and roll die in a high school cafeteria."
I take a sip of bourbon—another violation of modern radio protocol—and feel the warmth spread through my chest. Outside the booth windows, Boston glitters in the October darkness, all glass towers and gentrification where dive bars used to breed bands.
"You want to know why WBCN is dying? Why this is the very last broadcast? Let me tell you about two kids in 1979 who showed me exactly when American radio—and America itself—chose money over music."
The console lights blink expectantly. I've got carts loaded with Aerosmith's "Dream On," Queen's "Bohemian Rhapsody," The Cars, The J. Geils Band, all the Boston legends. But first, I need to take you back to when those legends were just beginning to cast shadows, when the real battles were fought not in recording studios but in the divided territories of suburban high schools.
"March, 1979. Disco was dying its overdue death. The Clash were about to teach America that punk had something to say. And in Sudbury, Massachusetts—that's about twenty miles west of here for you newcomers—two seventeen-year-old boys were about to go to war over a question that seems quaint now: What is authentic rock and roll?"
I spin my chair to face the wall of albums, each one a memory, a story, a moment when music mattered more than metrics. My fingers find the right cart without looking—muscle memory from five decades of curating sound.
"Nick Johnson lived in a house where the mortgage was always late and dreams were luxuries you couldn't afford. His father had died in a construction accident two years earlier, the kind of industrial tragedy that happens when safety regulations matter less than completion deadlines. His mother worked doubles at Friendly's, came home smelling like fryer oil and other people's happiness, and never once complained about her son spending grocery money on guitar strings."
The bourbon burns just right. In the engineering booth, the kid they've assigned to babysit my final show looks up from his phone. Maybe something in my voice warns him this won't be the nostalgia trip he expected.
"Jackie Marsh lived less than a mile away as the crow flies, but between them stretched Hop Brook—named a century ago for its hops, the old mill long gone to modernization. The brook divided Sudbury like a scar, separating the haves from the have-nots. In winter, kids from both sides might skate the same frozen pond, but when the ice melted, they returned to their proper territories. Jackie's father taught economics at Boston University, the kind of professor who wrote papers about the working class without ever living in their neighborhoods. Jackie had a voice trained by the best teachers money could buy, fingers that had touched pianos worth more than Nick Johnson's entire house."
I pause, letting the silence breathe. Radio silence used to terrify program directors, but I learned long ago that the spaces between words can say more than the words themselves.
"These boys should never have met. America designs its suburbs specifically to prevent these collisions—invisible lines drawn by property values, school districts, the careful architecture of keeping the classes in their proper lanes. But music has always been democracy's wild card, the frequency that ignores the boundaries we build."
Through the booth window, I can see the billboard across Boylston Street advertising luxury condos where The Rat used to host bands too raw for radio. Twenty-four hundred square feet starting at 2.2 million. The Ramones played that club in '77. Joey was so tall he had to duck under the pipes backstage.
"I met them both the night of March fifteenth, 1979. The Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School Battle of the Bands. I was there because the station had sponsored it, back when we could afford to care about local music. Back when Clear Channel hadn't swallowed half the FM dial and algorithms hadn't replaced intuition."
Another sip. The bourbon is smooth, expensive—a gift from Steven Tyler himself when he heard about the station closing. 'For the first lady who ever gave a damn,' the note said. He still remembers.
"I need you to understand something about 1979. It wasn't just another year. It was the pivot point when rock and roll had to decide what it wanted to be. The corporations were circling, smelling money in what had been rebellion. MTV was three years away from making image matter more than sound. We were living in the last moments when two kids with nothing but talent and rage could matter as much as marketing budgets."
I load the first song—not Aerosmith, not The Cars or J. Geils Band, but a scratchy demo that never made it to vinyl. The levels need adjusting; the left channel distorts if you push it past six. But that imperfection is the point.
"This is the only recording that exists of Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh playing together. Not from that night in 1979—no, this is from four years earlier, when they were thirteen and still believed harmony was possible between kids from different sides of the tracks. Listen to it. Listen to what we lost."
The demo starts—two young voices finding harmony over acoustic guitars, neither Aerosmith nor Queen but something uniquely theirs. It lasts ninety-three seconds before cutting off mid-verse. That's all that survived.
"Ninety-three seconds. That's how much evidence I have that America chose division over music. The rest of the story? That's what the next four hours are for. So pour yourself something strong, turn off your phone, and let me tell you how the Battle of the Bands became the war that killed rock and roll's democratic promise."
The ON AIR sign pulses like a heartbeat. Four hours left to tell the truth.
I crack my knuckles—arthritis now, too many decades of spinning vinyl—and lean into the microphone one more time.
"But first, we need to talk about a garage on Dutton Road, and why the ghost of a dead construction worker might have been the only adult who understood what was really at stake…"



Chapter 2: The Garage That Held More Than Cars
I cue up the next track—Aerosmith's "Dream On" from their first album—and let it play while I gather my thoughts. The bourbon catches the light from the console LEDs, amber in a glass older than my babysitting engineer.
"A garage," I continue on air, "can be a temple if you know how to worship."
I'd driven out to Dutton Road three weeks before the Battle of the Bands. My powder-blue Karmann Ghia—yes, I was that kind of '70s cool—looked ridiculous navigating the potholes that the town somehow never fixed on the working-class side of Hop Brook. But I'd heard rumors. A kid who made Steven Tyler sound like he was holding back. A garage where the sound hit you in the chest before you even opened the door.
The Johnson house squatted like it was tired: peeling paint, sagging gutters, lawn surrendered to New England winter. But from the detached garage came something alive—distorted power chords that made my radio-trained ears perk up.
"Mind if I listen?" I'd asked Mary Johnson when she answered the door. She was still in her Friendly's uniform, name tag reading MARY in that cheerful corporate font that lies about everything.
She studied me—this Boston radio personality in bell-bottoms and oversized sunglasses, standing on her cracked front step like some visitor from another planet. "You're that lady from WBCN. The one who broke Aerosmith."
"Guilty."
"Nick's in the garage." She paused, and I saw something flicker across her face—hope, maybe, or fear that hope was dangerous. "Don't fill his head with dreams we can't afford."
Back in the booth, 2024, I watch my engineer scroll through his phone. He has no idea what a garage means to rock and roll. He grew up with GarageBand, thinking music could be manufactured in a bedroom with the right plugins. He never felt the sacred geometry of four walls, concrete floor, and kids who had nothing to lose.
"The thing about Nick Johnson's garage," I tell my invisible audience, "was that it remembered his father."
Dan Johnson had died two years earlier, crushed beneath a load of steel beams when a crane cable snapped—the kind of industrial accident that makes page three of the Metro section and gets forgotten by everyone except the families who eat silence for dinner afterward. His tools still hung on the pegboard: hammers arranged by size, wrenches that would never turn another bolt, a socket set missing the 9/16ths he'd had in his pocket that day.
When I entered the garage, Nick was mid-solo, his fingers finding notes that shouldn't have been possible on a guitar held together with electrical tape and determination. Three other kids backed him—all Lincoln boys, I'd learn later, from the even-smaller town that shared a high school with Sudbury but not much else. They stopped when they saw me, that teenage terror of being caught in the act of caring about something.
"Don't stop on my account," I said, settling onto a paint can in the corner. "Play it like you mean it."
They exchanged glances—four working-class boys deciding whether to trust this intruder from the world of actual radio. Nick made the decision for them, launching back into "Dream On" with a fury that transformed the garage into something larger.
Here's what I need you to understand about authenticity: it's not something you can learn at Berklee. It's not in the perfect pitch or the precision fingering. It's in the way Nick Johnson's voice cracked when he hit the high notes, not from poor technique but from feeling too much to contain. It's in the way the garage walls seemed to lean in, listening, as if Dan Johnson's ghost was nodding along to his son's pain made music.
"Every time that I look in the mirror," Nick sang, "all these lines on my face getting clearer..."
Seventeen years old, singing about aging. But he understood something about time that most teenagers don't—how fast it steals what you love, how young you can feel old when you're the man of the house before you're even a man.
When they finished, the silence in that garage was holy. The kind of quiet that happens after truth has been spoken.
"What's your band called?" I asked.
Nick shrugged, that teenage gesture that protects against caring too much. "Don't have a name yet. We're just... playing."
"You're doing more than playing," I said. "You got a demo?"
The other boys looked at Nick. He shook his head. "Can't afford studio time."
I pulled out my card—back when WBCN business cards meant something. "Come by the station. After midnight, when the suits are gone. We'll lay down some tracks."
The hope on those boys' faces could have powered half of Boston. But Nick hesitated.
"Why?" he asked. "Why would you help us?"
I stood up, my knees protesting—even at thirty-five, concrete floors were unforgiving. "Because rock and roll is supposed to be for kids like you. Kids who play in garages because concert halls don't know they exist yet. Because your father's tools on that wall know more about honest work than half the industry executives in this city."
I paused at the garage door, looking back at four boys holding instruments like life preservers. "And because Steven Tyler played his first gig in a garage not much bigger than this one. The only difference between him and you is someone opened a door."
But here's the thing about doors—I thought I was opening one for Nick Johnson. I didn't know I was also closing another.


















Chapter 2X: The Belt Buckles That Started Everything
"But first," I say, stubbing out another cigarette and reaching for the cart I've been saving, "I need to tell you about two belt buckles and how they almost changed the world."
Fall 1974. Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh, both thirteen, both starting eighth grade at Ephraim Curtis Middle School—named after Ephraim Curtis, born in Sudbury in 1642, son of a carpenter who'd migrated from England during the Great Puritan Migration. Like his father, he would become a carpenter, but also a trading post owner, a military officer during King Philip's War, a scout and negotiator who understood that survival sometimes required building bridges between communities that preferred to remain separate.
But Curtis Middle School in 1974 wasn't concerned with historical bridge-building. It was concerned with cafeteria politics, social geography, and the eternal middle school question of which table you belonged at during lunch period.
The cafeteria buzzed with typical institutional chaos that September afternoon. Brown bag lunches from mothers who worked at home, cafeteria trays from families who qualified for subsidized meals, individual milk cartons that kids mixed with Jello like science experiments nobody wanted to grade. But cutting through the ordinary acoustic confusion of twelve and thirteen-year-old social navigation came something that would define the next four years of American music: disco.
"Dancing Queen" poured from someone's transistor radio, the kind of synthesized Euro-pop that made rock and roll feel endangered, that suggested American music was surrendering to production values over authentic expression. Most kids accepted it as background noise, the soundtrack to their surrender to social pressures that required choosing sides, choosing tables, choosing identities that would follow them through high school and beyond.
At a corner table, Jackie Marsh sat alone, clearly the new kid trying to figure out where he fit in geography that made no sense to someone whose family had just moved from Ithaca, New York to suburban Massachusetts. His father's promotion had brought them from the university town where everything revolved around Cornell, where professors' kids mixed naturally regardless of their parents' bank accounts, where intellectual curiosity mattered more than ZIP codes. But Sudbury was different. Sudbury had rules he didn't understand yet.
He was smaller than he'd become but already carried himself with the careful precision that comes from understanding that every movement was being evaluated by kids who'd known each other since kindergarten. He proudly wore a brown leather belt with a gold script "QUEEN" buckle—not fashion statement but musical manifesto, a Christmas gift from his aunt who lived in London, who'd seen them at Hammersmith Odeon and understood that Freddie Mercury was redefining what rock stars could be, what music could accomplish when it stopped worrying about rules and started making its own.
Nick Johnson entered the cafeteria scanning for a place to sit, wiry and already showing the restless energy that would define his relationship with authority, his approach to music, his eventual understanding that authenticity couldn't be taught but could be recognized when it appeared. His black leather belt sported a chrome buckle with "AEROSMITH" in gothic letters—polished to mirror brightness, catching fluorescent light like a signal flare. He'd bought it with lawn-mowing money at Spencer's in the mall, the same weekend he'd spent his birthday cash on "Rocks," their latest album that proved rock and roll was still dangerous, still real, still the sound of America when it stopped pretending to be civilized.
The sun streaming through cafeteria windows caught Nick's buckle like a spotlight, and for a brief instant the reflection blinded Jackie. He covered his eyes with his forearm until the metal stopped broadcasting light across institutional linoleum. As Nick walked closer, searching for empty space at tables that seemed designed to exclude rather than welcome, their eyes met.
Not because they recognized each other—they were strangers navigating social territory neither fully understood. They met because they both noticed each other's belt buckles at exactly the same moment, both registering musical allegiances that suggested they might understand each other despite coming from different quadrants, different economic realities, different assumptions about what constituted normal family life.
Nick approached with confidence masking nervousness, the social calculation that required working-class kids to project strength even when uncertain about reception.
"Queen?" he said, pointing at Jackie's buckle with the directness that comes from understanding that subtlety was luxury his background couldn't afford. "Seriously?"
"What's wrong with Queen?" Jackie replied, defensive but curious, his voice carrying genuine interest rather than automatic hostility, suggesting he'd been waiting for someone to engage with his musical choices rather than dismiss them as pretentious foreign nonsense.
"Nothing wrong," Nick continued, grinning while displaying his own buckle with the pride that comes from representing something larger than individual preference. "Just... Aerosmith's better."
Jackie stood, energy building like electrical charge before thunderstorm, understanding that this conversation mattered more than typical cafeteria small talk. "Better? Have you heard 'Bohemian Rhapsody'?"
"Have you heard 'Dream On'?" Nick matched his energy, both boys recognizing that they'd stumbled into genuine debate rather than social posturing, that musical preference was revealing something more significant than taste.
They stared at each other while "Le Freak" blasted from someone's radio, the disco rhythm providing ironic soundtrack to confrontation that had nothing to do with synthesized dance music and everything to do with authentic expression versus manufactured entertainment.
"Can we agree that disco sucks?" Jackie said, gesturing toward the radio with disgust that transcended their Aerosmith-versus-Queen disagreement, that suggested common ground might exist beneath surface opposition.
"Oh my God, finally someone who gets it!" Nick replied, immediate bonding replacing musical competition. "It's like... music without soul. Music designed by committees who never understood why rock and roll mattered in the first place."
"Exactly!" Jackie's excitement carried the relief that comes from finding someone who understood that music was more than entertainment, more than background noise, more than social soundtrack. "It's everything that's wrong with American culture—artificial, manufactured, designed to make people dance rather than think."
And in that moment of shared disgust for disco's commercial manipulation, two boys from different worlds discovered they might have more in common than their belt buckles suggested. Not just musical preferences, but musical values. Not just favorite bands, but understanding that rock and roll represented something worth defending against cultural forces that prioritized sales over authenticity.
"I'm Nick," he said, extending his hand like they were sealing a treaty. "You're the new kid, right?"
"Jackie. From Ithaca," Jackie corrected, taking the handshake and finding it firm, honest, nothing like the limp greetings he'd grown used to from his father's faculty parties.
"Ithaca," Nick repeated, testing the word. "College town, right? Cornell?"
"Yeah. My dad teaches there. Psychology." Jackie paused, waiting for the usual reaction—either intimidation or dismissal—but Nick just nodded like professors' kids were normal occurrence in his world.
"Cool. So... Queen versus Aerosmith," Nick said, settling into the conversation like they had all the time in the world. "You play anything, Jackie from Ithaca?"
"Piano. Guitar. A little violin."
"Jesus," Nick laughed, but not meanly. "What are you, the von Trapp family?"

"Jackie. From Ithaca," Jackie corrected, taking the handshake and finding it firm, honest, nothing like the limp greetings he'd grown used to from his father's faculty parties.

And so began the Great Debate of 1974, conducted in whispers during lunch, continued walking between classes, resumed in the weeks that followed—a rolling argument that covered every possible angle: technical skill versus raw power, British sophistication versus American authenticity, Freddie Mercury's four-octave range versus Steven Tyler's raw emotion.
Jackie's father was Dr. Richard Marsh, economics professor at Boston University, the kind of academic who wrote scholarly papers about working-class neighborhoods he'd never lived in, who could analyze the economic geography of Sudbury's four quadrants without understanding that those invisible lines actually shaped real lives, real friendships, real possibilities.
By March 1975, their friendship had moved beyond cafeteria debates to garage sessions that proved Queen's precision and Aerosmith's raw power weren't musical opposites but complementary forces that could create something neither band had achieved alone. They'd spend afternoons in Dan Johnson's converted garage, trying to crack the code of what made great rock and roll transcendent rather than merely entertaining. Taking Queen songs and making them more American, taking Aerosmith songs and making them more sophisticated, finding the place where British theatricality met Massachusetts authenticity.
The middle school announced its annual talent show: May 15th, 1975. The same competition where Karen Bowers had won first place for four consecutive years with flawless renditions of Broadway standards that showcased technical proficiency while revealing nothing about the person performing them.
Karen Bowers: the princess of Ephraim Curtis Middle School, daughter of the music teacher at the elementary school, blessed with perfect pitch, classical training, and the kind of confidence that comes from never having been surprised by failure. She sang like she was auditioning for roles she was destined to win, performed like she was doing everyone a favor by sharing her obvious talent.
Every year, the same result: Karen first place, some instrumental act second, everyone else grateful for participation ribbons and the chance to tell their parents they'd tried their best.
"But Nick and Jackie had other ideas," I tell the darkness beyond the booth. "They'd been working on something. A secret project. A song that would prove their argument was actually harmony in disguise."
Nick and Jackie decided to collaborate, not because the assignment required it but because their friendship had discovered something that neither could achieve individually. They chose "Under Pressure"—not the eventual Queen-David Bowie collaboration that wouldn't exist for another six years, but their own acoustic version that proved harmony was possible between different musical philosophies, different economic backgrounds, different assumptions about what constituted success.
They practiced in secret, after the official garage sessions, when Dr. Marsh thought Jackie was at the library and Grace Johnson thought Nick was doing homework. Two thirteen-year-olds teaching themselves how to harmonize, how to take turns with the melody, how to make two different musical philosophies into one song.
May 15th, 1975. The night everything changed.
The middle school gymnasium was packed—parents with cameras, kids with attitudes, teachers hoping for the best and expecting chaos. Karen Bowers was scheduled to close the show, the traditional spot for the inevitable winner, performing "Tomorrow" from "Annie" with the kind of polish that made everyone else feel like amateurs.
Nick and Jackie were somewhere in the middle, two kids with acoustic guitars, listed in the program simply as "Johnson & Marsh." Nobody expected much. The audience was still settling in, still talking, still treating this like background entertainment before the real show.
Then they started playing.
The first chord cut through the chatter like a knife through silk. Not loud—they didn't have amplifiers—but somehow commanding in a way that made people stop talking and start listening. The opening notes of "Under Pressure" stripped down to just two voices and two guitars, but rebuilt into something that was both familiar and completely new.
Jackie took the first verse, his classically trained voice carrying the melody with precision and unexpected emotion. Nick joined on the chorus, his rougher voice providing the counterpoint that made the harmony complete rather than perfect. They'd found the spaces between Queen's sophistication and Aerosmith's authenticity, creating something that belonged to neither band and both boys.
The audience was silent now. Even the teachers had stopped their sidebar conversations. Something was happening that nobody had expected, something that transcended the usual talent show categories of cute, impressive, or awkward.
The performance lasted three minutes and twenty-one seconds. When they finished, the silence stretched for what felt like forever before the applause began—not polite appreciation, but genuine surprise, the kind of response that comes when people witness something they didn't know was possible.
They won. It wasn't even close.
Karen Bowers delivered her usual flawless performance of "Tomorrow," but the crowd had already given their hearts to something they'd never heard before: rock and roll performed with acoustic guitars and middle school voices, proving that authenticity could trump technique, that collaboration could create something bigger than individual talent.
That demo tape sits in my personal collection now, waiting for the right moment. Not yet. First, Boston needs to understand what connected them, what pulled them apart, why their story matters more than the rock and roll history books that never recorded their names.
But that recording—that three minutes and twenty-one seconds when Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh proved that chrome Aerosmith and gold Queen weren't enemies but different voices in the same song—that's the proof that everything I'm about to tell you actually mattered, actually happened, actually represented possibility rather than just teenage dreams that died when real life demanded choosing sides.
The belt buckles that started everything: working-class authenticity meeting privileged rebellion, a kid from Ithaca discovering Massachusetts, the beginning of a friendship that should have changed music history but instead became casualty of American class warfare disguised as high school competition.







Chapter 3A: Best Friends
"October fifteenth, 1974," I say, adjusting the microphone arm with the precision that comes from thirty years of radio muscle memory, "the day everything changed."
The incident started in the hallway between third and fourth periods at Ephraim Curtis Middle School, where the fluorescent lights hummed like dying insects and the polished linoleum reflected faces in ghostly doubles. These corridors had witnessed a thousand small cruelties, a thousand moments when children learned that survival required choosing sides, that friendship was a luxury some kids couldn't afford and others weren't allowed to keep.
Tommy Morrison—no relation to the judge's family, just a working-class kid whose size and early growth spurt had earned him social status through intimidation—had been watching Jackie and Nick's lunch table alliance with the calculating patience of a predator who understood that disruption required perfect timing.
"Hey, rich boy," Tommy called out, his voice cutting through the hallway noise like chalk on a blackboard, designed to stop conversation and demand audience. "You lost? Your people eat over by the windows."
Jackie stopped walking. At twelve years old, he'd already learned that running only delayed the inevitable, that middle school hierarchies required public enforcement, witnesses to humiliation. The hallway smelled of industrial disinfectant and adolescent anxiety, that particular cocktail of fear and bravado that seasoned every middle school corridor in America.
"I'm fine where I am," Jackie replied, his voice steady despite the adrenaline that comes from recognizing danger, from understanding that this conversation would determine whether his friendship with Nick Johnson was strong enough to survive social pressure.
"Doesn't look fine to me," Tommy continued, stepping closer with movements that suggested escalation, the kind of choreographed aggression that bullies perfected through daily practice. "Looks like you're slumming."
That's when Nick appeared.
Not dramatically, not with theatrical timing, just the natural emergence of someone who'd been walking to the same destination and had encountered a situation that required response. Nick was smaller than Tommy, carried himself with the careful neutrality that working-class kids learned for survival, but there was something in his approach—a quiet authority—that suggested he understood exactly what was happening and had already decided what he was going to do about it.
"Problem here?" Nick asked, his voice carrying that particular calm that comes from growing up in neighborhoods where backing down wasn't an option.
"No problem for you, Johnson," Tommy replied, confident that class solidarity would prevent Nick from defending someone from the elevated section. "Just having a conversation."
"Seems like a problem for me if you're bothering my friend," Nick said, and the word 'friend' hung in the hallway air like a declaration, heavier than either boy had expected.
Tommy paused, recalculated. Nick Johnson wasn't known for confrontation, but he also wasn't someone who backed down when something mattered to him. And apparently Jackie Marsh mattered to him.
"Your friend?" Tommy asked, genuine surprise flickering across his face.
"Yeah," Nick replied simply, stepping closer to Jackie. "My friend."
Tommy looked between them, processed the recognition that this confrontation had become more complicated than anticipated. "Whatever. Your loss, Johnson."
He walked away, and Jackie and Nick stood in the empty hallway, understanding that something important had just happened, that their belt buckle alliance had become something deeper, something worth defending.
"Thanks," Jackie said simply.
"No problem," Nick replied. "That's what friends do."
And in that moment, their friendship became real.

The autumn of 1974 belonged to discovery. While other kids their age worried about homework and television schedules, Nick and Jackie had found something more compelling: the alchemy that happened when two different approaches to music collided and created something neither could achieve alone.
The garage on Dutton Road became their laboratory. Dan Johnson's tools still hung on the pegboard in perfect order, but now the workbench held guitar picks and sheet music, chord progressions written in Jackie's careful handwriting next to Nick's rough sketches of amplifier modifications. The space smelled of motor oil and sawdust, but it also carried the ghost of music, the resonance of hours spent learning that Aerosmith's power and Queen's precision weren't opposites but complementary forces.
"Play it again," Jackie would say, perched on a paint can turned upside down, his ten-speed leaning against the wall beside Nick's dirt bike. "But this time, try it with more space between the chords."
Nick's guitar—that twenty-five-dollar pawn shop miracle—sang differently in Jackie's presence. Where Nick had learned to play with working-class efficiency, getting the most sound from the least equipment, Jackie brought classical structure, the understanding that music could be architecture, something built with precision and patience.
The first song they wrote together happened by accident. Nick was playing the opening riff to "Dream On," letting it hang in the garage air like morning mist, when Jackie started humming a harmony that shouldn't have worked but did. The melody line he added—trained by years of piano lessons but shaped by something more intuitive—transformed Aerosmith's darkness into something hopeful, something that acknowledged pain but refused to surrender to it.
"What if we wrote something ourselves?" Jackie asked one November afternoon, his breath visible in the unheated garage. "Not covering other people's songs, but creating something that's ours?"
Nick stopped playing, considered the idea with the seriousness that twelve-year-old boys bring to proposals that might change everything. "You think we could?"
"I think we have to," Jackie replied, and there was something in his voice—a certainty that came from recognizing possibility—that made Nick nod before he'd fully processed what they were agreeing to attempt.
They called it "Different Streets, Same Dreams," though the title came months later, after they'd discovered what the song was actually about. The music began with Nick's riff—simple, repetitive, hypnotic—while Jackie built harmony around it, piano lines that conversation with the guitar rather than competing for space.
The lyrics emerged slowly, written on notebook paper during study halls and lunch periods, refined during garage sessions that stretched until Grace Johnson called them in for dinner. They sang about growing up in the same town but different worlds, about finding friendship across boundaries that weren't supposed to be crossed, about the possibility that music could create unity where society insisted on division.
By December, they were performing it for audiences of one: Grace Johnson, who would pause in hanging laundry to listen through the garage window, her expression shifting from maternal tolerance to genuine surprise at what these boys were creating together.
"That's beautiful," she told them one afternoon, standing in the garage doorway with her Friendly's uniform covered by Dan's old work jacket. "Really beautiful. You boys have something special."
Jackie's parents were more complicated. Dr. Richard Marsh would nod politely when Jackie mentioned his musical collaboration, but his questions always focused on technical aspects—music theory, compositional structure—rather than the emotional content that actually mattered. Mrs. Marsh worried about time management, about balancing creative pursuits with academic responsibilities, about the wisdom of investing too much energy in a friendship that crossed social boundaries she understood better than her twelve-year-old son.
"The Johnson boy seems nice enough," she would say, the phrase carrying subtle emphasis that suggested niceness might not be sufficient for long-term social viability. "But you need to remember that you have obligations, expectations."
Jackie learned to navigate these conversations carefully, to emphasize the musical education aspects of his garage sessions while downplaying the friendship elements that seemed to make his parents uncomfortable. He was beginning to understand that his family's acceptance of Nick would always be conditional, always dependent on whether the relationship served purposes they could approve.
But in the garage, none of that mattered. In the garage, they were just two kids who had discovered that music could transcend everything—class, expectations, the social geography that told them they weren't supposed to be friends. In the garage, they were building something that belonged only to them, something that proved harmony was possible if you cared more about the sound than the rules.
Winter turned to spring, and their friendship deepened into something that felt unbreakable. They rode bikes together through the conservation land trails, explored neighborhoods neither would have discovered alone, found swimming holes and secret places where middle school kids could escape adult supervision. Jackie would navigate his ten-speed through Professor territory, past houses filled with books and intellectual conversation, then meet Nick at the conservation land boundary where working-class practicality met academic aspiration.
Their musical collaboration evolved beyond simple covers and original songs into something more complex: an understanding that different backgrounds could strengthen rather than weaken their creative partnership. Nick's intuitive approach to rhythm complemented Jackie's theoretical knowledge of harmony. Jackie's classical training provided structure for Nick's experimental instincts.
They spent entire afternoons in the garage, learning not just each other's songs but each other's musical languages, discovering that Queen and Aerosmith weren't opposing philosophies but different approaches to the same fundamental truth: that rock and roll could be both cerebral and visceral, both careful and wild.
"You know what I like about this?" Nick said one April afternoon, setting down his guitar after they'd finished running through "Different Streets, Same Dreams" for what might have been the hundredth time. "It doesn't sound like either of us alone. It sounds like something new."
Jackie nodded, understanding exactly what Nick meant. Their collaboration had created something that transcended their individual abilities, something that could only exist when they played together, something that proved the whole could be greater than its parts.
By spring 1975, they weren't just friends who played music together. They were musical partners who happened to be best friends, two boys who had discovered that the most powerful harmonies come from voices that shouldn't blend but do, that the most beautiful music emerges from the spaces between different worlds.
If only they could have stayed twelve years old forever. If only the world outside the garage had been as willing to accept their harmony as the music was. If only America in 1975 had believed that different kinds of kids could create something beautiful together without choosing sides.
But twelve-year-old boys don't stay twelve forever. And garages, no matter how magical, can't keep the outside world away indefinitely.
The spring of 1975 was ending, and with it, the last season when their friendship would be simple, uncomplicated, free from the pressures that would soon force them to choose between music and social survival, between harmony and the hierarchies that insisted some friendships were too dangerous to survive.






Chapter 3: The Other Side of Hop Brook
"Now," I continue on air, checking the clock—three hours and twenty-seven minutes left of WBCN—"let me tell you about the prince in the tower."
The same week I discovered Nick Johnson's garage, I was invited to a very different kind of performance. Dr. Richard Marsh had called the station personally, which was unusual. University professors typically had their secretaries handle such mundane tasks. But Dr. Marsh wanted to make sure I understood the significance of his son's recital.
"Jackie trained at the New England Conservatory prep division," he'd said, his voice carrying that particular Boston Brahmin accent that turns 'car' into 'cah' but somehow makes it sound aristocratic rather than working class. "He's being considered for early admission to Juilliard."
I'd wanted to decline. Classical recitals weren't my beat. But something in his voice—a father's pride mixed with something else, something almost like fear—made me agree.
The Marsh house stood on the good side of Hop Brook like it had been placed there by a decorator: Colonial perfection, black shutters against white clapboard, lawn so green it looked painted. You could walk from Nick Johnson's garage to this front door in twelve minutes, but you'd cross an invisible border that might as well have been made of barbed wire.
"The thing about that recital," I tell my audience, fading down The Cars' "Just What I Needed," "was that it showed me the other half of the tragedy."
The living room had been transformed into a concert space. Folding chairs in perfect rows, a rented Steinway that cost more than Mary Johnson made in a year, champagne chilling in silver buckets for the afterward. The audience was prep school parents and university faculty, the kind of people who applauded with their fingertips and discussed summer houses on the Cape between selections.
Jackie Marsh entered in a tuxedo that fit like money, seventeen years old but carrying himself like someone who'd been preparing for this moment since birth. He was beautiful in that way that good nutrition and orthodontics and SAT tutors can manufacture. But when he sat at the piano, something changed.
He opened with Chopin, because of course he did. The notes were perfect—every one struck with precision that spoke of thousands of hours of practice, of lessons that started before memory, of parents who considered anything less than excellence a character flaw. The audience nodded appreciatively, already composing the compliments they'd deliver with their champagne.
But I was watching Jackie's face, and I saw something his parents missed: he was dying in there. Behind the technical perfection was a boy playing someone else's definition of music, speaking someone else's language with flawless pronunciation but no soul.
Then something shifted. Between the Chopin and the obligatory Bach, Jackie paused. His father cleared his throat—a warning about maintaining proper recital pacing. But Jackie wasn't listening to his father. He was listening to something else.
His fingers found different keys. The opening notes of "Bohemian Rhapsody" emerged from that Steinway like escapees from prison.
The room froze. You could hear pearls clutching themselves.
"Is this the real life?" Jackie sang, his classically trained voice doing things Freddie Mercury would have killed for. "Is this just fantasy?"
Here's what that room full of culture didn't understand: Jackie Marsh wasn't defiling their temple. He was consecrating it. He took their precious Steinway and made it confess that rock and roll was just classical music that had learned to dance.
For four minutes and fifty-five seconds, Jackie Marsh was free. His hands found harmonies that shouldn't have been possible on a solo piano, his voice soared through octaves that made the champagne glasses vibrate. He turned that stuffy living room into Wembley Stadium, and for once in his carefully managed life, he wasn't anyone's trophy son.
When he finished, the silence was different from the holy quiet in Nick's garage. This was the silence of scandal, of proper people trying to process impropriety.
Dr. Marsh was the first to speak. "That was... unexpected."
"That was rock and roll," I said, standing up because someone needed to. "That was your son showing you who he really is."
Jackie looked at me then, really looked, and I saw the same thing I'd seen in Nick Johnson's eyes: a desperate need to be heard as himself, not as someone's projection of who he should be.
"You're Maxanne Sartori," he said. "You played our record."
Our record. Not Aerosmith's or Queen's, but our record, like every kid who'd ever heard their dreams on the radio and thought maybe, just maybe, they mattered.
"I play music," I corrected. "Sometimes it comes from famous bands. Sometimes it comes from kids in garages. And sometimes," I looked directly at Dr. Marsh, "it comes from piano prodigies who understand that Chopin would have loved electric guitars if he'd lived to see them."
I gave Jackie my card—the same one I'd given Nick three days earlier. "Come by the station. Bring whatever music lives in you, not what lives in your parents' expectations."
But here's the cruelest part of this story: I thought I was saving two boys from the boxes they'd been born into. I didn't realize I was setting them on a collision course that would prove boxes are easier to escape than each other.
Back in 2024, I drain my bourbon and pour another. My engineer looks concerned—probably worried I'll pass out before the final sign-off. But he doesn't understand that some stories require anesthesia, especially the ones where you were supposed to be the hero but turned out to be just another adult who didn't see the train coming until it had already crashed.
"Two boys," I tell whoever's still listening out there in the dark. "Two sides of Hop Brook. Both prisoners of expectations, both gifted with the kind of talent that makes the universe pay attention. They should have been allies. Hell, they had been allies, once upon a time in middle school."
I check the clock: three hours and twelve minutes left.
"But I'm getting ahead of myself. Before I tell you about the battle, I need to tell you about the war. And every war needs its recruiting station. In this case, it was a bulletin board in the Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School music room, and a simple piece of paper that would turn a competition into a crusade…"





Chapter 4: The Bulletin Board That Started a War
"March fifth, 1979," I say into the microphone, cueing up Boston's "More Than a Feeling" because sometimes you need the obvious choice. "A Monday morning that felt like any other Monday morning, except for one piece of paper thumb-tacked to a bulletin board."
I wasn't there, of course. But I've reconstructed this moment from so many tellings—Nick's bitter version, Jackie's defensive one, their music teacher Bob Wentworth's regretful account—that I can see it like I was standing in that music room, breathing in the smell of valve oil and teenage ambition.
The Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School music room occupied a corner of the building where acoustics went to die. Cinder block walls painted institution green, fluorescent lights that buzzed in B-flat, instrument lockers that hadn't been properly tuned since the Ford administration. But on the bulletin board between the jazz band schedule and a faded poster for the Boston Symphony, someone had tacked up a fresh white announcement.
BATTLE OF THE BANDS March 15, 1979 Open to all regional high schools Grand Prize: $500 and professional recording session Sponsored by WBCN "The Rock of Boston"
"What you need to understand," I tell my dwindling late-night audience, "is that five hundred dollars in 1979 was real money. For a kid like Nick Johnson, it was three months of his mother's grocery budget. For a kid like Jackie Marsh, it was... well, it was validation that cost couldn't buy."
Bob Wentworth found them both standing there at 7:47 AM, staring at that poster from opposite sides of the room like gunslingers who'd accidentally chosen the same saloon. Wentworth was one of those music teachers who'd given up dreams of symphony halls for steady paychecks and summers off, but he'd never quite lost the ability to hear potential in the noise teenagers made.
"Gentlemen," he'd said, because Wentworth called all his students gentlemen or ladies, even the ones who'd never live up to either title. "I see you've noticed the announcement."
Neither boy moved. They just kept staring at that poster like it might reveal secrets if they looked hard enough.
"We'll need to submit band names by Friday," Wentworth continued, trying to navigate the minefield he suddenly realized he was standing in. "Performance slots are limited."
That's when Nick finally looked at Jackie. Really looked at him for the first time in three years. And what he saw made his hands clench into fists.
Jackie had grown into everything Nick had suspected he'd become: tall, clean, wearing clothes that fit like they'd been born on his body. His hair fell across his forehead in that carelessly perfect way that took serious money to maintain. He looked like the kind of kid who'd never wondered if the electricity would stay on till the end of the month.
And Jackie looked back at Nick, seeing what he'd feared: someone who'd hardened instead of grown, whose talent had been sharpened into a weapon by circumstance. Nick's clothes were clean but worn, his boots resoled with duct tape, his hands already showing the calluses of someone who worked for a living. He looked like the kind of kid who'd never had the luxury of treating music as art instead of escape.
"I suppose," Wentworth said into the silence that stretched between them like a tripwire, "you'll both be entering."
"With a Queen tribute," Jackie said, his voice carrying that prep school pronunciation that turned statements into declarations.
"Aerosmith," Nick countered, the single word loaded with three years of stored resentment.
Wentworth sighed the sigh of a man who'd just watched a fuse being lit. "The competition is meant to celebrate musical diversity, not—"
"It's about proving who's real," Nick interrupted, still looking at Jackie. "Who understands what rock and roll actually means."
"It's about excellence," Jackie shot back. "About showing that rock can be more than three chords and teenage angst."
Back in the booth, I pause to light another cigarette—yes, I'm smoking in the studio on our last night, sue me—and think about how many wars have started with two people saying the same thing in different languages.
"What happened next," I continue on air, "spread through that high school like flood water finding every possible crack."
By lunch, the entire school knew about the challenge. It wasn't just Nick Johnson versus Jackie Marsh anymore. It was working class versus privilege, garage rock versus conservatory training, Aerosmith versus Queen. Kids who'd never cared about music suddenly had opinions about production values and authenticity.
The Lincoln kids—the ones from the even smaller, even poorer town that shared the regional high school—lined up behind Nick. The Sudbury kids split along economic lines: the Hop Brook divide made visible in cafeteria seating charts. The few students from Wayland who got bused in watched from the sidelines, smart enough to recognize a civil war when they saw one brewing.
"But here's what kills me," I tell my audience, ashing into a WBCN coffee mug that's older than my engineer. "Nobody knew what I knew. Nobody knew that in 1975, Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh had won the middle school talent show as a duo. They'd stood on that gymnasium stage, thirteen years old and fearless, and made their guitars have a conversation that left not a dry eye in the house."
I dig through my pile of carts—unorganized, because who's going to fire me now?—and find the one I labeled "Evidence" all those years ago.
"I still have that recording," I say, clicking it into place. "Ninety-three seconds of what could have been. Listen."
The tape is degraded, recorded on someone's boom box from the back of the gym. But through the hiss and distance, you can hear it: two boys finding the frequency where Queen meets Aerosmith, where technical precision dances with raw emotion, where harmony isn't just musical but human.
And then it cuts off. Just... ends. Like someone hit stop in the middle of a sentence, in the middle of a possibility.
"Ninety-three seconds," I repeat, pulling the cart. "That's all the evidence that remains of Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh as allies instead of enemies. By the time they walked out of that music room on March fifth, 1979, they'd chosen their weapons and their armies. The Battle of the Bands wasn't going to be a competition. It was going to be a war."
My engineer holds up three fingers. Three hours left.
"After the break," I say, though there won't be any breaks, there won't be any commercials, there won't be any tomorrow for WBCN, "I'll tell you about how a friendly competition turned an entire town into a battlefield. But first, here's the song that became Nick Johnson's anthem, the one that explained everything he couldn't put into words..."
I cue up Aerosmith's "Walk This Way" and lean back in my chair. Outside, Boston glitters like a circuit board, all connections and currents. Somewhere out there, Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh are in their sixties now, probably listening. Probably remembering. Probably wondering, like I am, if there's a universe where that ninety-three-second tape kept playing, where harmony won over division, where America chose the frequency that unites over the static that separates.
But that's not the universe we live in. In our universe, the Battle of the Bands was ten days away, and everyone was already choosing sides.













Chapter 5: The Brook That Divided More Than Land
"Let me tell you about Hop Brook," I say into the microphone, watching my bourbon swirl like amber history. "Because you can't understand what happened next without understanding the geography of division."
I fade up James Taylor's "Sweet Baby James"—not rock and roll, I know, but James understood Massachusetts in a way that mattered. He understood how water could divide more than land.
"The brook ran through Sudbury like God had drawn a line with his finger, saying 'This side for the comfortable, that side for the strivers.' But water doesn't respect property values. Fish don't check bank accounts before taking bait. And in 1979, kids from both sides of that brook would stand knee-deep in the same water, casting for the same bass, pretending for a few hours that the border didn't exist."
I remember driving along Old Sudbury Road one afternoon, maybe four days after the bulletin board announcement, and seeing them—Nick Johnson on the east bank, Jackie Marsh on the west, both fishing in that careful way of people pretending not to notice each other. Their lines crossed in the middle of the stream, tangled for a moment before they wordlessly worked them free.
"But the real border," I continue, "wasn't the water. It was what happened in the woods on either side."
The conservation land between the two Sudburys should have been neutral territory—hundred acres of pine and oak, old growth that remembered when all of Massachusetts was forest. But by 1979, those woods bore scars that told the real story.
On Nick's side, dirt bike trails wound through the trees like varicose veins. The mini-bikes and dirt bikes had carved paths so deep that grass would never grow there again. Every weekend, the air filled with two-stroke exhaust and the angry hornet sound of engines, kids in hand-me-down helmets finding freedom in speed and mud.
On Jackie's side, those same trails were seen as wounds. The parents in those colonial houses wrote letters to the town council about noise ordinances and environmental damage. They put up "No Motorized Vehicles" signs that were routinely torn down. They talked about property values the way other people talked about virtue.
"Here's what you need to understand," I tell my dwindling audience, maybe a few hundred insomniacs and third-shifters still tuning in to WBCN's death rattle. "Those mini-bike trails weren't just about recreation. They were about different definitions of freedom."
For kids like Nick Johnson, those bikes meant mobility that didn't depend on parents who worked double shifts. They meant access to parts of town that public transportation didn't reach. They meant the kind of dangerous joy that rich kids could buy with skiing trips to Vermont but poor kids had to find in their own backyards.
For kids like Jackie Marsh, the woods were supposed to be pristine, a buffer between their careful world and the chaos beyond. The bikes were intrusions, reminders that borders could be crossed, that nowhere was really safe from the sound of people who didn't know their place.
"Four days after the Battle of the Bands announcement," I continue, lighting another cigarette because who's going to stop me, "something happened in those woods that should have warned everyone about what was coming."
Back in the booth, my engineer looks up from his phone. Even he can sense when a story is about to turn.
It was a Tuesday afternoon, unseasonably warm for March. Nick and three of his Lincoln boys were riding the trails, working off the frustration of another day of dividing the school into camps. They'd been practicing every night, turning Aerosmith covers into something rawer, angrier, more honest than the originals.
Jackie was in the woods too, but on foot. He'd been walking the paths since childhood, knew every tree, every stone wall that marked some colonial farmer's ambition. He went there to think, to escape the pressure of being Richard Marsh's son, to hear music in wind through pine needles.
They met where the main trail crossed Hop Brook via a wooden bridge that was older than either of their families' time in Sudbury. Nick was leading, riding a Honda CR125 that he'd rebuilt from parts, the engine singing that two-stroke song that sounds like defiance.
Jackie was standing on the bridge, right in the middle, looking down at the water like it might tell him something important.
Neither one would back up.
"You need to move," Nick said, killing the engine but not dismounting.
"It's a walking bridge," Jackie replied, not looking up. "Always has been."
Behind Nick, the other bikes idled, their riders sensing the tension but not understanding it was about more than right of way.
"Lot of things used to be different," Nick said. "Doesn't mean they can't change."
Jackie finally looked up, and for a moment—I swear this is true, they both told me later, separately—they almost smiled at each other. Almost remembered being thirteen and fearless and making music in Nick's garage before the world taught them they were supposed to be enemies.
Then a voice called from the woods on Jackie's side. "Everything okay here?"
Dr. Richard Marsh emerged from the trail, dressed for his afternoon constitutional in pressed khakis and a Boston University windbreaker. He took in the scene—his son facing down four kids on dirt bikes—and his face set into the expression of a man who'd found evidence supporting his worst assumptions.
"These boys bothering you, son?"
And there it was. These boys. Not classmates, not neighbors, not even kids. These boys. The pronoun that built walls.
"No, Dad," Jackie said, but something in his voice had changed. The almost-smile was gone, replaced by the mask he wore at recitals. "I was just explaining about the walking bridge."
Dr. Marsh looked at Nick, and in that look was every letter to the town council, every property value concern, every fear that the wrong element was getting too close to what mattered.
"Perhaps you boys could find another trail," he said. "One more... appropriate for your vehicles."
Nick's hands tightened on the handlebars. Behind him, someone gunned their engine—not threatening, exactly, but not not threatening either.
"It's a public trail," Nick said. "We got as much right—"
"Rights," Dr. Marsh interrupted, "come with responsibilities. Something you might understand when you're older."
I pause in my narration to take another sip of bourbon. Even forty-five years later, the casual cruelty of that moment makes me angry. The way adults could sharpen class differences into weapons, could teach kids to hate each other with such pedestrian efficiency.
"What happened next," I continue, "set the stage for everything that followed."
Jackie moved. Stepped aside, gave ground, chose peace over principle. The gesture was small—just a few steps to the right—but in the economy of teenage pride, it was everything.
Nick could have just ridden past. Could have taken the victory and moved on. But something in the way Dr. Marsh stood there, protective and possessive and so goddamn sure of his righteousness, made that impossible.
Instead, Nick revved his engine and rode across the bridge at full throttle. The old wood groaned, the sound echoing across the water like a scream. His friends followed, four bikes turning the walking bridge into a statement about whose freedom mattered more.
When they were gone, the silence in the woods felt like aftermath. Dr. Marsh put his hand on Jackie's shoulder—not comforting, but claiming.
"Those boys," he said again, and this time the pronoun carried even more weight. "They don't understand boundaries. That's why they'll never amount to anything."
"Dad," Jackie started, but what could he say? That one of those boys had once been his best friend? That they'd made music together that felt like truth? That the boundary wasn't about bridges or trails but about something neither of them had chosen but both were trapped by?
"Come on," Dr. Marsh said. "Your mother's expecting us for dinner."
They walked back toward the big houses, leaving the bridge scarred by tire tracks and the brook running underneath like it always had, indifferent to the human tendency to turn geography into ideology.
Back in 2024, I stub out my cigarette and check the clock. Two hours and forty-three minutes left.
"That was the last time Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh were alone together before the Battle of the Bands," I tell whoever's still listening. "The last chance they had to choose connection over division. But those mini-bike trails and walking paths had already taught them different languages, and Dr. Marsh had reminded them that translation was forbidden."
I cue up the next song—Lynyrd Skynyrd's "Simple Man" because sometimes the South understands New England better than we understand ourselves.
"After the break that will never come, I'll tell you how a high school divided itself into armies, and how two teachers—one who meant well and one who didn't—lit matches near all that gasoline…"

Chapter 6: The Teachers Who Fanned the Flames
"Every tragedy needs its supporting cast," I say, watching Boston's skyline shimmer through the booth window like a fever dream. "In this one, we had two teachers who couldn't have been more different, yet both managed to make everything worse."
I'm thinking about Bob Wentworth, the music teacher who'd seen it all coming and tried to stop it with the intellectual equivalent of a garden hose against a forest fire. And Jim Harrison, the history teacher who saw the division between Nick and Jackie as a teachable moment about American democracy, never understanding he was teaching the wrong lesson.
"March seventh, 1979. Two days before the Battle of the Bands. Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School had split into something that looked like democracy but felt like civil war."
The lunchroom had reorganized itself into territories. Nick's supporters took the tables near the windows facing the parking lot—the ones where you could see the dirt bikes and beat-up cars that meant freedom. Jackie's people claimed the tables near the interior courtyard—the ones with the view of the memorial garden that someone's parents had donated after someone else's parents had complained about the state of the landscaping.
The kids from Lincoln—the ones who'd been bused in from the even smaller, even poorer town—had to choose sides in a war that wasn't theirs but somehow was.
Bob Wentworth watched it all from the faculty table with the kind of expression you see on emergency room doctors—professionally calm but personally horrified. He'd been teaching music for eighteen years, had seen kids fight over girlfriends and starting positions and parking spaces. But this was different. This was about something America pretended didn't exist.
"Mr. Wentworth tried to fix it with music," I tell my audience, laughing but not because it's funny. "He actually thought he could heal the divide with harmony."
It was after school, the jazz band practice that always ran long because Wentworth believed in something he called 'finding the pocket'—that magical moment when individual musicians became a single organism. Nick played guitar in the jazz band, had been since sophomore year. Jackie played piano, had joined junior year when he realized jazz was the respectable rebellion his parents would tolerate.
For two years, they'd managed to exist in the same musical space by carefully not existing in the same personal space. They played together but never talked. They found harmonies in performance but maintained discord in life.
But that Wednesday, with the Battle of the Bands forty-eight hours away and the school ready to explode, Wentworth decided to force a reconciliation.
"Gentlemen," he announced to the seventeen-piece band, "we're going to try something different today. Johnson and Marsh, I want you front and center. We're going to work on a dual arrangement."
The rest of the band exchanged looks—the kind teenagers perfect when adults are about to do something catastrophically naive.
Nick and Jackie moved to the front of the room like prizefighters being told to touch gloves. Nick carried his guitar like a shield. Jackie approached the piano like it might explode.
"Now," Wentworth continued, clutching his conductor's baton like a magic wand that could make this work, "I want you to play 'Take Five.' But I want you to find where Brubeck meets your own... idioms. Johnson, bring the Aerosmith. Marsh, bring the Queen. Show us how jazz can bridge any gap."
In the booth, I laugh again, and this time it is funny, in that way that tragedy becomes funny when you've had enough bourbon and enough years to see the cosmic joke.
"'Take Five' is in 5/4 time," I explain to listeners who might not know. "It's already about being off-balance, about finding beauty in the unexpected. Asking those boys to reimagine it through their battle lenses was like asking them to translate their pain into a language everyone could dance to."
They started to play. Jackie's classical training made the unusual time signature easy—his left hand finding Brubeck's rhythm while his right hand started adding flourishes that were pure Freddie Mercury. Nick listened, found the spaces between Jackie's notes, and started threading Aerosmith's blues-based rock through the jazz structure.
For thirty seconds, it worked. For thirty seconds, Bob Wentworth was right and music could heal anything. The two boys weren't looking at each other, but they were listening, and in that listening was the ghost of what they'd been at thirteen—two kids who could make magic when they stopped thinking about why they shouldn't.
Then Jim Harrison walked in.
Harrison taught AP American History, coached debate, and considered himself a maverick educator because he wore jeans on Fridays and let kids call him "Jim" after school. He was thirty-two, had a master's from Harvard, and believed that everything was a teachable moment if you were just clever enough to frame it right.
"Sounds like democracy in action," he announced, loud enough to break the spell. "The American experiment playing out in real time. You know what this reminds me of? The Lincoln-Douglas debates. Same state, different ideas about what America should be."
Nick's fingers stumbled on the fretboard. Jackie's right hand fell silent.
"Think about it," Harrison continued, warming to his theme because Jim Harrison loved nothing more than the sound of his own relevance. "Two young men, different backgrounds, competing visions. This Battle of the Bands? It's not just about music. It's about whose America we're living in."
"Jim," Wentworth warned, but Harrison was on a roll.
"Johnson here represents the Jacksonian tradition—man of the people, authentic democracy, rough edges and all. Marsh is more Jeffersonian—educated, refined, believing that excellence elevates everyone. Both American traditions! Both valid!"
"We're just playing music," Nick said, but his voice carried danger.
"Are you though?" Harrison asked, doing that thing teachers do when they think they're Socrates. "Or are you playing out the eternal American tension between—"
"Between the kids who matter and the kids who don't?" Nick interrupted. "Between the ones whose parents donate memorial gardens and the ones whose parents die in industrial accidents?"
The jazz band had gone completely silent. Seventeen teenagers watching a car crash in slow motion.
"That's not—" Harrison started.
"You want to make us a lesson?" Nick continued, standing up. "Fine. Here's your lesson. Jackie's going to Juilliard or Yale or wherever kids like him go. I'm going to work construction like my dad, maybe get lucky and die quick like him too. That American enough for you?"
"Nick," Jackie said quietly, and it was the first time he'd said Nick's name in two years.
"What? You want to pretend this is about music? Like we're equals 'cause we can both play some notes?" Nick was unpacking years of resentment in real time. "Your dad made it pretty clear on that bridge. These boys don't understand boundaries."
"That's not what I—" Jackie started.
"Yeah? Then why'd you move? Why'd you step aside like a good little prince letting the peasants pass?"
"Because I was trying to avoid this!" Jackie gestured at the space between them, at the unbridgeable everything. "Because I thought maybe we could get through one week without—"
"Without what? Without admitting that Hop Brook might as well be the Atlantic? Without pretending that your Queen covers aren't just you slumming with the real people?"
"Slumming?" Jackie's voice cracked. "You think this is slumming? You think I want—" He stopped, looked around the room full of their classmates, their teachers, their judges. "You know what? Forget it. You want a war? Fine. Friday night, we'll see whose America wins."
He left then, didn't storm out so much as evacuated with dignity intact. The piano bench scraped against the floor, a harsh note that hung in the air.
Harrison looked around the room at the wreckage of his teachable moment. "I was just trying to provide historical context—"
"Jim," Wentworth said, and his voice carried the exhaustion of every teacher who'd ever watched good intentions detonate. "Get out."
Back in 2024, I drain my bourbon and pour the last of the bottle. My hands shake slightly—age or emotion or just the accumulation of forty-five years of watching America eat its young.
"Bob Wentworth tried to build a bridge with music," I tell the darkness beyond the booth. "Jim Harrison tried to build one with intellect. Both failed because you can't bridge a divide when the powerful benefit from the gap."
I check the clock: two hours and twenty-one minutes left.
"When we come back from the silence that comes for all of us, I'll tell you about the final rehearsals. How Nick Johnson and his band of Lincoln boys turned Aerosmith into something like prayer. How Jackie Marsh and his recruited orchestra made Queen sound like revolution. And how a girl named Patty Lally from Concord was about to show them both what they were missing..."
The ON AIR sign pulses like a wound that won't heal. Outside, Boston sleeps fitfully, dreaming of what it was, forgetting what it's become. And somewhere in the dark, two old men in their sixties are listening to their story being told, maybe wondering—like I wonder—if there's still time to find that bridge they burned, if there's still a frequency where their voices might harmonize again.
But probably not. Some bridges, once burned, illuminate only what we've lost.












Chapter 7: The Gathering Storm
"Thursday, March fourteenth," I announce to the thinning darkness. "Twenty-four hours before the Battle of the Bands. If you've ever been in a small town before a big storm, you know that feeling—air pressure dropping, birds going silent, everyone pretending normal while bracing for impact."
I cue up Peter Frampton's "Do You Feel Like We Do"—the live version with the talk box, because sometimes you need technology to help you speak the unspeakable.
Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School vibrated with the kind of energy that made teachers reach for their coffee and administrators check their insurance policies. The parking lot told the story: on one side, cars with "Aerosmith" spray-painted on windows, on the other, neat "Queen" bumper stickers. Some brave souls had tried to remain neutral, but neutrality had become its own statement.
"What you have to understand," I continue, fishing out a fresh pack of cigarettes from my purse—yes, I still carry a purse, sue me for being seventy-three and set in my ways—"is that this had stopped being about two bands. This was about two different ideas of what mattered."
Nick Johnson's band—they'd finally settled on calling themselves "Walking the Line" which was either about Johnny Cash or about class boundaries, depending on who you asked—had taken over the auto shop for their final rehearsal. The shop teacher, Carl Dukowski, was a Vietnam vet who understood what it meant to need noise to drown out other noise. He'd cleared bay three, the one with the best acoustics.
The Lincoln boys had gathered their army: Stephen Murphy on bass, looking like a young John Entwistle if Entwistle had grown up poor in Massachusetts. David Cassel behind a drum kit they'd cobbled together from three different sets, none matching but all thunderous. Mark Pianka on keyboards, quiet kid who let his fingers do the screaming. And Nick, center stage, channeling every moment of working-class rage into six strings and a voice that could strip paint.
They ran through their set list one more time:
"Walk This Way" - their anthem, their declaration
"Dream On" - slowed down, turned into something that sounded like a prayer for kids who'd stopped believing
"Sweet Emotion" - sped up, aggressive, less seduction and more accusation
"Train Kept A-Rollin'" - their secret weapon, nine minutes of pure sonic assault
"The thing about Nick's version of Aerosmith," I tell my audience, ashing carefully into my WBCN mug, "was that he understood something most cover bands miss. He wasn't trying to be Steven Tyler. He was trying to tell Steven Tyler's story through his own scars."
While Nick's band turned the auto shop into a temple of noise, Jackie Marsh had transformed the school auditorium into something else entirely. His band—"Crown Jewels," because subtlety had died somewhere between the bulletin board and the bridge—had the advantage of proper acoustics and actual music stands.
His recruited ensemble read like a who's who of suburban achievement: Elizabeth Marsh on violin, Jackie's thirteen-year-old sister who played with the precision of someone who'd been taking lessons since she could hold a bow. Arthur "Ace" Callahan on drums, the freshman soccer star who approached rhythm like athletics—all discipline and explosive power. Max Wesslen on bass, gangly kid who'd gotten good because his parents believed in lessons even when it meant skipping vacations.
And Jackie himself, moving between piano and guitar and vocals like a maestro conducting his own fever dream, his voice trained to find notes that didn't exist in nature.
Their set list was ambitious to the point of arrogance:
"Bohemian Rhapsody" - all six minutes, every movement, arranged for their instruments
"Somebody to Love" - gospel influences intact but somehow made whiter
"We Will Rock You" - reimagined as symphony
"We Are the Champions" - their closer, their declaration of war
But here's what the gathering crowds didn't know, what even I didn't know until later: both bands were scared shitless.
Nick stood in bay three of the auto shop, surrounded by his brothers-in-arms, and felt the weight of representing everyone who'd ever been told they weren't good enough. Every kid who'd watched their parents struggle, every family that knew the exact price of milk because every penny mattered, every dream deferred because rent came first—they were all counting on him to prove that authentic meant more than educated.
Jackie sat at the Steinway they'd moved into the auditorium, surrounded by kids who'd never wondered if the lights would stay on, and felt the weight of representing everyone who'd been told they were only good enough because of their advantages. Every kid who'd been accused of buying their talent, every achievement dismissed as privilege, every genuine emotion reduced to performance—they were all counting on him to prove that excellence could exist alongside soul.
"Both of them called me that night," I tell my listeners, and this is something I've never admitted on air before. "Separately, of course. Both wanting the same thing—reassurance that what they were doing mattered."
Nick had called at eleven-thirty, just as I was finishing my show. His voice was rough, like he'd been screaming or trying not to cry.
"What if I'm wrong?" he'd asked. "What if Jackie's right and rock needs to evolve? What if I'm just holding onto something that's already dead?"
Jackie had called at one in the morning, catching me in the parking lot. His voice was controlled, but I could hear the cracks.
"What if Nick's right?" he'd asked. "What if I'm just playing dress-up with other people's authenticity? What if everything I do is just... academic?"
"I gave them both the same advice," I continue, lighting another cigarette because this is the hard part. "I told them to play their truth. To stop worrying about the other guy and just be honest about who they were and what they heard when they closed their eyes."
I pause, take a long drag, feel the smoke burn lessons into my lungs.
"It was terrible advice. Not because it was wrong, but because it assumed their truths could coexist. I forgot that in America, some truths are louder than others, and volume has nothing to do with validity."
Back in the booth, I watch my engineer stifle a yawn. He has no idea what it cost those boys to carry their communities' hopes. He grew up in a world where music is content, where authenticity is a marketing strategy, where battles of the bands are won by whoever has the most Instagram followers.
"But there was one more rehearsal happening that night," I continue, "and this one would change everything.”




















Chapter 8: The Wild Card from Concord
"Concord, Massachusetts," I say, stubbing out my cigarette with perhaps more force than necessary. "Nineteen miles from Boston, six miles from Sudbury, and approximately one universe away from the war between Nick and Jackie."
I spin up Fleetwood Mac's "Go Your Own Way" because sometimes the obvious choice is the right choice, and because Lindsay Buckingham understood something about harmony and discord that felt appropriate.
"While Lincoln-Sudbury was tearing itself apart, while Nick and Jackie were turning a music competition into Gettysburg with guitars, a girl named Patty Lally was sitting in a Concord church basement with her band, practicing a kind of magic that neither boy could have imagined."
St. Michael's Episcopal Church was one of those New England churches that looked like it had been painted by Norman Rockwell—white clapboard, modest steeple, graveyard full of Revolutionary War soldiers and their eternally patient wives. The basement smelled like decades of coffee hours and AA meetings, hope and desperation ground into the linoleum.
Patty Lally didn't look like a revolutionary. Seventeen years old, wheat-colored hair that fell straight to her waist, the kind of quiet that people mistook for shy until she opened her mouth to sing. Then things got complicated.
Her band—they called themselves "Rumours" after the Fleetwood Mac album, but also after what they knew people would say about them—consisted of musicians who didn't fit the usual categories. Robin Bosshard on guitar, a transfer student from Switzerland who played like she was homesick for mountains. Ben Chen on bass, math genius who approached rhythm like calculus and somehow made it groove. Amy Kowalski on drums, Polish kid from the poor part of Concord that tourists never saw, who hit the skins like she was trying to wake the dead.
"Here's what made Rumours different," I tell my audience, checking the clock—one hour and fifty-two minutes left of WBCN, left of my career, left of an era. "They weren't trying to be anyone else. While Nick was channeling Aerosmith's authenticity and Jackie was perfecting Queen's precision, Patty Lally was doing something radical: she was being herself."
I'd heard about them, of course. In the incestuous world of Boston-area music, rumors about Rumours had been circulating for months. But I hadn't paid attention because I was focused on the drama in Sudbury, the clash of the titans, the binary choice between raw and refined.
That Thursday night, while Nick and Jackie were making final preparations for their war, Rumours was in that church basement working on a different kind of arsenal.
They weren't doing covers. Let me repeat that for the kids in the back: a high school band entering a Battle of the Bands in 1979 wasn't doing covers. They'd written their own songs—not teenage diary entries set to three chords, but actual compositions that understood something about life that most adults spend decades avoiding.
"The Ballad of Sarah Hutchinson" - about a Concord girl who'd hanged herself in 1692 rather than confess to witchcraft, but really about the price of refusing to be what power demands.
"Minuteman's Lament" - ostensibly about Revolutionary War soldiers, actually about how every generation thinks it's fighting for freedom while building new prisons.
"Common Ground" - their secret weapon, a song that started soft and built to a crescendo that could make you believe in democracy again, at least for three minutes and forty-two seconds.
"But here's the kicker," I continue, fishing for my lighter because sometimes you need fire to tell the truth. "Patty Lally had history with both Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh. History that neither boy knew about, history that would make the Battle of the Bands about more than anyone had imagined."
In the summer of 1974, back when they were all twelve and thirteen, back before high school taught them who they were supposed to hate, there had been a regional summer music camp. Two weeks at a place called Camp Winnipesaukee, where kids from different towns learned that music was bigger than zip codes.
Nick had been there on scholarship—one of those programs designed to make rich donors feel good about themselves. Jackie had been there because his parents thought it would look good on future applications. Patty had been there because her grandmother had left her a small inheritance with the stipulation that it be used for "something beautiful."
"For two weeks," I tell the darkness beyond the booth, "those three kids made music together. Nick's raw talent, Jackie's technical skill, and Patty's ability to find the frequency where both could coexist. They wrote songs, performed together, made promises about staying in touch and forming a band that would change the world."
Then camp ended. They went back to their respective towns, their respective lives, their respective futures. The promises faded like summer tans. By the time high school started, they moved in different orbits, occasionally passing in hallways at regional events but never acknowledging what they'd shared.
"Patty remembered, though," I continue. "She remembered what they sounded like together, remembered the possibility that existed before the world taught them to choose sides. And when she saw that Battle of the Bands poster, she didn't see a competition. She saw a chance to remind two boys what they'd forgotten."
But—and this is crucial—she didn't tell them she was entering. Didn't reach out, didn't warn them, didn't try to broker peace. Patty Lally understood something at seventeen that most people never learn: sometimes the only way to stop a war is to show both sides what they're really fighting for.
"So while Nick perfected his working-class rage and Jackie polished his educated precision, Patty Lally and Rumours prepared something else entirely," I say, crushing my cigarette with finality. "They prepared to tell the truth."
Back in that church basement, as Thursday bled into Friday, Patty stood at the microphone and sang "Common Ground" one more time. Her voice—not trained like Jackie's, not raw like Nick's, but somewhere in between, somewhere human—filled the space with a question that neither boy was ready to hear:
"What if the divide isn't real? What if it's just something we've been taught to see?"
"Tomorrow," she told her band as they packed up their equipment. "Tomorrow we remind them what music is really for."
Robin Bosshard looked at her, this quiet girl with revolution in her throat, and asked, "Do you really think they'll remember? Nick and Jackie?"
Patty smiled, and it was the smile of someone who'd kept a secret for five years and was finally ready to tell it.
"They'll remember," she said. "They just don't know it yet."
In my booth, I check the clock one more time. One hour and thirty-seven minutes left. Time enough to tell the rest, time enough to get to the battle itself, to the moment when three kids' shared past collided with their divided present and created something nobody expected.
"When we come back from the commercial break that exists only in memory," I say, "I'll tell you about March fifteenth, 1979. The night of the Battle of the Bands. The night when Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School became a battlefield, when WBCN became kingmaker, and when a girl named Patty Lally reminded two boys that some frequencies are stronger than the static we create to drown them out."
I reach for the next cart, the one labeled "March 15, 1979 - Live Recording," my hands trembling slightly. Not from age, not from bourbon, but from the weight of what comes next. Because some stories don't have happy endings. Some stories just have endings that teach us what we should have known all along.
The ON AIR sign pulses. Boston sleeps. And somewhere out there, Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh are listening to their story being told, maybe remembering a girl with wheat-colored hair who tried to save them both, maybe wondering why we so often destroy what we should cherish, maybe hearing—finally, after all these years—the frequency they lost.


Chapter 9: The Geography of Division
"Before I tell you about the battle," I say, swirling the last inch of bourbon in my glass, "I need to tell you about the battlefield. Because Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School wasn't just divided by a brook. It was quartered like a medieval criminal."
I cue up Pink Floyd's "Welcome to the Machine" because Roger Waters understood how systems digest people and excrete compliance.
"Picture Sudbury from above in 1979," I continue, my voice getting that late-night raspiness that used to drive program directors crazy. "Hop Brook running north to south like God's property line. But that's only half the story. Running east to west was something even more American than class division—the town dump."
My engineer looks up from his phone, possibly interested for the first time tonight. Even young digital natives understand dumps. They're universal. Every town has one, had one, or is one.
"The Sudbury dump wasn't just where garbage went to die. It was where families went on Saturday mornings, station wagons loaded with a week's worth of American excess. Fathers and kids—mostly fathers and sons, let's be honest about 1979—would drive up to the landfill and participate in that most democratic of rituals: throwing your shit away and watching bulldozers bury it."
I light another cigarette, wondering if my lungs will last the next ninety minutes. Probably. They've lasted this long.
"East of the dump, right up against it like a joke God was telling, was Cavicchio's Pig Farm. Three hundred acres of Yankee pragmatism—pigs eating garbage, garbage feeding pigs, the circle of life with a Boston accent. The smell when the wind was wrong could peel paint off houses a mile away. Guess which side of town had to smell it most?"
The answer was obvious: Nick's side. The working-class quarters of Sudbury got the brook, the dump, and the pig farm. A trifecta of reminders about their place in the ecosystem.
"So you had four Sudburys," I explain, ashing into my mug. "Northeast: working-class families who dealt with both water and waste. Southeast: rural poor who worked the pig farm and smelled like it. Southwest: the professors and professionals protected by the forest. Northwest: the real money, buffered by distance from everything unpleasant."
But here's the thing about that Friday, March 15, 1979—all four quarters were heading to the same place. The Battle of the Bands had become the kind of event that transcends geography. Like a tornado or a hanging, it demanded witnesses.
"By six PM, the Lincoln-Sudbury parking lot looked like a sociological experiment," I continue. "Mercedes parked next to pickup trucks. Kids who'd never acknowledged each other's existence suddenly sizing up the competition. And in the middle of it all, three bands preparing to prove that music could either unite or divide—depending on who was playing and who was listening."
The high school cafeteria had been transformed into a venue. The lunch tables cleared out, a professional sound system trucked in—WBCN had sprung for quality equipment because we still believed in investing in local music. The stage was actually just risers from the choir room, but under the lights it looked like Madison Square Garden to kids who'd never been farther than Boston.
"I arrived early," I tell my dwindling audience. "Wanted to feel the room before it filled with bodies and dreams and the particular desperation of teenagers who think this moment will define their entire lives."
The banner hanging across the stage read "WBCN PRESENTS: BATTLE OF THE BANDS 1979" in letters three feet tall. Below it, smaller text: "May the Best Band Win." As if it were that simple. As if "best" wasn't a word that meant different things depending on which side of the dump you lived on.
Chuck Morrison, WBCN's morning drive DJ, was already there, sound-checking the PA system with his signature "Testing, one-two, one-two, is this thing on?" that had become a Boston catchphrase. He saw me and waved me over.
"Maxanne! Ready for some rock and roll?" He was cocaine-thin and enthusiasm-thick, the kind of DJ who believed every night could be legendary if you just wanted it hard enough.
"Ready for something," I said, watching the crews setting up equipment. "You hear about the tension between the kids?"
"Tension?" Chuck laughed. "I heard it's more like civil war. Rich kids versus townies, or something?"
"Something like that," I said, not bothering to correct him. Chuck was from California. He thought all of Massachusetts was quaint.
By six-thirty, the cafeteria was filling up. The geography of the town replicated itself in the seating. Northeast Sudbury claimed the bleachers stage left. Southwest Sudbury took stage right. The Lincoln kids—caught between loyalties—scattered throughout like reluctant ambassadors. And the kids from the other towns—Concord, Wayland, Framingham—took the back, smart enough to recognize a local conflict when they saw one.
"What struck me," I tell my listeners, checking the clock—one hour and twenty-eight minutes left—"was how young they all looked. How impossibly, heartbreakingly young. They thought they were so grown up, so ready to take on the world. They had no idea the world was about to take them on instead.”


Chapter 10: The Order of Battle
"Seven o'clock," I continue, stubbing out my cigarette with the finality of a judge's gavel. "Time to announce the order of performance. In any battle, position matters. First can set the tone. Last gets remembered. Middle risks getting lost in the noise."
Chuck Morrison took the stage with the boundless energy of someone who'd done a line in the bathroom—which, knowing Chuck, he probably had. The crowd noise dropped to a buzz, then to whispers, then to that electric silence that happens when something is about to begin.
"LINCOLN-SUDBURY!" Chuck shouted, and the crowd erupted. "ARE YOU READY TO ROCK?"
The roar that went up could have powered half of Boston. Kids who'd been too cool to care suddenly cared very much.
"Before we begin," Chuck continued, "let's thank our sponsors at WBCN, the Rock of Boston, for making this possible. And remember, our panel of judges includes yours truly, the legendary Maxanne Sartori..."
He gestured to where I sat at the judges' table, and I gave a little wave. The applause was polite but real—some of these kids had grown up listening to me break their favorite bands.
"...Carter Alan, afternoon drive's finest..."
Carter stood and bowed. He was the kid on our staff, only twenty-five, but he had ears like radar for what was coming next.
"...and from Strawberries Records and Tapes, Bob Walker, who's offered the winning band one hundred hours of free studio time!"
That got the biggest cheer. Studio time was gold, the bridge between bedroom dreams and radio reality.
"Now," Chuck said, producing an envelope with theatrical flair, "the moment you've been waiting for. The order of battle!"
He opened the envelope slowly, milking the suspense. The crowd leaned forward collectively.
"Opening our show tonight, from right here at Lincoln-Sudbury, give it up for... THE JESTERS!"
Polite applause. The Jesters were a freshman band doing Cars covers. Safe choice for an opener.
"Second, from Wayland High School... DETROIT ROCK CITY!"
Bigger applause. The KISS tribute band had a following, mostly among kids who thought makeup was rebellion.
"Third, from Framingham North... THE DEAD HEADS!"
Scattered whoops from the stoner contingent.
"Fourth, all the way from Boston Latin... TALKING HEADS TALKING!"
Confusion in the crowd. The Talking Heads were too new, too weird for suburban Massachusetts. David Byrne's jerky anxiety hadn't translated to the masses yet.
"Fifth, from Lincoln-Sudbury... CROWN JEWELS!"
The southwest Sudbury section exploded. Jackie's people were ready for their champion. I saw Jackie himself standing in the wings, his face carefully neutral, his hands steady. He'd drawn the middle spot—dangerous territory, but he had the talent to make it memorable.
"Sixth, from Concord-Carlisle... RUMOURS!"
Polite applause mixed with curiosity. Nobody knew what to expect from Concord.
"And closing our show, from Lincoln-Sudbury... WALKING THE LINE!"
The northeast section went berserk. Nick's people screaming like their lives depended on it, which in a way, they did. Nick had drawn the power position—closing the show, the spot where legends were made.
"But wait," Chuck said, and the crowd quieted. "That's only seven bands. Where's number eight?"
He paused for effect, the old DJ trick of making silence speak.
"Ladies and gentlemen, due to a family emergency, Sharp Dressed Men from Weston has had to withdraw. So we'll have seven bands competing for glory tonight. May the best band win!"
In the booth, I pause my narration to light another cigarette. I remember thinking at the time that seven was a mystical number. Seven deadly sins. Seven days of creation. Seven bands to tell the story of a divided town.
"What I didn't know," I continue, "was that the family emergency was bullshit. Sharp Dressed Men had withdrawn because the lead singer's father—a banker who lived in the northwest quarter, the one protected from both brook and dump—had heard about the 'element' that would be attending. He didn't want his son associated with, and I quote from the letter he sent to the school, 'an event that celebrates division rather than unity.'"
The irony was so thick you could spread it on toast. A rich man keeping his kid away from division while creating more of it.
"Seven-fifteen," I say, checking the clock in 2024, feeling the weight of time in my bones. "The Jesters took the stage. Four freshmen who looked like they'd raided their fathers' closets, trying to channel Ric Ocasek's cool but achieving something closer to math club chic. They opened with 'My Best Friend's Girl,' and for three minutes, the crowd forgot about civil war."
That's what music does when it's pure—it makes you forget the borders. Four kids who barely needed to shave were making eight hundred teenagers move as one. The magic of three chords and teenage hormones.
"But then they finished," I continue, "and the geography reasserted itself. Different sections applauded with different enthusiasm. The judges scribbled notes. Chuck Morrison prepared to introduce the next act. And in the wings, Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh stood on opposite sides of the same stage, both feeling the weight of what they were about to do."
I drain my bourbon and pour another—the emergency bottle I keep in my bottom drawer for emergencies and final broadcasts.
"What happened next," I say, "would determine more than just who won a battle of the bands. It would determine whose America we were living in. Whether music was about perfection or passion. Whether authenticity could be learned or only lived. Whether two boys who'd once harmonized over Hop Brook could find that frequency again, or whether the static between them had grown too loud to overcome."
The ON AIR sign pulses like a neon heartbeat. Boston sleeps, dreaming its gentrified dreams. And somewhere in the dark, the ghosts of 1979 are stirring, ready to take the stage one more time, ready to play the songs that divided a town and broke two hearts that should have been beating in rhythm.
"Stay tuned," I whisper, though there's nowhere else to tune to after tonight. "The real battle is about to begin.”





Chapter 11: The Opening Salvos
"Seven-thirty," I announce, watching the second hand on the booth clock like it might reveal the meaning of life. "The Jesters finish their set to genuine applause. Four freshmen who'd managed three Cars covers without embarrassing themselves—no small feat when you're fifteen and your voice is still deciding what octave it wants to live in."
I cue up The Velvet Underground's "Rock & Roll" because Lou Reed understood that music could save lives, even lives that hadn't figured out they needed saving yet.
The Jesters cleared their equipment with the efficiency of kids who'd practiced the breakdown as much as the performance. In the wings, I could see the other bands watching, calculating, adjusting their mental math of what it would take to win.
"But before I tell you about Detroit Rock City's assault on good taste," I continue, "let me paint you the full picture of that cafeteria. Because every corner told a story."
Near the emergency exit, the kids from the northeast quarter—Nick's people—had commandeered the bleachers like a fortress. They wore their uniform of defiance: denim jackets with band patches, concert tees from shows at the Worcester Centrum, jeans worn through at the knees from actual work, not fashion. Some of them clutched comic books—Marvel mostly, Spider-Man and the X-Men, heroes who were outsiders, freaks, mutants who found family in their difference.
The southwest quarter kids—Jackie's tribe—had claimed the risers near the judges' table. They dressed like they were already in college: corduroys and button-downs, sweaters draped over shoulders, the careful casualness that took money to achieve. Their reading material ran to science fiction paperbacks and the occasional DC comic—Superman and Batman, heroes with rules, codes, the luxury of choosing justice over survival.
"Comic books in 1979," I tell my dwindling audience, "were still disreputable. Kids hid them like contraband, traded them like currency, argued about them with the passion of theologians. Whether you were Marvel or DC said everything about how you saw the world. Marvel was working-class angst—heroes who were broke, bitter, brilliant but unrecognized. DC was establishment aspiration—heroes who owned corporations, had secret identities that mattered, saved cities that appreciated them."
Between the two camps, the Lincoln kids perched uneasily, some literally holding both Marvel and DC comics, trying to keep a foot in each world. One kid I noticed had a Fantastic Four issue (#14, I think—the one where they fight the Sub-Mariner) tucked next to a worn copy of The Great Gatsby. That's high school in a single image: trying to be everything to everyone while figuring out who you actually are.
"Seven thirty-five," I continue, lighting another cigarette. "Detroit Rock City takes the stage, and suddenly the cafeteria smells like greasepaint and adolescent ambition."
Four kids from Wayland done up in full KISS regalia—face paint, platform boots, the works. The lead singer had fashioned Gene Simmons' demon makeup with what looked like his mother's eyeliner and possibly some shoe polish. The guitarist wore Ace Frehley's spaceman silver, though it was already starting to run under the hot stage lights.
They opened with "Rock and Roll All Nite," and I'll give them this—they committed. Full theatrical nonsense, including an attempted fire-breathing bit that was actually just the lead singer spitting water into the lights. The crowd loved it for all the wrong reasons.
"Here's the thing about tribute bands," I tell my listeners, ashing into my increasingly full mug. "They can go one of two ways. Either they channel something essential about the original—the spirit, the energy, the reason those songs mattered—or they become karaoke with better costumes. Detroit Rock City was firmly in category two, but they were having so much fun that nobody cared."
During their version of "Detroit Rock City"—obligatory, given their name—I watched Nick and Jackie from my judge's perch. Nick stood stage left, arms crossed, his expression suggesting he was personally offended by the theatrical excess. Jackie stood stage right, studying the performance like an anthropologist, probably taking mental notes on what not to do.
Neither of them saw Patty Lally standing at the back of the cafeteria, still in her street clothes, watching them both with an expression I couldn't quite read from that distance. Later, she'd tell me she was praying—not to God, exactly, but to the spirit of music itself, asking it to remember what it was for.
"Seven forty-five," I say. "Detroit Rock City finished with 'Beth,' their drummer handling the lead vocals while sitting behind his kit. It was supposed to be the emotional moment, the proof they could do more than theatrics. Instead, it sounded like a sixteen-year-old boy trying to convince his girlfriend that joining a KISS tribute band was a career move."
The applause was generous—suburban kids rewarding effort over achievement, the participation trophy of rock and roll.
"But then," I continue, "things got interesting. Because the third band wasn't what anyone expected from a Grateful Dead tribute.”





Chapter 12: When the Dead Came Alive
"Seven fifty-two," I announce, stubbing out one cigarette and immediately lighting another because some stories require chain-smoking. "The Dead Heads from Framingham North took the stage, and the first thing you noticed was that they looked like they'd raided different decades' closets."
The lead guitarist wore a Duck Soup vintage shirt—and here I should mention Duck Soup, that eccentric shop on Route 20 that sold everything from Victorian medical equipment to Jefferson Airplane albums, run by folks who understood that weird was a compliment. The bassist sported bell-bottoms that might have actually seen the real Grateful Dead in concert. The drummer wore what looked like his father's business suit, complete with tie, which on a seventeen-year-old kid playing Dead covers was its own form of rebellion.
"What people didn't understand about the Dead in 1979," I tell my audience, "was that they weren't just a stoner band. They were America's greatest bar band that happened to play arenas. They understood something about collective improvisation that jazz musicians spent lifetimes chasing."
The Dead Heads opened not with "Truckin'" or "Casey Jones"—the obvious crowd-pleasers—but with "Fire on the Mountain," the slow burn from Barlow and Mickey Hart that builds like a conversation getting progressively more urgent.
And something happened in that cafeteria. The kids who'd been segregated by geography, by class, by comic book preference, started to sway in unison. Not dancing exactly—this was Massachusetts, after all—but moving like kelp in a current, all responding to the same tide.
"Here's what killed me," I continue, pouring another finger of bourbon into my coffee mug. "The kid on lead guitar—skinny boy with hair down to his shoulders and fingers like spider legs—was from the southeast quarter. The part of Framingham that backed up to industrial parks and auto body shops. He played like Jerry Garcia had personally whispered secrets in his ear, finding that space between country and cosmic that made the Dead more than the sum of their parts."
During the jam section—and yes, these high school kids had the audacity to jam, to actually improvise instead of just recreating—I saw something extraordinary. Nick Johnson, Mr. Working-Class Authenticity himself, was nodding along. Not quite smiling, but acknowledging something real in the music. And Jackie Marsh had uncrossed his arms, was actually leaning forward, listening to how the pianist was finding jazz progressions in a rock structure.
"For six minutes," I say, "the Dead Heads made that cafeteria forget about quarters and comics and who belonged where. They were preaching a gospel that Bob Weir had learned from Pigpen who'd learned it from Lightnin' Hopkins: music is a river, and rivers don't care about property lines."
But then they made a fatal error. Success made them confident, and confidence made them ambitious. They decided to close with "Dark Star," the Dead's legendary space exploration that could run anywhere from twenty minutes to the heat death of the universe.
"Eight-oh-three," I note. "Three minutes into 'Dark Star,' and you could feel the room's patience evaporating like morning dew. The magic they'd captured was leaking out through the emergency exits. Kids started talking, checking the comic books they'd brought, arguing about whether the X-Men could beat the Justice League."
By minute seven, even the judges were getting restless. Carter Alan was doodling on his score sheet. Bob Walker from Strawberries was checking his watch like he had somewhere better to be—which, being a record store owner in 1979, he probably did.
"Eight-oh-nine," I continue. "Chuck Morrison made the executive decision to fade the stage lights—the universal signal for 'wrap it up.' The Dead Heads, lost in their own cosmic journey, didn't notice for another minute. When they finally stopped, the applause was relieved rather than enthusiastic."
They'd learned what the real Grateful Dead knew but rarely admitted: the line between transcendence and tedium is thinner than a guitar string and twice as likely to snap.
"Eight-fifteen," I say, checking the clock in 2024. "Time for a brief intermission before the out-of-town bands took their shots. Kids streamed out to smoke in the parking lot—cigarettes mostly, though the sweet smell of pot drifted from behind the dumpsters where it always did during school events."
I watched Nick's crew head out one exit, Jackie's out another, maintaining their careful geography even during a bathroom break. But I also noticed something else: kids were starting to mix at the edges. A northeast kid borrowing a lighter from a southwest kid. A Lincoln boy sharing his Fantastic Four with a Sudbury girl who admitted she'd never read Marvel because her dad said it was trash.
"Small cracks in the wall," I tell my invisible audience. "But cracks have a way of spreading if you hit them with the right frequency."
In the parking lot, I bumped into Grace Johnson—Nick's mother, still in her Friendly's uniform because she'd come straight from work.
"Ms. Sartori," she said, and her voice carried that particular exhaustion of single mothers who've been tired since their children were born. "How's he doing?"
"Haven't heard him yet," I said. "But Nick's got something. You know that."
She looked toward the school, where her son was probably running through his set list one more time. "Sometimes I wonder if I should have pushed him toward something more... practical."
"Like what?" I asked.
"Like anything that doesn't require him to pour his heart out in front of people who might not care." She laughed, but it was the laugh of someone who'd learned that caring too much was a luxury she couldn't afford. "His father had dreams too. Look how that turned out."
Before I could respond, the lights flashed—intermission over, time for the out-of-town bands to show the locals how it was done.
"Eight twenty-five," I say, stubbing out my cigarette with finality. "Talking Heads Talking from Boston Latin took the stage, and nobody knew what to make of four kids in matching gray suits playing music that sounded like anxiety having a panic attack."
They opened with "Psycho Killer," David Byrne's meditation on alienation that wouldn't become iconic for another few years. The Latin kids—the first Black faces we'd seen on stage all night—played it with mathematical precision and zero soul, which was probably the point but missed the mark in a cafeteria full of kids who thought soul was the point.
"Eight thirty-five. They finished to confused applause, the kind that says 'we appreciate your effort but have no idea what you were trying to do.' Which brings us to eight forty," I say, lighting what I promise myself is the last cigarette of this segment. "Time for Crown Jewels to show us what all those music lessons had been for."
I pause, looking out at Boston's skyline, all those lights representing lives I'll never know, stories I'll never tell.
"Jackie Marsh was about to take the stage. And in the wings, Nick Johnson was about to learn what jealousy really meant. Because sometimes the enemy isn't the other army—it's your own memory of when you fought on the same side."
The stage lights dimmed. The crowd hushed. And somewhere in that cafeteria, the ghost of two thirteen-year-old boys who'd once made magic together was about to haunt everyone in the room.








Chapter 13: Crown Jewels and Class Warfare
"Eight forty-one," I say into the microphone, watching my hand shake slightly as I light another cigarette. Not from age or bourbon, but from remembering what came next. "Jackie Marsh walked onto that stage like he owned it. Which, in a way, he did. Ownership was in his DNA."
Crown Jewels took their positions with the precision of a military operation. Elizabeth Marsh, Jackie's thirteen-year-old sister, cradled her violin like a weapon of mass destruction. Arthur "Ace" Callahan sat behind a drum kit that cost more than most kids' cars, his soccer-player reflexes ready to deploy. Max Wesslen stood with his bass, gangly frame trying to project confidence through sheer will.
And Jackie. Jackie positioned himself at the Yamaha grand piano they'd somehow convinced the school to let them use—the one usually locked in the music room, reserved for recitals and special occasions. His tuxedo wasn't rented. That was the first thing you noticed. It fit like architecture, like his body had been designed to wear formal wear while the rest of us stumbled through life in denim and hope.
"Ladies and gentlemen," Jackie said into the mic, his voice carrying that prep school pronunciation that turned consonants into statements of purpose, "we are Crown Jewels. And we'd like to take you on a journey."
In the audience, I saw Nick Johnson physically flinch at the word 'journey.' It was such a Jackie word—implying that music was tourism, that you could visit authenticity like a vacation home.
"They opened with 'Killer Queen,'" I tell my late-night audience, "and within thirty seconds, you understood why Jackie Marsh was dangerous."
The arrangement was audacious. Elizabeth's violin took the lead line, transforming Mercury's camp masterpiece into something that sounded like it had been written by Mozart on cocaine. Jackie's piano filled every possible space with runs and flourishes that shouldn't have been possible on ten fingers. The rhythm section played with metronomic precision, turning rock and roll into mathematics.
"She's a Killer Queen," Jackie sang, his classically trained voice finding notes that Freddie Mercury had only implied, "Gunpowder, gelatine, dynamite with a laser beam..."
And here's what killed me, what I noticed from my judge's seat: Jackie wasn't just singing the song. He was deconstructing it, showing us all the architecture Mercury had hidden under the glam. Every note was a thesis statement about how rock and roll was just classical music that had learned to wear leather.
"The southwest quarter kids went insane," I continue, ashing into my perpetually full mug. "They'd come to see their prince claim his throne, and he was delivering. But watch the northeast kids—Nick's people. They sat frozen, torn between admitting it was brilliant and feeling betrayed by that brilliance."
Because that was the thing about Jackie's version of Queen: it was technically perfect and emotionally vacant. Like a museum piece that you admired but didn't want to touch. Like a girl you dated because she looked good on your application to life.
"Eight forty-seven," I note. "They transitioned into 'Somebody to Love,' and Jackie made his first mistake."
See, "Somebody to Love" is a gospel song dressed up like rock. It's about desperation, about being so lonely that you'd pray to any god who'd listen. Freddie Mercury wrote it from a place of genuine pain—a queer man in the '70s, looking for connection in a world that wanted him to hide.
Jackie sang it like a mathematical proof. Every note hit, every harmony precisely layered, every dynamic exactly as written on the sheet music he'd probably memorized. But there was no pain. No desperation. No sense that he'd ever had to beg anyone for anything.
"I watched Nick during this performance," I tell my audience, "and saw something break in his face. Not anger anymore. Something worse. Recognition."
Because Nick suddenly understood what I'd figured out that night in Jackie's living room: the boy was technically gifted but emotionally imprisoned. All those lessons, all that training, all that privilege—it had built him a beautiful cage, and he was singing from inside it without realizing the bars were there.
"Eight fifty-three. They moved into their third song, and this is where things got interesting. Because Jackie decided to take a risk."
Instead of another Queen anthem, he sat alone at the piano. The rest of Crown Jewels stepped back, giving him the stage.
"This is a song," Jackie said into the sudden quiet, "that Nick Johnson and I used to play. Back when we were thirteen. Back when we thought music was bigger than..." He paused, looking directly at the northeast bleachers. "Bigger than where we came from."
The opening notes of "Your Song" filled the cafeteria. Elton John's masterpiece, but slowed down, stripped bare, turned into something that sounded like an apology.









Chapter 14: The Song That Changed Everything
"I need you to understand," I say, fumbling for my lighter because my hands won't stop shaking now, "what that song meant. Because 'Your Song' wasn't just another cover. It was a loaded gun pointed at the past."
Summer of 1974. Camp Winnipesaukee. Two thirteen-year-old boys in a practice room that smelled like mildew and possibility. Nick had been trying to learn "Walk This Way" on a guitar held together with duct tape and prayer. Jackie had been practicing Chopin etudes because that's what you did when your parents paid for lessons.
"Play something together," the counselor had suggested, probably just trying to keep them busy while he snuck a cigarette. "Something you both know."
They'd landed on "Your Song" because it was on the radio constantly that summer. Elton's voice coming through tinny speakers, teaching kids that love could be simple and complicated at the same time.
"It's a little bit funny, this feeling inside," Jackie sang now in the cafeteria, his voice carrying something I'd never heard from him before. "I'm not one of those who can easily hide..."
In the northeast bleachers, Nick Johnson stood up. Not to leave, but like he'd been physically pulled by invisible strings. His crew tried to get him to sit, but he shook them off, standing alone while everyone else remained seated.
"I don't have much money," Jackie continued, and the irony of those words coming from him should have been laughable. But something in his voice made it true. "But boy, if I did..."
Here's what made it devastating: Jackie was singing it exactly the way he and Nick had arranged it that summer. Not the polished version he'd probably learned in lessons, but the raw, imperfect version two thirteen-year-olds had cobbled together. He even included the mistake they'd made in the bridge—a wrong chord that they'd decided sounded better than the right one.
"So excuse me forgetting, but these things I do," Jackie's voice cracked—actually cracked, the first imperfect note he'd hit all night. "You see I've forgotten if they're green or they're blue..."
I watched the cafeteria transform. The careful geography that had defined the night—northeast versus southwest, Marvel versus DC, dump versus forest—started to blur. Kids were looking at each other like they'd just realized they were all in the same story.
"Anyway, the thing is, what I really mean," Jackie was openly crying now, tears rolling down his perfect face, ruining his perfect makeup, making him perfectly human, "Yours are the sweetest eyes I've ever seen."
When he finished, the silence was different from all the other silences that night. This wasn't the quiet of judgment or confusion. This was the silence of recognition—everyone in that room recognizing something they'd lost or never had or didn't know they were looking for.
Nick Johnson was the first to clap. Alone in the bleachers, tears streaming down his own face, he brought his hands together in slow, deliberate applause. The sound echoed through the cafeteria like gunshots, like prayers, like the sound of walls coming down.
"Nine-oh-two," I whisper into the microphone, my voice hoarse with memory and nicotine. "Crown Jewels finished their set with 'We Are the Champions,' but it was anticlimactic. How do you follow the collapse of everything that divided you?"
They played it straight, no flourishes, no attempts to improve on Mercury's original. Just four kids playing a song about winning while their leader had just admitted that victory was the loneliest place in the world.
When they finished, the applause was strange—enthusiastic from some corners, confused from others, but threaded through with something new. Conversation. Kids turning to each other, asking "Did you know they used to be friends?" and "What happened?" and "Why did that song sound like goodbye?"
"Nine-ten," I continue. "Time for Rumours from Concord. Time for Patty Lally to remind everyone what they'd all forgotten."
But Jackie wasn't done surprising people. As Crown Jewels cleared their equipment, he walked directly across the stage—crossing the invisible line that had divided it all night—and stood in front of Nick.
"I'm sorry," he said, loud enough for the front rows to hear. "I'm sorry for the bridge. For stepping aside. For choosing safe over..."
"Don't," Nick said, but his voice was rough with emotion. "Not here. Not now."
"Then when?" Jackie asked.
Before Nick could answer, Chuck Morrison's voice boomed over the PA. "Ladies and gentlemen, all the way from Concord-Carlisle, please welcome... RUMOURS!”











Chapter 15: The Wild Card Plays Her Hand
"Nine-fourteen," I announce, crushing my cigarette with unnecessary force. "Four kids from Concord took the stage, and nobody knew what to expect. Which, as any good DJ will tell you, is exactly where you want your audience."
Patty Lally walked to the center microphone with the quiet confidence of someone who had nothing to prove. Her wheat-colored hair caught the stage lights like a midwest sunset, and her dress—simple, handmade, probably by her grandmother—made every other outfit that night look like trying too hard.
Robin Bosshard, the Swiss transfer student, carried a guitar that had seen better decades. Ben Chen took his position at bass with the focus of someone solving equations. Amy Kowalski settled behind her kit, cracking her knuckles like a prizefighter.
"Good evening," Patty said, her voice carrying that strange quality that made eight hundred teenagers suddenly stop texting—wait, wrong era. Stop passing notes. Stop existing anywhere but that moment. "We're Rumours. And we're not here to play covers."
A ripple went through the crowd. Not covers? At a Battle of the Bands built entirely on whose version of which famous song was better?
"We're going to play three original songs," Patty continued, seemingly unaware she'd just committed social suicide. "Songs about Concord. About Massachusetts. About what happens when we forget we're all in the same story."
In the judges' section, Chuck Morrison looked at me with panic. Original songs weren't explicitly forbidden, but they weren't expected. How do you judge something that has no reference point?
"The first song," Patty said, adjusting her microphone with steady hands, "is called 'Common Ground.' It's about a place where all rivers meet. Some of you might recognize it."
She looked directly at Nick, then at Jackie, holding each gaze for a moment that stretched like taffy. Then she nodded to her band, and they began.
The opening was simple—Robin's guitar finding a melody that sounded familiar even though you'd never heard it before. Ben's bass creating a foundation that felt like earth under your feet. Amy's drums measuring time like heartbeats, like breathing, like the rhythm of walking through woods.
And then Patty sang:
"There's a place where the brook runs shallow,
Where the kids from both sides come to play,
Where the water doesn't know from ZIP codes,
And the fish don't care about your name..."
"I need to stop here," I tell my 2024 audience, "and explain something about Patty Lally's voice. It wasn't trained like Jackie's. It wasn't raw like Nick's. It was something else entirely. It was true. Not true like authentic, but true like gravity. True like mathematics. True like the way water always finds its level."
"Where the woods hold our secrets in silence,
Where the trails remember every bike,
Where the dump was our treasure island,
And Saturday mornings taught us life..."
In the audience, I saw kids from all four quarters of Sudbury starting to recognition-smile. That universal expression when someone names something you thought only you knew. The Brook. The trails. The dump as democracy. Someone was singing their geography.
But then the chorus hit, and everything changed:
"Common ground, common ground,
We're all standing on common ground,
The earth don't care about your side of town,
We're all dancing on common ground..."
"Nine-eighteen," I note. "Two minutes into their first song, and Rumours had done something none of the other bands had managed. They'd made everyone in that cafeteria realize they were from the same place."
I watched Nick and Jackie during this performance, standing on opposite sides of the stage but both transfixed. Because here's what Patty was doing—she was singing the song they should have written together. The song that used Nick's understanding of place and Jackie's understanding of structure. The song that proved what they could have been if they'd stayed friends.
"Nine-twenty-one. They moved into their second song, 'Minuteman's Lament.' And this is where Patty showed she wasn't just some folk singer with delusions of depth."
The song started with Amy's military snare, crisp and urgent. Then Ben's bass came in playing a line that sounded like Paul Revere's horse if Paul Revere had understood funk. Robin's guitar was pure revolution—not the historical kind, but the kind that happens in your chest when you realize you've been lied to.
"They tell us that the fight is over,
They tell us that the war's been won,
But I see battles in the playground,
I see children choosing guns..."
"Remember," I tell my audience, lighting what I swear is my last cigarette, "this is 1979. Vietnam was still a raw wound. Kids in that cafeteria had older brothers who'd come back different, or not come back at all. Patty was singing about war to children of war, and they heard her."
"The British left but the lines remained,
The revolution's still not done,
We're still fighting over table scraps,
While the owners eat the whole damn bun..."
Chuck Morrison looked like he wanted to pull the plug. A seventeen-year-old girl singing about class warfare at a school event? In another year, at another time, she might have been shut down. But something about that night, about what had already happened between Jackie and Nick, made even the adults understand we were past the point of pretending.
"Nine-twenty-six," I continue. "Time for their final song. Time for Patty Lally to play her ace."
She stepped back from the mic, looked at her band, then looked at Nick and Jackie again.
"This last song," she said, "is about what we lose when we stop listening. It's about three kids who met at summer camp. Who made music together for two weeks. Who promised to stay friends forever." She paused. "Who forgot that promise the second the world told them who they were supposed to be."
The opening notes were devastating in their simplicity. Just Robin's guitar, finding a melody that sounded like summer, like possibility, like thirteen years old.
"We were younger then, we were braver then,
We were stupid enough to think the music mattered more,
Than the side of the tracks, than the size of the house,
Than the names on our clothes or the schools on our doors..."
In my booth in 2024, I have to stop. Have to breathe. Because even forty-five years later, that song breaks me. Patty Lally, seventeen years old, writing about loss with the wisdom of someone who'd lived three lifetimes.
"But here's what destroyed everyone in that cafeteria," I continue when I can speak again. "The second verse. The second verse that named names."
"Jackie had the fingers, Nicky had the soul,
I had the voice that could make them whole,
But August came calling with its separate ways,
And we buried our promise in the last summer days..."
She'd used their names. Their childhood names. Jackie and Nicky, not the armored versions they'd become. And in doing so, she'd told eight hundred people that these two warriors had once been children. Had once been friends. Had once made music for the pure joy of making music.
The cafeteria was dead silent except for Patty's voice, Robin's guitar, and the sound of seventeen years' worth of tears being held back by teenagers who'd forgotten they were allowed to cry.
"Nine-thirty-one," I whisper. "Rumours finished to applause that sounded like rain. That started soft and built to thunder. That came from every corner of the cafeteria because Patty Lally had done something none of us expected. She'd told the truth."
As they cleared their equipment, I saw Nick and Jackie moving toward each other through the crowd. Drawn like magnets, like gravity, like kids who'd just remembered they were kids.
They met in the middle of the cafeteria floor. Said nothing. Just stood there, looking at each other while eight hundred teenagers held their breath.
Then Chuck Morrison's voice shattered the moment: "Our final act of the evening—Walking the Line!"
Nick looked at Jackie. Jackie looked at Nick. And in that look was everything—apology, regret, possibility, impossibility.
"I have to go," Nick said.
"I know," Jackie replied.
Nick turned toward the stage. Turned toward his destiny as the working-class hero. Turned toward what everyone expected him to be.
But before he took the stage, he looked back one more time.
"After," he said. Just that. After.
Jackie nodded. After the battle. After the votes. After the world stopped watching. After they could stop being symbols and start being seventeen again.
"Nine-thirty-five," I say, crushing my last cigarette like I'm putting something out of its misery. "Time for the hometown hero to show us what authenticity looked like when it had nothing left to lose."
The stage lights dimmed. Four boys from the wrong side of everything took their positions. And Nick Johnson prepared to sing like his life depended on it.
Which, in a way, it did.















Chapter 16: The Sound of Authenticity
"Nine-thirty-six," I announce, watching the clock like it might reveal secrets. "Walking the Line took the stage, and you could feel the energy shift in that cafeteria. This wasn't performance anymore. This was testimony."
Nick Johnson stood center stage in jeans that had been washed too many times and a black t-shirt that said nothing because it didn't need to. No costume. No pretense. Just a kid with a guitar that had more miles on it than most people's cars.
Behind him, the Lincoln boys took their positions with the quiet confidence of underdogs who'd stopped apologizing for it. Stephen Murphy on bass, instrument slung low like he was heading into battle. David Cassel behind drums that didn't match but thundered in unison. Mark Pianka at keyboards that they'd borrowed from three different people to make a full rig.
"Before we start," Nick said into the microphone, his voice carrying that sandpaper quality that comes from too much shouting or too much silence, "I want to say something."
The crowd quieted. Even the kids who'd been sneaking sips from flasks in the back rows paid attention.
"Music isn't about being better than someone else," Nick continued, looking directly at Jackie. "It's about being better than yourself. It's about taking whatever life throws at you—" his voice caught for just a moment, "—and turning it into something that matters."
He nodded to his band, and they launched into "Walk This Way" with the kind of ferocity that made the original sound like a rehearsal.
"Here's what you need to understand about Nick's version," I tell my 2024 audience, lighting a cigarette with hands that remember that night too well. "He wasn't trying to be Steven Tyler. He was trying to explain Steven Tyler. To translate him for kids who knew what it meant to walk the wrong way home because the right way went through neighborhoods where you weren't welcome."
The drums hit like hammerfalls. The bass line didn't just walk—it strutted, it demanded space, it claimed territory. And Nick's guitar work was pure controlled chaos, finding notes in the margins that Aerosmith had only hinted at.
But it was his voice that transformed the song. When he sang about backstroke lovers and schoolgirl sweeties, he wasn't singing about sex. He was singing about every experience that working-class kids were told they couldn't have, every door that was closed before they even knocked.
"Nine-forty," I note. "The northeast section of the cafeteria had become a single organism, moving in rhythm, shouting the lyrics back at the stage like prayers at a revival meeting."
And here's what killed me—the southwest section, Jackie's people, they were moving too. Not with the same abandon, but with recognition. Because Nick's authenticity was undeniable. You couldn't fake that kind of hunger. You couldn't learn it in lessons.
"They transitioned into 'Dream On,'" I continue, "and the cafeteria transformed into something else entirely."
Nick moved to the piano—yes, the same piano Jackie had played. But where Jackie had made it sound like Juilliard, Nick made it sound like a barroom confession at three AM.
"Every time when I look in the mirror," he sang, and his voice carried all the weight of a seventeen-year-old who'd been the man of the house since fifteen. "All these lines on my face getting clearer..."
I watched Grace Johnson in the audience, still in her Friendly's uniform, tears streaming down her face. Because her son was singing her story too—every extra shift, every deferred dream, every time she looked in the mirror and saw her youth sacrificing itself for survival.
"Dream until your dreams come true," Nick sang, and it wasn't a promise. It was a question. It was a challenge. It was an admission that dreams were something you had to fight for, not something you inherited.
"Nine-forty-five," I say. "Time for their third song. Time for Nick to show us what he'd learned from Patty's example.”

Chapter 17: The Song No One Expected
"We're going to do something different," Nick announced, wiping sweat from his forehead with the kind of unconscious gesture that rock stars practice but real people just do. "This is a song I wrote. Well, we wrote. Last night."
A murmur through the crowd. Original songs were Patty's thing. Working-class heroes didn't write their own material—they channeled other people's authenticity, right?
"It's called 'Hop Brook Blues,'" Nick said, and even from the judges' table I could see him trembling. "It's about growing up in a town that's divided by water but united by..." he paused, searching for words, "by disappointment."
Stephen Murphy played a bass line that sounded like the brook itself—fluid, constant, dividing everything it touched. David Cassel found a rhythm that was part train, part heartbeat, part the sound of skateboards on pavement.
Oh yeah, skateboarding. By 1979, it had crept into Massachusetts consciousness like a California fever dream. Kids who couldn't afford ski trips were finding freedom on four wheels and a piece of wood. The old Raytheon warehouse in Watertown had been converted into an indoor skate park, and kids from both sides of Hop Brook would hitchhike there on weekends, united in their desire to defy gravity if not geography.
Nick started to sing, and his voice found that space between speaking and crying:
"There's a brook that runs through my hometown,
Dividing poor from rich, dividing lost from found,
My daddy crossed it every morning till the morning that he died,
Now I stand on the east bank wondering why we picked this side..."
"I need to stop here," I tell my listeners, ashing into my eternally full mug, "because this wasn't just a song. It was sociology with a backbeat. It was every conversation that happened in hushed tones at kitchen tables. It was the question every working-class kid eventually asks—why us?"
"The west side kids got skateboards, man, they're California dreaming,
While east side kids got bicycles and minimum wage scheming,
But we all meet at the package store when the Red Sox break our hearts,
'Cause losing is the one thing in this town that don't care about your part..."
That line got a laugh—rueful, knowing, the laugh of people who'd lived through 1975's Game Six and 1978's Bucky Dent homer. The Red Sox were democracy in reverse—they disappointed everyone equally.
"But here's what made the song brilliant," I continue. "The bridge. The goddamn bridge that Nick Johnson, seventeen years old, working-class poet who didn't know he was a poet, delivered like truth serum."
"But I met a boy from the other side, when we were just thirteen,
He played piano like an angel, had the finest voice I'd seen,
We made music in my garage until the world explained,
That brooks don't just divide the land, they divide the dreams from pain..."
Jackie. He was singing about Jackie. In front of eight hundred people, in front of judges and parents and everyone who'd picked a side, Nick Johnson was admitting that he'd lost more than a competition. He'd lost a friend.
"Nine-fifty," I whisper. "The song ended, and for the second time that night, silence fell like a hammer."
Then—and I swear this is true, you can listen to the bootleg recording that circulates if you don't believe me—Jackie started clapping. Standing in the wings where he'd been waiting, still in his performance tuxedo, bringing his hands together in slow, deliberate rhythm.
The sound carried across the cafeteria like a signal. Grace Johnson joined in. Then Patty Lally. Then kids from every quarter, every division, every carefully maintained border.
"Nick's band had one more song," I say, "but everybody knew it was already over. Not the competition—that still had to be decided. But the war. The war was over."
They closed with "Train Kept A-Rollin'," nine minutes of pure sonic assault that was less performance than exorcism. Nick played guitar like he was trying to break through to another dimension, where brooks didn't divide and dreams didn't depend on ZIP codes.
When it ended, when the last feedback faded and the stage lights dimmed, Nick stood there looking lost. Like a soldier who'd won a battle but couldn't remember what the war was about.
"Ten-oh-two," I announce. "Time for the judges to pretend we were judging music instead of witnessing transformation."
Chuck Morrison took the stage to announce that we'd need fifteen minutes to deliberate. The crowd dispersed into chaos—kids arguing about who was better, who was realer, who deserved to win. But the arguments lacked conviction. Something had shifted. The neat divisions that had defined the night were blurring.
I found myself in the hallway with Carter Alan and Bob Walker, supposedly discussing scoring. But we all knew the score was beside the point.
"So," Carter said, running his hands through his hair. "We really going to pretend that was about music?"
"What else would it be about?" Bob asked, but his smile said he knew exactly what else.
"Three kids," I said, lighting another cigarette because judges got to smoke indoors in 1979. "Three kids who used to be friends. Who just reminded a whole town what they'd forgotten."
"So who wins?" Carter asked.
I looked through the door glass at the cafeteria, where kids from different quarters were actually talking to each other. Where Nick and Jackie were standing near the stage, not speaking but not fleeing either. Where Patty Lally was surrounded by kids who wanted to know how to write their own songs.
"Maybe," I said, "that's the wrong question."
But we had to ask it anyway. That's what competitions do—they force decisions that shouldn't need forcing. They create winners and losers when sometimes everybody's just trying to survive.
"Ten-fifteen," I say to my 2024 audience, checking the clock one more time. "Time to announce a winner. Time to divide what had just been united. Time to prove that America always finds a way to make us choose sides, even when we're trying to choose each other."
The stage lights came back up. Chuck Morrison approached the microphone with an envelope that might as well have contained a bomb. And somewhere in that cafeteria, three teenagers who'd briefly remembered how to harmonize were about to learn whether harmony was enough.
Spoiler alert: it never is. Not in America. Not in 1979. Not when there's always another brook to cross, another division to maintain, another reason why we can't have what we all claim to want.
But for just a moment—for the length of a song, for the space between heartbeats, for the time it takes to remember you're seventeen and everything is still possible—we'd forgotten that.
And forgetting, sometimes, is the most revolutionary act of all.













Chapter 18: The Weight of Winning
"Ten-seventeen PM, March 15th, 1979," I announce to my dwindling audience, the booth clock ticking toward WBCN's final hour. "Chuck Morrison stood at that microphone holding an envelope that contained more than just a winner's name. It contained proof of which America we were choosing to live in."
The cafeteria had reorganized itself, but differently than before. The rigid geography of the evening had softened—kids from different quarters standing closer, boundaries blurred but not erased. Like magnets that had been shaken but would eventually find their poles again.
Chuck milked the moment because that's what DJs do—we understand that anticipation is half the drug.
"Before I announce our winner," he said, "I want to say something. In twenty years of radio, I've seen a lot of battles of the bands. I've watched kids imitate their heroes, chase their dreams, compete like their lives depended on it. But tonight..."
He paused, and I could see him struggling with whether to say what he was thinking or what was expected.
"Tonight, I watched something else. I watched a conversation. I watched kids remember they're from the same town, drinking the same water, breaking their hearts over the same baseball team."
A ripple of nervous laughter at the Red Sox reference. Some truths were safer than others.
"But," Chuck continued, because there's always a but in America, "this is a competition. And competitions have winners."
I watched Nick and Jackie during this speech. They stood on opposite sides of the stage still, but their bodies had turned slightly toward each other, like plants bending toward light they didn't fully trust.
"In third place," Chuck announced, "with a performance that reminded us all why original music matters... RUMOURS!"
The applause was thunderous, genuine, the kind that said 'you should have won but we're not ready for what you're selling.' Patty Lally smiled like she'd expected exactly this result. You don't change the world by winning competitions. You change it by telling truths that make competitions irrelevant.
"In second place," Chuck continued, and now the tension was electric, "with technical excellence that redefined what teenage musicians are capable of... CROWN JEWELS!"
The southwest quarter erupted in protest. Second place? For that level of perfection? I saw Dr. Marsh's face in the crowd, the kind of controlled fury that writes letters to school boards and gets music programs defunded.
But Jackie—Jackie was smiling. Not the practiced smile of recitals, but something real. Something that said he'd already won what mattered.
"Which means," Chuck said, unnecessary but obligated, "your 1979 Battle of the Bands winner is... WALKING THE LINE!"
The northeast section exploded like their team had just won the Series—which, given it was Boston, meant they were celebrating something they'd never actually experienced. Nick's crew rushed the stage, lifting him and the Lincoln boys like conquering heroes.
But here's what I saw from my judge's seat: Nick wasn't celebrating. He was looking at Jackie with an expression I'd seen before—on kids who'd gotten what they wanted only to realize they'd wanted the wrong thing.
"Ten-twenty-three," I note. "Time for the part nobody talks about—what happens after winning.”









Chapter 19: The Frequency Between Stations
"Winners are supposed to be happy," I say into the microphone, pouring the last of my bourbon and wondering if I should open another bottle. Probably not. Some stories are better told with diminishing resources. "But watch what happens when victory divides what almost united."
Nick stood on stage holding the trophy—a cheap plastic thing that tried to look like gold but fooled nobody. The $500 check was real though, as was the promise of studio time. For a kid whose mother counted every penny, it meant groceries, maybe even a car repair they'd been putting off.
"Speech!" someone yelled from the crowd. Then others took it up. "Speech! Speech!"
Nick approached the microphone like it might bite. Behind him, his bandmates beamed with the simple joy of underdogs validated. But Nick's face was complicated—victory and loss playing tag across his features.
"I, uh..." he started, then stopped. Looked at the trophy. Looked at Jackie. Looked at Patty. "This doesn't feel right."
The crowd quieted, confused. Winners were supposed to thank God and their mothers, not question the premise.
"This whole thing," Nick continued, finding his voice, "was supposed to be about music. About proving who was real, who was authentic, who deserved..." He trailed off again.
"But Patty showed us something. Jackie showed us something. Even those crazy kids in KISS makeup showed us something. Music isn't about winning. It's about..."
He was struggling, seventeen years old and trying to articulate something that adults spend lifetimes avoiding.
"It's about frequency," he finally said. "Finding the frequency where we all make sense together."
I watched Dr. Marsh's face during this speech—the way it shifted from anger to confusion to something more dangerous: dismissal. Because that's what power does when confronted with truths it doesn't like. It doesn't argue. It just reclassifies the truth-teller as someone who doesn't matter.
"So," Nick continued, "I want to share this. Jackie, Patty, get up here."
The crowd stirred. This wasn't how competitions worked. Winners didn't share. That was the whole point.
Jackie looked at his father, then at the stage, then made a choice that would cost him more than anyone knew. He walked toward Nick.
Patty was already moving, because she'd never believed in the divisions anyway.
"Ten-twenty-eight," I whisper. "Three kids on a stage, holding a plastic trophy between them like it was radioactive. Which, in a way, it was."
The photographer from the Sudbury Town Crier snapped a picture—three teenagers awkwardly sharing a trophy, trying to make meaning from plastic and promises. It would run in the paper under the headline "Battle Ends in Unity," missing the point entirely.
Because watch what happened next:
Dr. Marsh stood up. Didn't storm, didn't rage, just stood with the kind of dignity that had been perfected over generations of knowing when to leave. He caught Jackie's eye and made a small gesture toward the exit.
Jackie saw it. Understood it. The choice being presented: this moment of teenage idealism or the future that had been carefully planned since birth.
"I have to go," he said quietly to Nick.
"Yeah," Nick replied. "I know."
They were still sharing the trophy, but already letting go.
"Ten-thirty-one," I continue. "The competition was over, but the real battle was just beginning.”






Chapter 20: After the Music Stops
"Here's what they don't tell you about victories," I say, my voice getting rough from cigarettes and truth. "They're just the beginning of new divisions."
The cafeteria emptied in stages. First, the adults—Dr. Marsh leading an exodus of the offended, people who'd come expecting their children to win their wars by proxy. Then the kids from other towns, Concord and Wayland and Framingham, who had their own geographies to return to.
What remained was Sudbury itself, trying to figure out what had just happened.
I found myself in the parking lot, watching families load equipment into cars that told their own stories. The Crown Jewels' gear went into a Mercedes wagon, handled with the care of expensive things. Walking the Line's equipment got tetris'd into three different vehicles, none of which looked entirely road-legal.
"Ms. Sartori?"
I turned to find Grace Johnson, Nick's mother, still in her Friendly's uniform that now looked even more tired than when the evening began.
"Mrs. Johnson. Your son did good tonight."
She smiled, but it was complicated. "He won. His father would have been..."
She didn't finish. Didn't need to. We both knew that victories could feel like betrayals when the person you most wanted to impress was gone.
"That money will help," she said quietly. "The studio time, too. Maybe he can make something of this."
"Maybe," I agreed, but we both heard the doubt. Because making something in America required more than talent. It required access, connections, the kind of opportunities that didn't come to kids from the wrong side of Hop Brook.
Across the parking lot, I watched the Marsh family's careful choreography. Jackie loading his equipment while his parents waited in the running car, engine purring like a patient predator. Elizabeth had already been installed in the backseat, her violin case held like a teddy bear.
"Maxanne."
I turned to find Jackie approaching, having broken free from his family's orbit for a moment.
"That was brave," I said. "Going up there."
He laughed, but it sounded like something breaking. "Brave? I just signed my death warrant. Dad's already talking about summer programs, ways to 'refocus my priorities.'"
"And?"
"And he's probably right. This was just... this was just one night. Tomorrow, Nick goes back to his garage and I go back to my lessons and nothing really changes."
"You sure about that?"
He looked across the parking lot to where Nick was helping load amps, surrounded by his people, looking like a hero in denim and exhaustion.
"We're different species," Jackie said. "Tonight proved that. We can admire each other across the divide, maybe even remember when we were friends, but..."
"But?"
"But Hop Brook is still there. The dump is still there. The whole geography that makes us who we are is still there."
A horn honked—Dr. Marsh's patience exhausted.
"I have to go," Jackie said.
"Story of the night," I replied.
He started toward the car, then turned back.
"Tell Nick..." he started, then stopped. "Just tell him I remember. The garage. That summer. All of it."
Then he was gone, folded into the Mercedes, carried back to the side of town where victories were expected and therefore meaningless.
"Ten-forty-five," I announce to my 2024 audience. "I stood in that parking lot watching taillights disappear and understood that we'd just witnessed something both revolutionary and completely futile."
WBCN's news van was packing up, the story already being edited into something digestible. "Local Teens Bridge Differences Through Music." "Battle of the Bands Ends in Unity." Headlines that missed the point so completely they might as well have been about a different night entirely.
Because here's what really happened: for about forty-five minutes, three kids had shown us that the divisions we maintained were choices. That the brook could be crossed, the dump could be irrelevant, the geography of class could be temporarily suspended.
And then America remembered why it built those divisions in the first place.
"I need another cigarette," I say, fumbling with my pack. "Because the real tragedy wasn't that night. The real tragedy was what happened next. What always happens next. When the frequency that brought us together gets drowned in static, and we pretend that's just the way things are instead of the way we make them."
Chuck Morrison was loading the sound equipment, whistling "Dream On" off-key. He saw me and waved.
"Hell of a night, Maxanne. Think it'll change anything?"
I thought about Nick clutching that trophy he didn't want. About Jackie being driven back to a future he didn't choose. About Patty Lally already planning her next song about divisions that didn't need to exist.
"Yeah," I lied. "Sure it will."
Because that's what we do after moments of possibility. We lie about their impact. We pretend they matter more than they do. We tell ourselves that recognition is the same as change.
The parking lot was almost empty now. Just the janitors and the die-hards and those of us who professionally witnessed other people's dreams.
Somewhere, Nick Johnson was probably already home, trophy on a shelf where his father's tools used to hang, wondering if winning always felt this much like losing.
Somewhere, Jackie Marsh was being explained to about priorities and futures and the difference between hobbies and destinies.
Somewhere, Patty Lally was writing a new song about the night music almost mattered more than geography.
"Eleven PM," I say. "Time for me to drive back to the station, to play the hits that had already been chosen, to pretend that what we'd witnessed was just another battle of the bands instead of a small civil war where everybody lost."
I stood up to leave, and that's when I saw it—a piece of paper stuck under my windshield wiper. Not a ticket. A note.
"The frequency is still there. We just need better receivers. -P"
Patty. Of course. Still believing. Still seventeen. Still thinking songs could change things that money and geography and history had spent centuries perfecting.
I kept that note. Still have it, somewhere in the boxes I'll have to clear out when WBCN finally goes dark in about—I check the clock—thirty-seven minutes.
"Coming up," I say into the microphone, "Act Three. Where we learn what happens when you win a battle but lose a war. Where we discover that America always finds a way to put its children back in their proper boxes. Where we find out if any of it mattered, or if mattering is just another luxury that only one side of the brook can afford."
I cue up Aerosmith's "Dream On," the original this time.
"But first, a song about dreams. And about how sometimes the past really is gone. And about how that might be the saddest and most necessary truth we ever learn."
Steven Tyler's voice fills the booth, and I lean back, close my eyes, and remember three kids who almost changed everything.
Almost only counts in horseshoes and hand grenades.
And in music.
Sometimes.

Chapter 21: The Geography of Time
"Eleven-oh-five PM," I announce, my voice getting that late-night gravel that used to make program directors wince. "October 2024. Forty-five years since that battle, and I need to tell you what happened to those kids. Because America loves a reunion story almost as much as it loves creating the conditions that make reunions necessary."
I light what might actually be my last cigarette—the pack's almost empty, like everything else around here.
"But first, let me tell you about Sudbury now. Because geography changes even when people don't."
The dump? That stinking, Saturday-morning democracy where dads taught kids about waste and class without meaning to? It's gone. Closed in 1987 when the EPA discovered what forty years of American excess does to groundwater. But here's the kicker—the Cavicchio family, those pig farmers everyone looked down on? They bought the land for pennies on the dollar.
"Today," I continue, "that dump and pig farm is Cavicchio Greenhouses. Largest wholesale nursery in New England. The family that smelled like pig shit now sells million-dollar landscaping contracts to the tech executives who live in McMansions where the working-class quarters used to be."
I laugh, but it's the laugh of someone who's lived long enough to see every irony bloom.
"And Hop Brook? Some marketing genius started a microbrewery in 2003. Called it Hop Brook Brewing Company. Their flagship beer? 'Divided Waters Pale Ale.' Twenty-four dollars a six-pack. They sell it to hipsters who think they're buying authenticity instead of the commodification of class struggle."
But let me get back to 1979, to the weeks after the battle. Because that's when the real division happened—not with drama or confrontation, but with the slow inevitable drift of lives moving in directions that economics had predetermined.
"March twentieth, 1979," I say. "Five days after the battle. I had Nick Johnson and the boys in Studio C, using their prize studio time."
They showed up at midnight, as agreed. Four kids in their best attempt at rock star clothes—which meant their least-torn jeans and black t-shirts that had been washed for the occasion. They stood in the same studio where Aerosmith had recorded demos, where the J. Geils Band had found their sound, where Boston rock history had been made.
And they froze.
"It's too clean," Nick had said, looking at the pristine equipment, the foam-padded walls, the professional microphones that cost more than his mother made in a month. "It doesn't sound like us in here."
He was right. Walking the Line's authenticity came from garage acoustics, from the way sound bounced off concrete and tools, from the imperfection that made it real. In Studio C, they sounded like what they were—teenagers playing dress-up in a professional's world.
"We tried for six hours," I tell my dwindling audience. "Recorded four takes of 'Hop Brook Blues,' each one more sterile than the last. By six AM, we all knew the truth. Some music can't survive translation."

















Chapter 22: The Frequencies We Lost
"Meanwhile," I continue, pouring water because the bourbon's gone and my throat feels like I've been gargling gravel, "Jackie Marsh was undergoing what his parents called 'redirection.'"
Dr. Marsh had moved with the efficiency of someone who'd been planning for contingencies. By March 22nd, Jackie was enrolled in an intensive summer program at Tanglewood, the Boston Symphony Orchestra's summer home. By March 25th, his acceptance to Juilliard's pre-college program had been fast-tracked.
"I saw him once more that spring," I say, remembering the way hope dies in increments. "May of '79. He came to the station."
He looked different. Not older—it had only been two months—but carved down to someone his parents could recognize. The rebellion had been sanded off, leaving something smooth and acceptable and utterly lifeless.
"I wanted to tell you I'm leaving," he'd said, standing in the same spot where I'd first offered him the chance to be himself. "Juilliard accepted me for senior year. It's an opportunity I can't—"
"Can't refuse," I finished.
"Yeah."
We stood there in the control room, surrounded by the music that had promised to save us all, and said nothing that mattered.
"Nick?" I finally asked.
"We don't talk. Different worlds, you know? It was a nice dream, but..."
"But America doesn't believe in nice dreams."
He laughed, but it sounded like surrender. "Patty was right, though. About the frequency. Sometimes late at night, I can still hear it. That sound we made when we were thirteen. Before we knew we weren't supposed to harmonize."
"April 1980," I say to my 2024 audience. "I heard Nick had joined the Army. Not for patriotism or career. For the GI Bill. For the chance at college that Hop Brook had denied him."
Grace Johnson called me when he shipped out. Crying. Grateful. Terrified. Her boy was trading his body for education, the American working-class bargain that never gets discussed at Tanglewood.
"And Patty?" I continue. "Patty Lally kept writing songs. Moved to Cambridge, played the folk clubs, told truths that made people uncomfortable. Last I heard—this was '85—she was teaching music to kids in Roxbury, showing them how to find frequencies that the world insisted didn't exist.”



Chapter 23: The Last Frequency
"Which brings us to now," I say, checking the clock. Twenty-three minutes until WBCN dies. "October 2024, and I've got one more piece of this story to tell."
Two weeks ago, I got an email. Yeah, email—even dinosaurs like me learned to adapt. The subject line just said "The Frequency."
It was from Patty. First time I'd heard from her in fifteen years.
"Maxanne," she wrote, "I'm organizing something. Nick's construction company is doing the renovation on the old Lincoln-Sudbury music room. Jackie's in town—his father died, he's settling the estate. The timing feels like fate. Or tragedy. Or maybe they're the same thing."
She wanted me to be there. October 13th, 2024. A Sunday. The school would be empty except for construction crews and ghosts.
"I went," I tell my invisible audience. "Of course I went. When someone offers you the chance to see if frequencies can survive forty-five years of static, you go."
Nick owned Johnson & Sons Construction now—the sons being his two boys who'd never had to wonder if the lights would stay on. He looked like his father might have, if Dan Johnson had lived past thirty-five. Solid. Tired. Successful in the way that working-class kids define success—bills paid, kids fed, nobody dying in industrial accidents.
Jackie had become what his parents had crafted—a music professor at Berklee, teaching theory to kids who could afford to major in dreams. He'd married well, divorced better, and carried himself like someone who'd won by not losing too much.
They stood in that music room—renovated twice since '79, but still holding the same wrong acoustics—and looked at each other across forty-five years of different Americas.
"Patty made us play," I continue. "Dragged out three guitars from somewhere, wouldn't take no for an answer. Said something about frequencies not caring about time."
And here's the thing—when they started playing, it was terrible. Nick's fingers, thickened by decades of manual labor, stumbled on the frets. Jackie's technique was perfect but passionless, like a museum demonstration. They sounded like what they were—strangers who'd once been friends, trying to remember a language they'd been forced to forget.
But then...
"Then Patty started singing 'Common Ground,'" I whisper, because some memories require whispers. "The same song she'd sung in '79. And something shifted."
Maybe it was muscle memory. Maybe it was the ghosts in that room. Maybe it was just three people in their sixties realizing that time was running out to fix what America had broken. But they found it. For about ninety seconds, they found the frequency.
Nick's guitar simplified, found the spaces between notes where truth lives. Jackie's precision melted into something human. And when they harmonized on the chorus—"We're all standing on common ground"—I heard it. The sound of what could have been.
When they finished, the room was silent. Then Nick said what we were all thinking:
"Too late, isn't it?"
"Yeah," Jackie replied. "About forty years too late."
They packed up the guitars, shook hands like business acquaintances, and left separately. Nick to his truck. Jackie to his Tesla. Patty to her Subaru covered in Bernie stickers and lost causes.
"I stayed behind," I tell the darkness beyond the booth. "Sat in that renovated music room and thought about frequencies. About how we find them and lose them. About how America specializes in creating static between people who should be harmonizing."
On the wall, there's still a faded poster from 1979. You can barely make out the words: "Battle of the Bands." Some janitor had painted around it for forty-five years, preserving it like evidence of a crime or a miracle.
"Eleven-forty PM," I announce. "Twenty minutes left of WBCN. Twenty minutes to tell you what all this means."
I light my actual last cigarette—the pack's empty now, like the bourbon bottle, like this studio will be in twenty minutes.
"Here's what I learned in that music room," I say. "The frequencies are still there. Patty was right about that. But we've spent forty-five years building better static. Hop Brook got bridged, but by boutique shopping, not understanding. The dump became a greenhouse, but the shit just got buried deeper. Comic books became billion-dollar movies, but the kids who read them still divide into those who relate to outsiders and those who worship power."
I think about Chris Evans, Sudbury's own Captain America, playing a hero who believes in unity while the town he grew up in perfected division.
"We won that night in 1979," I continue. "All of us. For about forty-five minutes, we remembered that music could matter more than geography. That harmony was possible across any divide. That three kids who'd found the right frequency could make eight hundred people forget they were supposed to be enemies."
"And then we went home. And America reminded us why it built the divides in the first place. And we forgot. Or pretended to forget. Or remembered but decided remembering hurt too much."
The ON AIR light pulses. Eighteen minutes left.
"But here's the thing," I say, leaning into the microphone like I'm telling a secret. "Forgetting is a choice. Division is a choice. Static is a choice. We could choose differently. We could find the frequency again. We could..."
I stop. Because I'm seventy-three years old and I've run out of hope coupons. Because I've watched too many almosts become nevers. Because America is better at building brooks than bridges.
"Coming up," I whisper, "the final moments of WBCN. But first, one last song. For three kids who proved harmony was possible. For a town that chose static instead. For all of us who know the frequency exists but can't quite remember how to find it."
I reach for the cart I've been saving. The ninety-three-second demo from summer camp, 1974. Three thirteen-year-olds finding magic before the world explained why magic wasn't allowed.
Their voices fill the booth—young, hopeful, harmonizing like it's the easiest thing in the world.
Which it was.
Until we made it impossible.




Chapter 24: The Old North Bridge of Understanding
"Eleven-forty-three PM," I rasp into the microphone, my throat feeling like I've been swallowing history. "Seventeen minutes left, and I need to tell you about bridges. Real ones and metaphorical ones. The ones we cross and the ones we burn."
The ninety-three seconds of that demo tape fade out, leaving silence that feels heavier than sound.
"Sudbury has the Old North Bridge," I continue, "where the shot heard 'round the world was fired. Every schoolkid learns about it. April 19th, 1775. Patriots and Redcoats facing off over who owned the right to resist. But here's what they don't teach you—after the battle, both sides had to figure out how to live in the same town again."
I think about another battle, centuries later, in a church that had witnessed both revolutions and the smaller wars we wage against each other.
"1987," I say. "The Old North Church. A wedding that became its own battle of the bands, you might say. Catholic girl from the northeast quarter marrying a Jewish boy from Vermont. The mother who'd taught her children about the evils of prejudice suddenly discovering that principles are easier in theory than practice."
I don't name names—the family still lives in Sudbury, still carries those scars. But anyone listening who was there knows. The brother—couldn't have been more than twenty—standing at the podium, trying to read a poem about eternal love while sobbing like his heart was breaking.
"He cried through the whole reading," I remember aloud. "And everyone thought it was about young love, about sentimentality. But sometimes we cry because we recognize the moment when ideals meet reality. When the bridges we thought we'd built turn out to be more fragile than we'd imagined."
That young man crying at his sister's wedding—he was mourning something bigger than he could articulate. The discovery that even good people, loving people, people who'd taught you about equality and justice, could find themselves on the wrong side of their own values when the test came home.
"But she got married anyway," I continue. "In that church that had seen three centuries of people insisting their divisions mattered more than their commonalities. And you know what? They're still married. Raised kids who carry multiple histories, multiple frequencies. Built their own bridge across traditions that were never as separate as everyone insisted.”





Chapter 25: The Boston Post Road of Memory
"Eleven-forty-seven," I announce. "Thirteen minutes left, and I'm thinking about roads."
Boston Post Road. Route 20 to the modern world, but once the lifeline between Boston and the rest of America. Messages, packages, dreams, and disappointments all traveling by horseback along that corridor.
"Longfellow's Wayside Inn still sits on that road," I tell whoever's still listening at this ungodly hour. "Oldest operating inn in America. And down the road, the little red schoolhouse where Mary's lamb followed her to school, becoming the nursery rhyme every American child learns before they learn their own name."
I light a match—no more cigarettes, just the ritual of flame—and watch it burn down to my fingers.
"The Sudbury River runs under that road. 'Over the river and through the wood, to Grandmother's house we go.' Another children's song about journeys, about connections across distance. But here's what Lydia Maria Child didn't mention in 1844—sometimes the river is frozen, sometimes the woods are full of wolves, sometimes grandmother's house is on the wrong side of the economic divide."
But the road persists. Like music. Like memory. Like the frequencies we can't quite forget.
"I think about WBCN as another kind of post road," I continue. "For fifty-six years, we carried messages along the airwaves. Not by horseback but by radio wave. Songs traveling from Boston to the suburbs and back, carrying news of who we were, who we might become, who we were afraid to be."
The booth feels smaller now, or maybe I feel larger, swollen with all these stories I've been carrying.
"And just like the Boston Post Road got replaced by highways, bypassed by interstates, turned into a tourist curiosity instead of a lifeline, radio is being replaced by algorithms. By playlists that know what you want to hear before you do. By artificial intelligences that can predict your taste but can't recognize when a song might save your life."
I pause, feeling the weight of ending things.
"But roads leave traces. Even when they're paved over, built around, forgotten. The old paths persist under the new ones. The frequencies remain, waiting for someone to find them again.”






Chapter 26: The Frequency of Silence
"Eleven-fifty-one PM," I whisper. "Nine minutes. Time enough for one more story. One more song. One more chance to explain why any of this mattered."
I've saved this for last. The thing I've never said on air. The reason I've spent four hours telling you about three kids who found and lost harmony.
"1991," I begin. "I got a letter. Postmarked from Seattle, where the next revolution was starting without us. It was from Nick."
His handwriting looked like his father's—careful, solid, the penmanship of someone who'd learned that words on paper mattered because you might not get another chance.
"Maxanne," he'd written. "My son started a band. He's fifteen, same age I was. But he doesn't have to choose between authentic and educated. His bandmates include a kid from Bellevue who went to Exeter and a girl from Tacoma whose dad works at Boeing. They don't care about the divisions. They just care about the sound."
"He asked me to teach him 'Hop Brook Blues,'" the letter continued. "But when I played it for him, he said it sounded like ancient history. Like singing about a war nobody remembers. And maybe he's right. Maybe that's what victory looks like—when the battles become so irrelevant that the next generation can't understand why we fought them."
I never wrote back. What could I say? That Seattle would find its own divisions? That grunge would split into authentic and commercial just like every other movement? That his son's band would eventually have to choose sides?
"But here's what I wish I'd told him," I say now, to Nick wherever he's listening, to Jackie in his Tesla, to Patty still fighting wars everybody else considers over. "Your son was right. The divisions are ancient history. We just haven't figured out how to stop living in the past."
The clock on the wall—analog, because this booth is a museum already—ticks toward midnight.
"Eleven-fifty-five," I announce. "Five minutes left of WBCN. Five minutes left of the Rock of Boston. Five minutes left of pretending that music can change anything."
But I've got one more song to play. One I've been saving for forty-five years.
"In 1980," I say, "after Jackie had gone to Juilliard, after Nick had joined the Army, after the Battle of the Bands had become just another story about how things don't change, Patty Lally came back to the station. She had a demo tape. One song. She said it was for me, for Nick, for Jackie, for everyone who'd been there that night."
I've never played it on air. Never played it for anyone. Kept it like evidence of a crime or a prayer or a possibility that hurt too much to share.
"It's called 'After the Battle,'" I say, sliding the cart into the machine with hands that won't stop shaking. "And if you've stayed with me this long, through all these stories about division and frequency and the distances we maintain, then you deserve to hear how the story really ends."
Patty's voice fills the booth—older than in 1979 but still carrying that truth that made everyone listen:
"After the battle, when the smoke has cleared,
When the winners are forgotten and the losers disappeared,
When the trophies gather dust and the stages fall apart,
That's when music matters, that's when we restart..."
Her guitar is simple—three chords and the truth, as Woody Guthrie prescribed. But the words...
"Nick's in Carolina, building bridges out of steel,
Jackie's in a classroom, teaching kids what's real,
I'm in Massachusetts, singing to an empty room,
But the frequency that joined us still cuts through the gloom..."
"Eleven-fifty-eight," I whisper. "Two minutes left."
"So here's to all the battles that we thought would change our lives,
Here's to all the mornings when division still survives,
Here's to bridges built and burned and built again in vain,
Here's to finding frequencies despite the static's pain..."
The song fades out, but Patty's voice continues—spoken, not sung:
"Maxanne, Nick, Jackie—we proved it was possible. Even if just for one night. Even if the world made us forget. The frequency exists. The harmony is real. The bridges can be built. We just have to keep trying, keep playing, keep believing that music matters more than the silence between us."
"Eleven-fifty-nine and thirty seconds," I announce. "Thirty seconds left."
I lean into the microphone one last time.
"This is Maxanne Sartori, signing off from WBCN, 104.1 on your FM dial. For fifty-six years, we've been the Rock of Boston. We've played the hits and the misses, the legends and the losers, the battles and the truces."
Ten seconds.
"But mostly, we've played the frequency of possibility. The sound of what could be if we just—"
Five seconds.
"—stopped choosing static—"
Two seconds.
"—over songs."
Midnight.
The ON AIR sign goes dark. The booth falls silent. WBCN is dead.
But somewhere in Sudbury, on a road that used to carry messages by horseback, in a town divided by water and wealth and waste, three kids are still harmonizing in the garage of memory. Still proving it's possible. Still waiting for the rest of us to find the frequency.
The silence stretches out. But if you listen carefully—if you know how to tune past the static—you can still hear them singing.
You can always hear them singing.
If you remember how to listen.
