Chapter 2: The Garage That Held More Than Cars

I cue up Aerosmith's "Dream On" from their first album and let it play while I gather my thoughts, methodically arranging the remaining carts for tonight's broadcast with the obsessive care that thirty years of radio has trained into my muscle memory.
"A garage," I say on air, "can be a temple if you know how to worship."
I'd driven out to Dutton Road three weeks before the Battle of the Bands, my powder-blue Karmann Ghia looking ridiculous navigating the potholes that the town somehow never fixed on the working-class side of Hop Brook. But I'd heard rumors. A kid who made Steven Tyler sound like he was holding back. A garage where the sound hit you in the chest before you even opened the door.
The Johnson house squatted like it was tired: peeling paint the color of old mustard, sagging gutters that had given up pretending to channel water properly, front steps that listed to port like a ship taking on water slowly, gracefully, inevitably. The lawn had surrendered to New England winter months ago, brown grass poking through dirty snow like stubble on a dead man's face. A single window glowed yellow in the kitchen, where Grace Johnson was probably sitting at the Formica table, still in her Friendly's uniform, calculating whether the heat could stay on until next week's paycheck.
But from the detached garage came something alive—distorted power chords that made my radio-trained ears perk up like a dog hearing its master's key in the lock.
"Mind if I listen?" I'd asked Grace Johnson when she answered the door after three knocks, wiping her hands on a dish towel that had seen better decades. She was still in her Friendly's uniform, the polyester blend that made everyone look like they were melting slowly under fluorescent lights. The name tag read GRACE in that cheerful corporate font that lies about everything—how much you matter, how much you're worth, how much dignity comes with asking if someone wants fries with that.
She studied me—this Boston radio personality in bell-bottoms and oversized sunglasses, standing on her cracked front step like some visitor from a planet where people had money for clothes that didn't come from Jordan Marsh clearance racks. "You're that lady from WBCN. The one who broke Aerosmith."
"Guilty as charged."
"Nick's in the garage." She paused, and I saw something flicker across her face—hope, maybe, or fear that hope was dangerous, expensive, something they couldn't afford to budget for. "Don't fill his head with dreams we can't afford."
The warning hung in the March air between us like visible breath, each word carrying the weight of a woman who'd learned that dreams were luxuries that came after rent, groceries, car repairs, and all the other mathematics of survival that the middle class never had to calculate to the penny.
Back in the booth, 2024, I adjust the turntable speed with practiced precision, understanding that even memory requires proper calibration to be heard accurately.
"The thing about Nick Johnson's garage," I tell my invisible audience, "was that it remembered his father."
Dan Johnson had died on November fourteenth, 1977, a Tuesday morning so ordinary that nobody saw tragedy coming until it arrived with sirens and flashing lights. He was working the Prudential Tower project, thirty-seven stories of Boston ambition rising from the Back Bay like a middle finger pointed at the sky. Dan ran a crane, one of those massive yellow beasts that could lift ten tons of steel beam like it was picking up a pencil.
The cable snapped at 10:17 AM. OSHA would later determine that the cable had been scheduled for replacement two weeks earlier, but the general contractor had delayed the order to save money on a project already running over budget. The load—four steel I-beams destined for the thirty-second floor—fell like God's judgment onto the construction trailer where Dan had been checking blueprints, eating a tuna sandwich Grace had made that morning, probably thinking about Nick's algebra grades or the mortgage payment due the following week.
They said he died instantly, which is what they always tell the families because instant death is supposed to be a mercy, as if the speed of dying somehow makes up for the fact of it. Grace never believed them. She knew Dan well enough to know he would have fought it, would have tried to dive under the desk or behind the file cabinet, would have spent his last seconds thinking about getting home to his wife and seventeen-year-old son who was learning guitar in the garage Dan had built with his own hands.
The construction company paid the minimum required by law—$50,000, which sounds like a fortune until you realize it has to last the rest of two lives. The insurance company fought the claim for eight months, arguing that Dan had violated safety protocols by being in the trailer during a lift operation, as if death wasn't punishment enough without adding blame to grief.
Grace had to go back to work three weeks after the funeral. Friendly's gave her two weeks bereavement leave, which was considered generous for a part-time employee, and then she was back behind the counter asking if people wanted to make their sundae a full-size for just fifty cents more, smiling because tips depended on smiles and tips made the difference between keeping the house and moving to the kind of apartment complex where thin walls carried the sound of other people's failures.
I take a measured sip of bourbon, feeling the warmth while I clean the vintage needle with the reverent attention these mechanical companions deserve after decades of faithful service.
His tools still hung on the pegboard in the garage, arranged with the precision of a man who understood that organization was the difference between efficiency and chaos, between getting home in time for dinner and working overtime you couldn't afford to turn down. Hammers arranged by size, from the delicate eight-ounce finish hammer to the four-pound sledge that could drive fence posts or demolish small worlds. Wrenches in descending order, each one hanging exactly where Dan's muscle memory expected to find it. The socket set was missing the 9/16ths—he'd had it in his pocket that Tuesday morning, probably planning to tighten something when he got home, some small domestic repair that would never happen now.
The garage smelled like motor oil and sawdust, WD-40 and the ghost of Dan's Old Spice aftershave. It was twenty feet by twenty feet of American masculinity, the kind of space where fathers taught sons about the dignity of working with your hands, about the difference between the right tool and the good-enough tool, about how to fix things instead of throwing them away and buying new ones.
When I entered the garage that March afternoon, Nick was mid-solo, his fingers finding notes that shouldn't have been possible on a guitar held together with electrical tape and determination. The instrument had cost twenty-five dollars at a pawn shop in Framingham, the kind of place where dreams go to die and occasionally get resurrected by teenagers with lawn-mowing money and more hope than sense.
Three other kids backed him—all from that forgotten corner of Lincoln that most Sudbury kids pretended didn't exist. Stephen Murphy, David Cassel, Mark Pianka—inseparable since elementary school, musical outcasts who'd found in Nick the fourth corner of a square they didn't know they were building. Stephen on bass, tall and thin like John Entwistle if Entwistle had grown up on food stamps and hand-me-downs. David behind a drum kit assembled from three different sets, none matching but all thunderous. Mark on keyboards, fingers doing all his talking on a Casio his grandmother had given him for Christmas when he was twelve.
They were social pariahs at Lincoln-Sudbury, the Lincoln boys who rode the bus from the forgotten town, who ate lunch by themselves, who'd never been invited to parties they couldn't afford to attend anyway. But put instruments in their hands and they became something else—a rhythm section that locked together like they shared the same heartbeat, like they'd been born to make music no one else wanted to hear.
They stopped when they saw me, that teenage terror of being caught in the act of caring about something, of revealing that beneath the carefully constructed armor of not-giving-a-shit was a desperate need to matter, to be heard, to prove that their lives meant something more than the statistics that said kids like them didn't make it out.
"Don't stop on my account," I said, settling onto a paint can in the corner, breathing in the mixture of motor oil and teenage ambition that made the garage smell like possibility and limitation in equal measure. "Play it like you mean it."
They exchanged glances—four working-class boys deciding whether to trust this intruder from the world of actual radio, where records got played and careers got made and people like them occasionally escaped the gravity of their neighborhoods. Nick made the decision for them, launching back into "Dream On" with a fury that transformed the garage into something larger than its concrete dimensions.
Here's what I need you to understand about genuine expression: it's not something you can learn at expensive music schools, where privileged kids go to study the blues they've never lived. It's not in perfect pitch or precision fingering or the kind of technical skill that comes from costly lessons and patient teachers. It's in the way Nick Johnson's voice cracked when he hit the high notes, not from poor technique but from feeling too much to contain, from trying to compress seventeen years of grief and rage and love into three minutes of sound.
It's in the way his hands moved on the fretboard like they were trying to pull music out of the wood itself, like the guitar was a divining rod searching for water in a desert of broken dreams and deferred hopes. It's in the way he sang "Every time that I look in the mirror, all these lines on my face getting clearer" and made you believe that seventeen years could feel like seventy when you've been the man of the house since fifteen, when you've watched your mother cry over bills when she thought you weren't looking, when you've learned that dreaming was something you did quietly so nobody could take it away from you.
I organize my remaining playlist cards with the methodical precision that's kept me sane through four decades of Boston radio chaos.
When they finished, the silence in that garage was holy. Not church holy—none of us were the church type—but sacred in the way that only truth can be sacred, the kind of quiet that happens after something real has been spoken in a world full of lies.
"What's your band called?" I asked, my voice rougher than I'd expected.
Nick shrugged, that teenage gesture that protects against caring too much, against revealing how desperately you want something you're probably not going to get. "Don't have a name yet. We're just... playing."
"You're doing more than playing," I said, standing up from the paint can, my knees protesting because concrete floors are unforgiving even to thirty-five-year-old knees that think they're still young. "You got a demo?"
The other boys looked at Nick like he was the designated spokesperson for their collective dream. He shook his head. "Can't afford studio time."
I pulled out my card—back when WBCN business cards meant something, when showing one could open doors instead of starting conversations about the decline of traditional media and the rise of streaming services and how everything was different now and mostly worse.
"Come by the station. After midnight, when the suits are gone and the real work gets done. We'll lay down some tracks, see what you sound like when you're not competing with the ghost of a dead man's tools."
The hope on those boys' faces could have powered half of Boston. The kind of hope that hurts to look at directly, like staring at the sun, because you know how rare it is and how quickly it can be extinguished by the thousand small cruelties that America visits on kids who dare to believe they might matter.
But Nick hesitated, and in that hesitation was everything his mother had taught him about being careful, about not reaching for things that were too high, too far, too expensive for people like them.
"Why?" he asked, and his tone carried the wariness of someone who'd learned that adults offering opportunities usually wanted something in return. "Why would you help us?"
I looked around that garage—at Dan Johnson's tools hanging like relics of a more hopeful time, at the oil stains on the concrete floor that told the story of a thousand small repairs, at the four boys holding instruments like life preservers in an ocean of limited options.
"Because rock and roll is supposed to be for kids like you," I said, meaning every word. "Kids who play in garages because concert halls don't know they exist yet. Kids who understand that music isn't decoration—it's survival. Because your father's tools on that pegboard know more about honest work than half the industry executives in this city."
I paused at the garage door, looking back at four boys who were trying not to hope too hard, trying not to believe that this moment might be the one that changed everything.
"And because Steven Tyler played his first gig in a garage not much bigger than this one. The only difference between him and you is someone opened a door."
But here's the thing about doors—I thought I was opening one for Nick Johnson and his band of working-class dreamers. I didn't know I was also closing another, somewhere across Hop Brook, where a different boy was struggling with his own kind of prison, his own need to be heard, his own desperate desire to turn pain into music and music into meaning.
Sometimes the cruelest thing about opportunity is how little of it there is to go around.
