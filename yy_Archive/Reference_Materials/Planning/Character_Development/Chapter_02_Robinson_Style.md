# Chapter 2: The Garage That Held More Than Cars
## Marilynne Robinson Style

I cue up the next track—Aerosmith's "Dream On" from their first album—and let it play while I gather my thoughts, while I try to remember how to tell this story in a way that will make you understand what it meant, what it still means, though so much time has passed and so much has changed. The bourbon catches the console lights like amber trapped in glass, older than my babysitting engineer and twice as smooth, and Steven Tyler's voice fills the booth with that raw ache that made every working-class kid in Boston believe they could be heard, believe that their pain mattered, that their dreams were worth something in a world that seemed determined to crush them.

"A garage," I continue on air, "can be a temple if you know how to worship, if you understand that the sacred is not found in grand cathedrals or ornate sanctuaries but in the places where ordinary people gather to do extraordinary things, where the noise of the world is drowned out by the sound of something being born, something that has never existed before and may never exist again."

I had driven out to Dutton Road three weeks before the Battle of the Bands, my powder-blue Karmann Ghia—yes, I was that kind of 1970s cool, though the coolness seems foolish now, seems like the vanity of youth—looking ridiculous navigating the potholes that the town somehow never fixed on the working-class side of Hop Brook. The car bottomed out twice, sending sparks dancing across the asphalt like tiny fireworks, and I remember thinking that this was appropriate, that this was somehow fitting, that even the journey to discover Nick Johnson should be marked by small explosions, by the sound of metal scraping against stone, by the reminder that beauty often comes at a cost.

But I had heard rumors. A kid who made Steven Tyler sound like he was holding back, like he was somehow restraining himself when the whole point of rock and roll was to hold nothing back, to pour everything you had into the moment and let the chips fall where they may. A garage where the sound punched you in the chest before you even opened the door, where the music was not just heard but felt, where the walls themselves seemed to vibrate with the energy of something that could not be contained.

The Johnson house squatted like it was tired, like it had been carrying too much weight for too long and was finally beginning to sag under the burden. The paint was peeling, the gutters were sagging, the lawn had surrendered to New England winter and seemed to have no intention of ever recovering. The whole place looked like it was holding its breath, waiting for something to break, waiting for the moment when the accumulated weight of deferred maintenance and deferred dreams would finally prove too much to bear. But from the detached garage came something alive, something that refused to be defeated—distorted power chords that made my radio-trained ears perk up like a dog hearing its master's whistle, that made me understand, in that instant, that I was about to witness something extraordinary.

"Mind if I listen?" I had asked Mary Johnson when she answered the door, and she was still in her Friendly's uniform, the name tag reading MARY in that cheerful corporate font that lies about everything, that promises happiness and delivers only exhaustion, the polyester fabric clinging to her frame like a second skin that never quite fit right, that reminded you constantly of all the ways the world had tried to make her into something she was not.

She studied me—this Boston radio personality in bell-bottoms and oversized sunglasses, standing on her cracked front step like some visitor from another planet, like someone who had wandered into her world by accident and might wander out again just as easily. "You're that lady from WBCN," she said. "The one who broke Aerosmith."

"Guilty," I replied, though I had never thought of it that way before, had never considered that what I did was breaking anything, that I was somehow responsible for the trajectory of these young men's lives, for the way their music had changed the world.

"Nick's in the garage," she said, and then she paused, and I saw something flicker across her face—hope, maybe, or fear that hope was dangerous, or perhaps it was both, the terrible knowledge that hope and fear are often the same thing, that to dream is to risk disappointment, that to believe in someone is to make yourself vulnerable to their failure. "Don't fill his head with dreams we can't afford," she added, and her voice carried the weight of someone who had learned, through bitter experience, that dreams were luxuries, that hope was a currency that ran out too quickly, that the world was not kind to those who believed in impossible things.

Back in the booth, in 2024, I watch my engineer scroll through his phone, and I realize that he has no idea what a garage means to rock and roll, what it represents in the mythology of American music. He grew up with GarageBand, thinking music could be manufactured in a bedroom with the right plugins, with the right software, with the right algorithms. He has never felt the sacred geometry of four walls and a concrete floor, has never smelled the motor oil and sawdust that made every note sound like a prayer for escape, has never understood that the garage was not just a place where music was made but a place where dreams were born, where the impossible became possible, where kids who had nothing to lose discovered that they had everything to gain.

"The thing about Nick Johnson's garage," I tell my invisible audience, "was that it remembered his father, was that it held within its walls not just the tools of construction but the tools of creation, not just the evidence of honest work but the possibility of honest art."

Dan Johnson had died two years earlier, crushed beneath a load of steel beams when a crane cable snapped—the kind of industrial accident that makes page three of the Metro section and gets forgotten by everyone except the families who eat silence for dinner afterward, who learn to live with the particular kind of grief that comes when someone you love dies in a way that could have been prevented, in a way that makes you wonder if the world is not simply indifferent to human suffering but actively hostile to it, if there is not some malevolent force at work that takes pleasure in the destruction of good men. His tools still hung on the pegboard like a shrine to honest work: hammers arranged by size, wrenches that would never turn another bolt, a socket set missing the 9/16ths he had had in his pocket that day, the metal still warm from his last grip, still carrying the imprint of his hand, the evidence of his presence in a world that had moved on without him.

When I entered the garage, Nick was mid-solo, his fingers finding notes that should not have been possible on a guitar held together with electrical tape and determination, on an instrument that had been rescued from a pawn shop and nursed back to health through sheer force of will. The sound hit me like a physical force, vibrating through the concrete floor and up through my bones, and I understood, in that moment, that this was not just music but something more, something that transcended the ordinary categories of sound and became instead a form of prayer, a way of speaking to God or to the universe or to whatever force it was that had created us and given us the capacity for both joy and suffering.

Three other kids backed him—all Lincoln boys, I would learn later, from the even-smaller town that shared a high school with Sudbury but not much else, boys who had grown up in the shadow of their more prosperous neighbors but had somehow managed to retain their dignity, their sense of self-worth, their belief that they mattered even when the world seemed determined to prove otherwise. They stopped when they saw me, that teenage terror of being caught in the act of caring about something, of being vulnerable, of admitting that music mattered to them in a way that went beyond mere entertainment, the music dying mid-note like a bird shot out of the sky.

"Don't stop on my account," I said, settling onto a paint can in the corner, and I was aware, even then, of the absurdity of the situation, of the way that this moment, this sacred moment, was taking place in the most ordinary of settings, surrounded by the tools of construction and the evidence of honest work. "Play it like you mean it."

They exchanged glances—four working-class boys deciding whether to trust this intruder from the world of actual radio, their eyes darting between me and the door like cornered animals, like creatures who had learned, through bitter experience, that trust was dangerous, that hope was often punished, that the world was not kind to those who believed in the possibility of something better. Nick made the decision for them, launching back into "Dream On" with a fury that transformed the garage into something larger, something sacred, something that could hold all their dreams and fears at once, that could contain the full weight of their hopes and disappointments, their victories and defeats, their belief that somehow, someday, things would be different.

Here is what I need you to understand about authenticity: it is not something you can learn at Berklee, not something that can be taught or purchased or acquired through study. It is not in the perfect pitch or the precision fingering, not in the technical mastery that comes from years of practice and discipline. It is in the way Nick Johnson's voice cracked when he hit the high notes, not from poor technique but from feeling too much to contain, from having experienced so much pain and so much joy that his voice could no longer hold it all, from being so fully present in the moment that he could not help but reveal himself completely, could not help but show us who he really was beneath the armor of adolescence and the weight of circumstance. It is in the way the garage walls seemed to lean in, listening, as if Dan Johnson's ghost was nodding along to his son's pain made music, as if the very air itself was transformed by the sound of something being born, something that had never existed before and might never exist again.

"Every time that I look in the mirror," Nick sang, and his voice carried all the weight of a seventeen-year-old who had been the man of the house since he was fifteen, who had learned, through bitter experience, that childhood was a luxury he could not afford, that the world demanded more from him than he had ever imagined it would. "All these lines on my face getting clearer..."

Seventeen years old, singing about aging, about the way time leaves its mark on us all, about the way that experience etches itself into our flesh and our souls until we can no longer recognize ourselves in the mirror, until we become strangers to the people we used to be. But he understood something about time that most teenagers do not—how fast it steals what you love, how young you can feel old when you are the man of the house before you are even a man, when you have been forced to carry burdens that should never have been yours to bear.

When they finished, the silence in that garage was holy, was the kind of quiet that happens after truth has been spoken, when the last note hangs in the air like incense, when even the dust motes seem to hold their breath in reverence, when the very walls themselves seem to understand that something extraordinary has just happened, that they have witnessed the birth of something that will never be the same again.

"What's your band called?" I asked.

Nick shrugged, that teenage gesture that protects against caring too much, that allows you to maintain your dignity even when your heart is breaking, that lets you pretend that what you have just done is not the most important thing in the world, even though you know it is. "Don't have a name yet," he said. "We're just... playing."

"You're doing more than playing," I said, and I was aware, even then, of the weight of what I was saying, of the responsibility I was taking on by recognizing their talent, by acknowledging that what they were doing mattered, that it was not just noise or rebellion but something sacred, something that deserved to be heard. "You got a demo?"

The other boys looked at Nick, and I could see the hope in their eyes, the desperate need for validation, for someone to tell them that they were not just wasting their time, that their dreams were not foolish, that the music they were making was worth something in a world that seemed determined to prove otherwise. Nick shook his head. "Can't afford studio time," he said, and his voice carried the weight of someone who had learned, through bitter experience, that talent was not enough, that dreams required resources, that the world was not set up to reward those who had the most to offer but the least to give.

I pulled out my card—back when WBCN business cards meant something, when they represented not just a job but a calling, when they were a ticket to a world where dreams could come true—and I said, "Come by the station. After midnight, when the suits are gone. We'll lay down some tracks."

The hope on those boys' faces could have powered half of Boston, could have lit up the whole city from Southie to Somerville to Sudbury and back, could have reminded everyone who saw it that dreams were not dead, that hope was not foolish, that the world still contained the possibility of magic, of transformation, of the kind of beauty that can only be created when people believe in something larger than themselves. But Nick hesitated, his eyes flickering between the card and the tools on the wall, between possibility and the weight of responsibility, between the dream of what could be and the reality of what was.

"Why?" he asked. "Why would you help us?"

I stood up, my knees protesting—even at thirty-five, concrete floors were unforgiving, the kind of cold that seeps into your bones and reminds you that you are not as young as you used to be, that time takes its toll on us all, that even the strongest among us eventually succumb to the weight of years—and I said, "Because rock and roll is supposed to be for kids like you. Kids who play in garages because concert halls don't know they exist yet. Because your father's tools on that wall know more about honest work than half the industry executives in this city. Because the world needs your voice, needs your truth, needs the sound of something real in a time when everything seems artificial, when everything seems designed to numb us to the pain of living."

I paused at the garage door, looking back at four boys holding instruments like life preservers, like the only things that could keep them afloat in a world that seemed determined to drown them, and I said, "And because Steven Tyler played his first gig in a garage not much bigger than this one. The only difference between him and you is someone opened a door."

But here is the thing about doors—I thought I was opening one for Nick Johnson, thought I was giving him a chance to escape the limitations of his circumstances, thought I was offering him a way out of the garage and into the world of professional music, where his talent could be recognized and rewarded, where his dreams could finally come true. I did not know I was also closing another door, did not realize that by bringing him into the world of radio, by introducing him to the machinery of the music industry, I was also taking him away from something pure, something innocent, something that could never be recovered once it was lost.
