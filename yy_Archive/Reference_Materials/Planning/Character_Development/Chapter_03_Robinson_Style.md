# Chapter 3: The Other Side of Hop Brook
## Marilynne Robinson Style

"Now," I continue on air, checking the clock—three hours and twenty-seven minutes left of WBCN, three hours and twenty-seven minutes left to tell this story that has been waiting inside me for forty-five years, waiting for the right moment, the right audience, the right combination of circumstances that would make it possible to speak the truth without being dismissed as nostalgic or irrelevant—"let me tell you about the prince in the tower, about the other side of Hop Brook, about what happens when privilege meets passion and discovers that they are not as different as they appear to be."

The same week I discovered Nick Johnson's garage, I was invited to a very different kind of performance, and the invitation itself told a story about the way the world works, about the way that some people's dreams are nurtured while others are ignored, about the way that talent is recognized and rewarded when it comes wrapped in the right package, when it arrives with the proper credentials and the appropriate pedigree. Dr. Richard Marsh had called the station personally, which was unusual, because university professors typically had their secretaries handle such mundane tasks, typically delegated the work of arranging their lives to people whose job it was to make sure that the important people did not have to trouble themselves with the details of existence.

But Dr. Marsh wanted to make sure I understood the significance of his son's recital, wanted to ensure that I appreciated the magnitude of what I was about to witness, wanted me to know that this was not just another high school performance but something approaching art, something that deserved the attention of someone who understood the difference between amateur enthusiasm and professional excellence.

"Jackie trained at the New England Conservatory prep division," he had said, his voice carrying that particular Boston Brahmin accent that turns 'car' into 'cah' but somehow makes it sound aristocratic rather than working class, that transforms the ordinary act of speech into a statement of social position, a declaration of belonging to a world where certain things are simply understood, where certain assumptions are so deeply ingrained that they need never be spoken aloud. "He's being considered for early admission to Juilliard."

I had wanted to decline. Classical recitals were not my beat, were not the kind of music that spoke to the audience I had spent my career cultivating, were not the sound of rebellion and authenticity that had made WBCN the voice of a generation. But something in his voice—a father's pride mixed with something else, something almost like fear, like the terrible knowledge that even the most carefully cultivated talent could be destroyed by circumstance, that even the most promising future could be derailed by the wrong choice, the wrong moment, the wrong person—made me agree.

The Marsh house stood on the good side of Hop Brook like it had been placed there by a decorator, like it had been designed specifically to demonstrate the difference between those who had succeeded in life and those who had not, between those who understood the proper way to live and those who were still learning, still struggling, still trying to figure out how to make their way in a world that seemed determined to make things difficult for them. Colonial perfection, black shutters against white clapboard, lawn so green it looked painted, so carefully maintained that it seemed to exist in a different season than the rest of the world, in a different reality where winter did not come and grass did not die and everything remained exactly as it was supposed to be.

You could walk from Nick Johnson's garage to this front door in twelve minutes, could traverse the physical distance between these two worlds in less time than it takes to listen to a single song, but you would cross an invisible border that might as well have been made of barbed wire, that separated not just two different neighborhoods but two different ways of understanding the world, two different sets of assumptions about what was possible and what was not, two different languages for describing the same human experiences.

"The thing about that recital," I tell my audience, fading down The Cars' "Just What I Needed," "was that it showed me the other half of the tragedy, was that it revealed to me the way that privilege can be as confining as poverty, the way that having everything you need can sometimes mean having nothing you want, the way that being born into the right circumstances can sometimes be the greatest obstacle to discovering who you really are."

The living room had been transformed into a concert space, and the transformation itself was a statement about the way this family understood the world, about their belief that art deserved a proper setting, that music was something that should be presented with all the trappings of respectability and sophistication. Folding chairs in perfect rows, a rented Steinway that cost more than Mary Johnson made in a year, champagne chilling in silver buckets for the afterward—because even the celebration of art had to be done properly, had to follow the correct protocols, had to demonstrate that these people understood how things were supposed to be done.

The audience was prep school parents and university faculty, the kind of people who applauded with their fingertips and discussed summer houses on the Cape between selections, the kind of people who understood that culture was not just about enjoying music but about demonstrating that you knew how to appreciate it properly, that you understood the difference between entertainment and art, that you belonged to the class of people who could afford to care about such things.

Jackie Marsh entered in a tuxedo that fit like money, seventeen years old but carrying himself like someone who had been preparing for this moment since birth, like someone who had been raised to understand that his worth was measured not by who he was but by what he could accomplish, not by the content of his character but by the quality of his performance. He was beautiful in that way that good nutrition and orthodontics and SAT tutors can manufacture, beautiful in the way that comes from having never had to worry about whether you would have enough to eat, whether you would have a place to sleep, whether the people you loved would be safe from the violence and chaos that seemed to surround so many other children's lives.

But when he sat at the piano, something changed, something shifted in the atmosphere of the room, something that made everyone sit up a little straighter and pay a little more attention, because they could sense that this was not just a performance but something more, something that transcended the ordinary categories of recital and entertainment and became instead a form of communication, a way of speaking across the vast distances that separated human beings from one another.

He opened with Chopin, because of course he did, because Chopin was what you played when you wanted to demonstrate that you understood the proper way to approach the piano, that you had mastered the technical requirements of classical music, that you belonged to the tradition that stretched back through the centuries and connected you to all the great musicians who had come before. The notes were perfect—every one struck with precision that spoke of thousands of hours of practice, of lessons that started before memory, of parents who considered anything less than excellence a character flaw, who understood that talent was not enough, that potential was meaningless unless it was realized, that the world would judge you not by what you might become but by what you actually accomplished.

The audience nodded appreciatively, already composing the compliments they would deliver with their champagne, already preparing to tell Jackie's parents how impressed they were, how certain they were that this young man had a bright future ahead of him, how proud they were to know a family that had produced such a remarkable child.

But I was watching Jackie's face, and I saw something his parents missed, something the audience could not see from their carefully arranged chairs, something that made my heart ache with recognition: he was dying in there, was suffocating under the weight of all that perfection, was drowning in the expectations that had been placed upon him since before he could remember, was being crushed by the terrible knowledge that he was living someone else's dream, that he was playing someone else's music, that he was becoming someone else's idea of who he was supposed to be.

Behind the technical perfection was a boy playing someone else's definition of music, speaking someone else's language with flawless pronunciation but no soul, demonstrating that he could master the forms and conventions of classical music but revealing nothing of himself in the process, showing us that he had learned to perform but had forgotten how to feel, had acquired the skills of a musician but had lost the heart of an artist.

Then something shifted. Between the Chopin and the obligatory Bach, Jackie paused, and his father cleared his throat—a warning about maintaining proper recital pacing, about not allowing the moment to get away from him, about remembering that this was a performance and that performances had rules, had expectations, had audiences who had paid for a certain kind of experience and would be disappointed if they did not receive it. But Jackie was not listening to his father. He was listening to something else, to some voice that spoke from a place deeper than instruction, from a place more ancient than tradition, from a place where music was not about demonstrating mastery but about expressing truth.

His fingers found different keys, and the opening notes of "Bohemian Rhapsody" emerged from that Steinway like escapees from prison, like something that had been locked away for too long and was finally free to be itself, like a bird that had been caged and was suddenly able to spread its wings and fly. The room froze. You could hear pearls clutching themselves, could feel the shock that rippled through the audience like an electrical current, could sense the moment when everything changed, when the carefully constructed world of classical respectability was suddenly invaded by the raw energy of rock and roll, when the proper order of things was disrupted by something that refused to be contained, that refused to be categorized, that refused to accept the limitations that had been placed upon it.

"Is this the real life?" Jackie sang, his classically trained voice doing things Freddie Mercury would have killed for, his perfect technique now serving a different master, now expressing a different kind of truth, now speaking in a language that his audience did not understand but could not help but respond to, because it spoke to something deeper than their education, something more fundamental than their sophistication, something that connected them to the raw, unfiltered experience of being human. "Is this just fantasy?"

Here is what that room full of culture did not understand: Jackie Marsh was not defiling their temple. He was consecrating it. He took their precious Steinway and made it confess that rock and roll was just classical music that had learned to dance, that the distinction between high and low culture was artificial, that the barriers we build between different kinds of music are nothing more than the prejudices of people who have forgotten that all music comes from the same source, that all art is an attempt to express the inexpressible, that all creativity is an act of faith in the possibility of transcendence.

For four minutes and fifty-five seconds, Jackie Marsh was free, was finally able to be himself, was finally able to express what he really felt instead of what he was supposed to feel, was finally able to speak in his own voice instead of the voice that had been imposed upon him by years of training and expectation. His hands found harmonies that should not have been possible on a solo piano, his voice soared through octaves that made the champagne glasses vibrate, and he turned that stuffy living room into Wembley Stadium, transformed that space of privilege and respectability into a place where anything was possible, where the ordinary rules of behavior did not apply, where the only thing that mattered was the music and the truth it contained.

When he finished, the silence was different from the holy quiet in Nick's garage. This was the silence of scandal, of proper people trying to process impropriety, of an audience that had come expecting one thing and received something else entirely, something they were not prepared for, something that challenged their assumptions about what music was supposed to be, about what young people were supposed to do, about what constituted proper behavior in polite society.

Dr. Marsh was the first to speak. "That was... unexpected," he said, and his voice carried the weight of someone who was trying to salvage the situation, who was attempting to put the genie back in the bottle, who was hoping that if he just pretended that nothing unusual had happened, then perhaps it would be true.

"That was rock and roll," I said, standing up because someone needed to, because someone had to acknowledge what had just happened, because someone had to speak the truth that everyone else was too polite or too shocked or too confused to acknowledge. "That was your son showing you who he really is."

Jackie looked at me then, really looked, and I saw the same thing I had seen in Nick Johnson's eyes: a desperate need to be heard as himself, not as someone's projection of who he should be, not as the fulfillment of someone else's dreams, not as the embodiment of someone else's ambitions, but as a human being with his own thoughts and feelings and desires, with his own understanding of what music meant and what it was supposed to do.

"You're Maxanne Sartori," he said. "You played our record."

Our record. Not Aerosmith's or Queen's, but our record, like every kid who had ever heard their dreams on the radio and thought maybe, just maybe, they mattered, like every young person who had ever believed that their voice could be heard above the noise of the world, like every artist who had ever hoped that their work could transcend the limitations of their circumstances and speak to something universal in the human experience.

"I play music," I corrected. "Sometimes it comes from famous bands. Sometimes it comes from kids in garages. And sometimes," I looked directly at Dr. Marsh, "it comes from piano prodigies who understand that Chopin would have loved electric guitars if he had lived to see them, who know that the distinction between classical and popular music is artificial, who understand that all great art comes from the same place—the human heart trying to express what cannot be expressed in any other way."

I gave Jackie my card—the same one I had given Nick three days earlier—and I said, "Come by the station. Bring whatever music lives in you, not what lives in your parents' expectations. Bring the truth that you just showed us, the truth that you have been carrying around inside you all these years, the truth that you thought you had to hide but that the world needs to hear."

But here is the cruelest part of this story: I thought I was saving two boys from the boxes they had been born into, thought I was offering them a way out of the limitations that had been imposed upon them by circumstance and expectation, thought I was giving them a chance to be themselves in a world that seemed determined to make them into something else. I did not realize I was setting them on a collision course that would prove boxes are easier to escape than each other, that the greatest obstacle to freedom is not the external limitations imposed by society but the internal divisions we create within ourselves, that the hardest thing to overcome is not the circumstances of our birth but the ways we learn to see ourselves and each other.

Back in 2024, I drain my bourbon and pour another. My engineer looks concerned—probably worried I will pass out before the final sign-off, probably wondering if this old woman has finally lost her mind and is going to spend the last hours of WBCN's existence rambling about things that happened so long ago that they no longer matter. But he does not understand that some stories require anesthesia, especially the ones where you were supposed to be the hero but turned out to be just another adult who did not see the train coming until it had already crashed, just another person who thought they were helping but was actually making things worse.

"Two boys," I tell whoever is still listening out there in the dark. "Two sides of Hop Brook. Both prisoners of expectations, both gifted with the kind of talent that makes the universe pay attention. They should have been allies. Hell, they had been allies, once upon a time in middle school, when they were young enough to believe that music could overcome any obstacle, that friendship could transcend any boundary, that the world was full of possibilities instead of limitations."

I check the clock: three hours and twelve minutes left.

"But I'm getting ahead of myself. Before I tell you about the battle, I need to tell you about the war. And every war needs its recruiting station. In this case, it was a bulletin board in the Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School music room, and a simple piece of paper that would turn a competition into a crusade, that would transform a friendly rivalry into something that would divide a town and break two hearts that should have been beating in rhythm."
