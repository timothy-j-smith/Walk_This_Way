Novel Synopsis
WALK THIS WAY
On October 15, 2024, Maxanne Sartori, legendary DJ who discovered Aerosmith and launched Boston rock, prepares for WBCN's final broadcast before the station dies at midnight. At 73, she has four hours to tell one last story—not about the bands who made it, but about two boys whose musical partnership could have changed everything if America hadn't perfected the art of division.
The story unfolds in two timelines. In 2024, Maxanne broadcasts from WBCN's dying booth, weaving between on-air monologues and private revelations, counting down to midnight when the station will flip to Christian radio. In 1979, we witness the story she's telling: Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh, once inseparable friends in middle school, now seniors at Lincoln-Sudbury High, divided by Hop Brook—the stream that separates Sudbury's working class from its upper-middle elite.
Nick lives with his widowed mother Grace, who works double shifts at Friendly's restaurant since his father Dan died in a construction accident, crushed under steel beams when a crane cable snapped. His garage band, Walking the Line, practices among his dead father's tools, making authentic rock from grief and class rage. Jackie, son of a Boston University professor who writes about the working class without living among them, and a Wellesley-educated poet mother, has been trained for excellence since birth. His band, Crown Jewels, practices in a basement worth more than Nick's house, performing Queen covers with classical precision but no soul.
The novel's first act establishes Sudbury's geography of division: four quadrants separated by Hop Brook and the town dump (with its adjacent Cavicchio pig farm), by railroad lines that cross but don't connect, by parents who preach tolerance while practicing segregation. Through flashbacks, we learn Nick and Jackie once harmonized perfectly at a 1974 music camp with Patty Lally from Concord, before the world explained why working-class authenticity and upper-class education couldn't mix.
When Lincoln-Sudbury announces its Battle of the Bands for March 15, 1979, what begins as a school competition becomes a class war. WBCN's coverage, led by Maxanne herself, transforms it into a regional event. Eight bands enter, each representing different aspects of Sudbury's divisions: The Jesters (working-class Lincoln kids), Cherry Bomb (all-girl punk challenging male rock supremacy), The Establishment (rich kids' sterile precision), Dead Heads (stoner transcendence), Crown Jewels, Walking the Line, and unexpectedly, Rumours—Patty Lally's Concord band that refuses to choose sides.
The battle itself occupies the novel's entire second act, with each band receiving a full chapter for their performance. The evening progresses from segregated seating to gradual mixing as music temporarily dissolves boundaries. Jackie's technical perfection cracks when he breaks into Elton John's "Your Song," revealing the emotion his parents tried to train out of him. Patty's original songs explicitly name the shared past: "Three kids found a frequency in 1974 / Before the world explained what harmony was for." Nick's devastating original "Hop Brook Blues" tells the truth about division with such raw power that even the judges—teachers who maintain these divisions daily—can't ignore it.
When Walking the Line wins, Nick attempts to share the victory with Jackie and Patty, but Dr. Marsh's immediate intervention forces Jackie to choose between momentary unity and his predetermined future at Juilliard. The parking lot aftermath reveals all divisions immediately reasserting themselves, what Maxanne calls "both revolutionary and completely futile."
Act Three jumps between the immediate aftermath (1979-1980) and Maxanne's 2024 broadcast. Extended chapters follow the slow dissolution: Jackie's isolation at Tanglewood then Juilliard, becoming the musician his parents designed; Nick's decision to join the Army for the GI Bill, trading his body for education while his mother Grace continues her exhausting shifts at Friendly's; Patty's evolution into a Cambridge folk singer and Roxbury music teacher, "showing kids how to find frequencies the world insists don't exist."
By 2024, the geography has transformed but divisions persist. The Cavicchio pig farmers—who once blocked Henry Ford's factory with their water rights—now own the largest greenhouse empire in New England. The dump has become their goldmine. Railroad lines are bike paths where former enemies jog together without recognizing the irony. Hop Brook has a microbrewery selling "Divided Waters Pale Ale" for $24 a six-pack. Nick owns a construction company that restores the history he couldn't afford to inhabit. Jackie teaches at Berklee, brilliantly analyzing what he once lived. Patty still plays Tuesday nights to shrinking crowds.
As midnight approaches, Maxanne plays Patty's never-aired 1980 recording "After the Battle," a song about how "the frequency that joined us still cuts through the gloom." The novel culminates with WBCN going dark at midnight, but Maxanne's final words insist the frequency persists: "somewhere in Sudbury, three kids are still harmonizing in the garage of memory," waiting for us to stop choosing static over songs.
