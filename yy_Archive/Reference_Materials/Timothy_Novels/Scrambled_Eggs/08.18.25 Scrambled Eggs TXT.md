08.17.25 & 8.18.25 Scrambled Eggs

SCRAMBLED EGGS A Novel Timothy J. Smith

Chapter 1: Friday Evening - The Devil's Bargain
The 2 X 2 X 2 was always the best deal at Uncle Percy's, though there weren't a lot of fancy breakfast items on the one-page laminated menu that had been yellowed by fifteen years of coffee steam, cigarette smoke, and the accumulated weight of small-town secrets. The menu itself was a relic from another era, when breakfast cost under five dollars and nobody asked too many questions about where you'd spent the night or why your hands were shaking when you ordered your third cup of coffee.
Sometimes, if you were lucky, you could order breakfast all day long. That's if Percy was on the grill, and not his son Larry.
Larry was the better cook by miles—always preparing the eggs exactly to your specification, the toast browned to geometric precision that would have impressed a NASA engineer, and the bacon, ham, or sausage cooked to temperatures he monitored with an infrared thermometer he kept clipped to his apron like a surgical instrument. But Larry was a man whose internal clockwork ran on Swiss precision, wound too tight by demons that whispered about perfection and control and the terrible things that happened when either one slipped even slightly.
He would not—could not—accept any breakfast orders after 11:20 AM. He'd calculated that the Eggstravaganza Omelet, the most complex item on the menu, required exactly eleven minutes and forty-three seconds to execute to his standards, and therefore, being fair to all customers, breakfast service ended at 11:20 AM sharp. Not 11:21. Not 11:20 and thirty seconds. At 11:20:00, breakfast was dead to him, and he would stand sentinel at the grill like a bald-headed guard dog protecting his territory.
Percy watched his son work the dinner rush now, watched those precise hands moving with the mechanical efficiency of someone who'd learned that controlling small things might somehow let you control the big things that really mattered. Larry's head gleamed under the kitchen lights like a pearl formed by pressure—twenty-seven years old and completely bald, the hair abandoning ship during his senior year of culinary school when the stress of achieving perfection had finally overwhelmed his follicles' will to live. His face was perfectly round, flushed red like a ripe tomato even when he wasn't near the heat, as if his internal pressure was constantly seeking escape through his pores.
"Order up," Larry called, sliding a plate through the pass-through window with the kind of precision that suggested he'd measured the exact distance and calculated the optimal sliding velocity. "Chicken fried steak, mashed potatoes still steaming, green beans arranged in parallel formation."
Percy took the plate, noting how his son's left eye twitched whenever a green bean dared to roll out of alignment. That twitch had been getting worse lately, especially since the divorce papers had arrived. Not Larry's divorce—the boy had never let anyone get close enough to marry—but the final documentation of Percy's own marital dissolution, twelve years after the fact. Some ghosts took their time dying.
The diner was quiet for a Friday evening. Just Molly Swisher wiping down tables with the kind of careful attention that suggested she was thinking about something else entirely, and old Cooper Hendricks nursing his fourth cup of coffee in the corner booth, pretending to read a newspaper that was three days old.
Percy had learned to read the rhythms of his place like a cardiogram. Quiet could mean many things in the desert. Sometimes it meant a storm was coming, the pressure dropping so fast even the coyotes sought shelter. Sometimes it meant trouble was brewing somewhere down the highway, the kind that made smart people stay home and lock their doors. And sometimes, like tonight, it meant nothing at all except that the world had decided to take a breath.
Molly moved between the tables like a dancer who'd learned to make herself small, her black hair pulled back in a ponytail so severe it seemed to stretch the corners of her dark eyes. She'd been working here three months now, ever since she'd appeared one morning like a desert mirage, asking for work in a voice that carried traces of Texas wrapped in careful neutrality. She'd claimed restaurant experience, and her hands proved it—the way she could balance four plates on one arm, the economy of motion as she refilled coffee, the ability to appear at your table exactly when needed and disappear when conversation turned private.
But it was her eyes that had made Percy hire her. Dark brown, almost black, with the kind of hypervigilance that belonged to people who'd learned that danger could wear a smile and speak in pleasant tones. He recognized that look. He'd seen it in his own mirror for twelve years now, ever since that October afternoon in the Hollywood Hills when he'd been trimming the dying oak in Harrison Blackwood's backyard and had witnessed something that had transformed him from a man who shaped trees into a man who understood that some trees bore fruit too terrible to name.
"Coffee?" Molly asked, appearing at his elbow with the pot already tilted, steam rising like incense in the fluorescent light.
"Half a cup," Percy said. "Leave room for disappointment."
She almost smiled—he could see it flickering at the corners of her mouth before she suppressed it, as if joy was a luxury she couldn't afford. "Isn't that what the cream's for?"
"Cream's for optimists," Percy replied. "I take mine black as a tax attorney's heart."
This time she did smile, quick as a hummingbird's visit, there and gone before you could properly appreciate it. She moved on to Cooper's table, and Percy watched her in the convex security mirror mounted in the corner—a habit he'd developed without meaning to, tracking the movements of everyone in his space like a blackjack dealer watching for card counters.
The bell above the door chimed its tubercular wheeze, and Percy looked up to see a man in an expensive suit that belonged in Century City, not the Mojave Desert at the ass-end of nowhere. The incongruity was jarring, like finding a orchid growing in an oil stain.
Everything about the man screamed money and menace in equal measure. His shoes were Italian leather that had never known dust, polished to a mirror shine that reflected the diner's sickly fluorescent lighting. His suit was charcoal gray, tailored to accommodate the slight bulge under his left armpit that suggested he carried more than business cards. His hair was silver-gray, styled with the kind of precision that Larry would have appreciated if it had been applied to egg preparation instead of appearance.
But it was his smile that made Percy's hand move unconsciously toward the Louisville Slugger he kept behind the counter. The smile of a man who'd learned that showing teeth was just another form of aggression.
"Mr. Percy Jameson?" the man asked, extending a manicured hand that had never held anything heavier than a Mont Blanc pen or a Croatian's throat.
Percy didn't take the offered hand. In the security mirror, he saw Molly freeze mid-pour at Cooper's table, her body language shifting from server to something else, something coiled and ready. Cooper, bless him, just kept pretending to read his newspaper, though Percy noticed he'd moved his coffee cup to his left hand, leaving his right free.
"We're closing," Percy said, though the clock showed they had two hours left.
"Oh, I'm not here for the food." The man's smile widened, and Percy caught a glimpse of dental work that cost more than most cars. "Though I've heard wonderful things about your eggs. Always scrambled, never quite what people order. There's something almost... philosophical about that, isn't there?"
The man moved into the diner with the confidence of someone who'd never been denied entry anywhere. He chose the booth closest to the counter, sliding in with movements that were too smooth, too practiced, like someone who'd spent years perfecting the art of deception.
"I'm Richard Blackwood," he said, pulling out a leather portfolio that matched his shoes. "I believe you used to know my father. Harrison Blackwood? You did some tree work for him about twelve years ago."
The name hit Percy like a physical blow, though he kept his face carefully neutral. In the kitchen, he heard Larry's knife pause in its chopping, his son's radar for tension as finely tuned as his thermometer readings.
"I trimmed a lot of trees in my past life," Percy said carefully. "Can't say I remember every client."
"Oh, I think you'd remember this one." Blackwood's smile never wavered, but his eyes were cold as January stars. "October 15th, 2012. You were working on a diseased oak in the backyard. Beautiful tree, apparently. Shame it had to come down. Almost as much of a shame as what happened to the pool house that same day."
Percy's mouth went dry. The pool house. White marble and chlorine and a child's voice saying "Please" in accented English before the splash, before the silence that followed when pool water accepts what it shouldn't.
"I develop property now," Blackwood continued, spreading papers across the table like tarot cards revealing a future nobody wanted. "This whole corridor, actually. From Barstow to the Nevada line. Amazing what you can do with desert land when you have the right... transportation infrastructure."
He pushed a photograph across the table. An aerial view of Uncle Percy's Diner, surrounded by red circles and development projections. But Percy's eyes were drawn to the smaller photos paper-clipped to the corner—trucks at night, their license plates visible, their cargo doors open to reveal shadows that might have been anything. Or anyone.
"Two million dollars," Blackwood said. "Cash. No questions about the past, no complications about the future. You take your son, you take your staff, and you find somewhere else to scramble eggs. Somewhere far from here. Somewhere you won't be tempted to remember things that are better forgotten."
Percy looked at the photographs again. One of the trucks belonged to Earl Donovan, who drove for an independent outfit and always ordered his eggs over easy even though Percy always scrambled them. Another belonged to Jake Hendricks, who came in every morning at six sharp with Wally Morrison and Pond Anderson, the four of them moving like a unit, sitting in the same booth, ordering the same breakfast, talking about routes and weather and cargo manifests in voices just loud enough to be overheard but not understood.
The Train, Percy called them, though never to their faces. Four men bound by something more than friendship, something that had to do with the loads they carried and the routes they drove and the way they never asked questions about delays or detours or extra passengers who didn't appear on any manifest.
"I'm not selling," Percy said.
"Your father thought he saw something once," Blackwood said, his voice dropping to a whisper that carried more menace than a shout. "Thought he could be a hero. You know what happened to him? Neither do I. Neither does anyone. That's the thing about the desert, Mr. Jameson. It's so vast. So many places for things to get lost. Trucks. Cargo. People who remember things they shouldn't."
He gathered his papers with practiced efficiency, leaving only a business card on the table. Heavy stock, embossed lettering, the kind of card that whispered money and threatened violence in the same breath.
"Think about it over the weekend," Blackwood said, standing with the fluid grace of a man who stayed in shape for professional reasons. "Monday morning, I'll need an answer. After that, well... the market can be unpredictable. Property values can drop suddenly. Sometimes they burn down. Sometimes the health department finds violations that require immediate closure. Sometimes the owners just... disappear."
He walked to the door, paused, and looked back. "Your son makes excellent eggs, by the way. Perfect, even. It would be a shame if he never got the chance to cook them again."
The bell chimed as he left, and Percy watched through the window as Blackwood climbed into a black Mercedes with windows tinted dark enough to hide whatever went on inside. The car sat in the parking lot for a full minute before pulling away, heading east toward California, toward the Hollywood Hills, toward memories that Percy had spent twelve years trying to bury in the desert sand.
"You okay, boss?" Molly had materialized beside him, the coffee pot forgotten on Cooper's table. Her hand rested on the counter near his, not touching but close enough to offer comfort if he wanted it.
"Just business," Percy said, but his voice sounded hollow even to himself.
"The kind of business that comes with threats?" She was looking at the business card like it might bite. "The kind that knows about your past?"
Percy looked at her sharply, but her expression was carefully neutral. How much did she know? How much had she guessed? In three months, she'd never asked about his history, never wondered aloud why a man with a degree in botany and a successful tree surgery business was running a diner in the middle of nowhere. Just like he'd never asked why a woman with her intelligence and capability was waiting tables instead of doing whatever she'd done before that had taught her to spot danger before it fully revealed itself.
"Molly," he said quietly. "Whatever you're running from, whatever brought you here... it might be time to run again."
She was quiet for a long moment, then shook her head. "I'm tired of running. Aren't you?"
From the kitchen came the sound of Larry attacking vegetables with renewed vigor. But now he was dicing them smaller and smaller, reducing carrots to confetti, his knife moving in the rapid rhythm of someone trying to cut his way through to understanding. His son had heard everything, Percy knew. Had heard Blackwood's threat against him, against the perfect eggs he'd spent three years learning to cook with mathematical precision.
"Dad?" Larry's voice came through the pass-through, strained but trying for normal. "We're low on potatoes. And the carrots are... well, they're pretty much destroyed now."
Percy almost smiled despite everything. His son's way of saying he was scared, that he knew something was wrong, but he wasn't going to push. Not yet.
"We'll get more tomorrow," Percy said. "When The Train comes through."
But even as he said it, Percy wondered if there would be a tomorrow for any of them. In the parking lot, tire tracks from Blackwood's Mercedes were already being erased by the desert wind, but the threat lingered like the smell of rain in a drought—promising violence disguised as relief.
Through the window, Percy could see the first stars appearing in the darkening sky. Somewhere out there, trucks were moving through the night, carrying cargo that didn't appear on any manifest. Somewhere, a man in an expensive suit was making phone calls to people who specialized in making problems disappear.
And here, in a diner that served scrambled eggs no matter what you ordered, a small group of broken people were about to discover that sometimes the only way to stop running was to make a stand, even when you knew the ground beneath your feet was nothing but sand and secrets and the bones of those who'd stood before.
Chapter 2: Saturday Morning - The Train Arrives
The desert dawn came reluctantly, like a drunk being dragged toward sobriety. Percy hadn't moved from the corner booth since Blackwood left, hadn't slept, hadn't done anything but drink coffee that went from hot to cold to bitter memory. His reflection in the dark window showed a man who'd aged twelve years in one night—or maybe he'd always looked this old and just hadn't noticed.
At 4:30, Larry arrived. Percy heard the back door, the precise click of locks, the ritual of lights being switched on in exact sequence. Neither of them spoke when Larry entered the dining room and saw his father sitting there like a ghost haunting his own life. Larry just went to the kitchen and began his morning prep, the sound of his knife on the cutting board steady as a heartbeat.
By 5:45, the coffee was fresh and the bacon had begun its sizzle. Percy moved behind the counter on autopilot, his hands knowing their business even when his mind was twelve years in the past, watching water close over a young girl's face while Richard Blackwood checked his stopwatch.
The bell chimed at exactly 6:00 AM.
The Train entered in their established order, and Percy felt something crack inside his chest. They moved with such easy familiarity, such trust in the permanence of this ritual. Earl's massive frame, Jake's scarecrow angles, Wally's nervous energy, Pond's careful navigation of the harsh lighting. Four men who'd found something here they couldn't find anywhere else.
They arranged themselves in their booth—Earl and Jake facing the door, Wally and Pond trusting the others to watch their backs. Percy's hands moved without thought, pouring four coffees, each prepared exactly how they liked it. Earl black, Jake with one sugar, Wally with cream, Pond with two sugars and just enough cream to make it the color of his pale skin.
"Morning, Percy," Earl said, and Percy heard what wasn't said. The careful normalcy, the pretense that nothing had changed.
"Boys," Percy managed.
They drank their coffee in silence that felt different from their usual comfortable quiet. This was the silence of men who'd heard about the Mercedes, who understood that their sanctuary had been marked.
"Scrambled eggs all around?" Percy asked, though they always ordered the same things. Earl's over easy that would come out scrambled. Jake's bacon extra crispy. Wally's wheat toast that Percy only had white for. Pond's fruit cup that was usually just a sad apple Percy cut up.
"Actually," Earl said, and Percy's hand froze on the order pad. In five years, Earl had never deviated. "Maybe I'll try them sunny side up today."
The words hung in the air like a confession. Earl wanting his eggs different, wanting them perfect, as if perfect mattered. As if Percy could deliver perfect when he couldn't even protect the imperfect.
"I only do scrambled," Percy said quietly. "You know that."
"I know." Earl's massive hands wrapped around his coffee cup like he was holding something precious. "Just thought... maybe today could be different."
But today couldn't be different. Today was the same scrambled eggs in the same broken place with the same broken people pretending that routine could save them from what was coming.
From the kitchen, Larry's knife work had taken on a frantic quality. Chop chop chop, faster and faster, the sound of someone trying to cut their way out of a trap.
"Load troubles," Jake said into the silence. His weathered face showed every one of his sixty years this morning. "Dispatcher called last night. Said my regular Phoenix run got cancelled. No reason given."
"Mine too," Wally added, his usual smile nowhere to be found. "Tuesday's Nevada haul. Gone."
Pond just nodded, his pink eyes watering behind thick glasses. He didn't need to say it—they all understood. Someone was squeezing them, cutting off their income streams one by one.
"It's about me," Percy said. "Not you. I can—"
"Shut up, Percy." Earl's voice was gentle despite the words. "Just... shut up."
They sat in their booth, four men who'd driven together, eaten together, built something together that didn't have a name but mattered more than anything that did. Percy saw them clearly for perhaps the first time. Not The Train, not some unified entity, but four individual men with individual fears.
Earl, whose massive hands shook slightly when he lifted his cup. Those hands that could wrestle a big rig through a sandstorm, trembling over coffee.
Jake, pressing his palm against his lower back where the pain lived, the arthritis that would eventually end his driving days. How many more years did he have? How many more mornings in this booth?
Wally, drumming his fingers on the table in a rhythm that suggested medication wearing off, anxiety creeping back in. The smile he wore like armor, absent now, revealing the frightened man beneath.
Pond, hunched deeper into himself than usual, knowing that men like Blackwood saw men like him as disposable. Weird. Weak. The kind that disappeared and nobody asked questions.
"I should take the money," Percy said.
"Probably," Jake agreed.
"Two million buys a lot of new starts," Wally added.
"Could set Larry up real nice," Earl contributed.
"Safe," Pond whispered. "Could be safe."
But they all knew safe was a lie. Men who paid two million to bury the past didn't leave witnesses walking around with full bank accounts. Percy had run once, and Blackwood had found him. Running again would only delay the inevitable.
Molly appeared with the coffee pot, moving quietly as always. But this morning Percy noticed the way she checked the windows, the door, the parking lot. The way her free hand stayed close to her apron pocket where something heavier than an order pad created a telling bulge.
She refilled their cups without being asked, and Percy saw her notice Earl's tremor, Jake's pain, Wally's anxiety, Pond's fear. Saw her catalog their weaknesses with the clinical assessment of someone who'd learned that weakness got you killed.
"Food'll be up in a minute," she said, though no one had ordered yet.
She was giving them normalcy, Percy realized. The gift of routine when routine was all they had left.
The bell chimed and everyone tensed. But it was just Cooper Hendricks, shambling to his usual counter stool with his three-day-old newspaper. He nodded to The Train, ordered his coffee, and began his performance of reading news he'd memorized days ago.
This was their world. These were their people. Not heroes, not fighters, just tired men going through motions that meant everything precisely because they meant nothing.
"My wife," Earl said suddenly, staring into his coffee, "used to make perfect eggs. Every morning for thirty years. Sunny side up, whites firm, yolks runny. Like art."
They waited. Earl never talked about his wife.
"When she died, I tried to make them myself. Couldn't do it. Burned them, broke them, made a mess. So I came here." He looked up at Percy. "And you served me scrambled eggs. Didn't apologize, didn't explain, just... scrambled. And they were good. They were honest."
His voice broke on the last word.
"That's what this place is. Honest. We come here because everywhere else wants us to pretend we're something we're not. Successful. Whole. Normal." He laughed, bitter as burnt coffee. "But here? Here we can just be scrambled."
Jake shifted, pressing harder against his back pain. "Remember when I started coming here? Five years ago?"
Percy nodded.
"I'd just lost my house. Sixty years old and sleeping in my truck because I'd trusted the wrong people with my money. Embarrassed to tell anyone. But I could come here, order breakfast, feel human for an hour."
"The lights," Pond said softly. "Everywhere else, the lights hurt my eyes. But here, Percy hung those amber filters. Never said anything, just... did it. So I could eat without squinting."
Wally's fingers drummed faster. "My anxiety meds. Sometimes I can't afford them. Makes me jumpy, makes people nervous. But here? Nobody stares. Nobody asks if I'm okay. Just pour my coffee and let me be."
They were confessing, Percy realized. Not sins, but vulnerabilities. The real reasons Uncle Percy's mattered. Not because it was special, but because it wasn't. Because it let them be unspecial together.
"He wants to take this," Percy said. "Turn it into something else. Something efficient. Something profitable."
"Something perfect," Larry said from the kitchen doorway. He'd been listening, had stopped his frantic chopping to hear these men explain why his father's imperfect diner mattered more than his perfect eggs ever could.
The sound of an engine made them all turn. A white van, official-looking, pulling into the parking lot. The health inspector's seal on the door like a brand.
"Saturday morning," Jake said. "They never inspect on Saturday."
"They do when they're paid to," Molly said quietly.
The inspector was young, nervous, carrying a clipboard like a shield. He didn't want to be here—Percy could see it in the way he avoided eye contact, the way he kept checking his phone as if hoping for a reprieve.
"Mr. Jameson? I need to conduct an emergency inspection. There've been... complaints."
"On Saturday," Percy repeated.
"I'm sorry. I have orders."
Orders. Of course. Everyone was just following orders. The inspector who would find violations that didn't exist. The bureaucrats who would rubber-stamp the closure. The men who would eventually come to ensure compliance. All just following orders, none responsible for the small destructions that added up to devastation.
"Do what you need to do," Percy said.
The inspector began his performance, and it was clearly that—a performance with a predetermined outcome. He found problems with equipment that had passed every previous inspection. He measured temperatures that were suddenly wrong. He discovered code violations in systems that hadn't changed in fifteen years.
The Train watched from their booth, witnesses to a murder committed with paperwork and procedure. Larry stood frozen in the kitchen doorway, watching his perfectly maintained equipment fail an inspection that was never meant to be passed.
Two hours later, the inspector handed Percy a notice that might as well have been a death certificate. Closure effective Monday morning. Extensive remediation required. Fines that would bankrupt them even if they could afford the repairs.
"I'm sorry," the kid said again, and Percy believed him. Another small cog in Blackwood's machine, grinding forward regardless of what got crushed beneath.
After he left, they sat in silence. The breakfast rush should have been starting, but who had appetite for food when they'd just watched their sanctuary condemned?
"Monday," Earl said finally. "Two days."
"We could fight it," Wally suggested without conviction. "Lawyers, appeals—"
"With what money?" Jake asked. "We can't even get loads anymore."
They were beaten and they knew it. Not by violence, but by paperwork. Not by threats, but by procedure. This was how the world really worked—not with dramatic confrontations but with quiet strangulation, cutting off air supply until you begged for the mercy of a quick death.
"I need some air," Percy said.
He stood in the parking lot, looking at his diner in the harsh morning light. Such an ugly little building, really. Aluminum siding gone gray with age, windows that needed replacing, a sign with letters missing so it sometimes read "UNC E P RCY'S" depending on which bulbs had burnt out.
But God, how he loved it.
Molly appeared beside him, quiet as always. "You're going to take the money."
It wasn't a question.
"I have to. Look at them. Earl's about to lose his truck. Jake can barely drive with his back. Wally needs his meds. Pond... Christ, what happens to someone like Pond when places like this disappear?"
"They find other places."
"No," Percy said. "They don't. They just... fade. Become invisible. Stop mattering to anyone."
"You can't save everyone, Percy."
"I couldn't save anyone. Couldn't save that girl twelve years ago. Couldn't save my marriage. Can't even save this place." He laughed, short and bitter. "You know what I am? I'm a coward who makes scrambled eggs."
"No," Molly said firmly. "You're a man who makes scrambled eggs for other scrambled people. And that matters. It matters so much that Richard Blackwood has to destroy it, because it proves his father was wrong. That broken things can still be good. That imperfect people can create perfect moments."
She touched his arm, briefly, the first time she'd ever initiated contact.
"Whatever you decide, we understand. But don't lie to yourself about what this place means. Don't diminish it just because you couldn't save it."
She went back inside, leaving Percy alone with the desert and his thoughts and the weight of Monday morning pressing down like the sky had turned to stone.
Inside, he could see The Train still in their booth. His family of choice, gathered for what might be their last real morning together. By Monday, the diner would be closed. By Tuesday, they'd scatter to the winds. By Wednesday, it would be like Uncle Percy's had never existed except in the memories of people who didn't matter to anyone but each other.
Two million dollars.
It was so much money. And so little. Not enough to buy back twelve years of guilt. Not enough to purchase redemption for the girl in the pool. Not enough to compensate for the small death that happened every time a sanctuary became a commodity.
But maybe enough to help The Train find new roads to travel. Maybe enough to get Larry started somewhere fresh. Maybe enough to buy Molly time to keep running from whatever chased her.
Percy walked back inside to the smell of bacon and coffee and the quiet conversation of people who knew their world was ending but were too polite to mention it. He picked up the coffee pot and began his rounds, pouring refills for men who pretended their hands weren't shaking.
This was love, he realized. Not the dramatic kind, but the quiet kind. The kind that poured coffee and scrambled eggs and never asked why you were crying into your breakfast. The kind that couldn't be bought or sold or improved into something more efficient.
The kind that was about to die because that's what love did in the face of power. It died quietly, with dignity if you were lucky, leaving only the faint smell of bacon grease and the ghost of conversations that had mattered desperately to people who didn't.
Two days.
Two days to say goodbye to everything that had made his exile bearable. Two days to pretend that money could heal what money had destroyed.
Two days before Uncle Percy's became just another casualty of progress, and the scrambled eggs gave way to whatever perfection Richard Blackwood had in mind.
The coffee was bitter this morning. Or maybe that was just the taste of surrender.
Chapter 3: Saturday Afternoon - The Weight of Knowing
The lunch rush never came. Word traveled fast in the desert, carried on CB radios and whispered through truck stop networks: Uncle Percy's was dying. The health notice might as well have been a quarantine sign. Only the desperate or loyal would eat at a condemned diner.
By 2 PM, the place was empty except for Percy and Larry. The Train had dispersed to their trucks after breakfast, each man needing to make phone calls, check loads that wouldn't come, count money that wouldn't last. The parking lot looked like a truck stop cemetery, big rigs sitting silent in the heat.
Larry was cleaning the grill with obsessive attention, scraping at spots that existed only in his mind. His round face was redder than usual, sweat beading on his bald head despite the air conditioning. Percy recognized the behavior—his son cleaned when he couldn't control anything else.
"You're going to wear through the metal," Percy said.
Larry's hands stilled. "Mom called this morning."
Percy's stomach clenched. Helen never called. Their divorce had been finalized twelve years ago, but the paperwork had just been the funeral for something that died the day Percy came home from the Hollywood Hills and couldn't explain why he'd been crying.
"What did she want?"
"To know if the rumors were true. About someone offering to buy this place." Larry set down his scraper with careful precision. "She said I should convince you to take it. Said it was time to stop punishing myself for your mistakes."
"She always was practical."
"Was it a mistake, Dad?" Larry turned from the grill, and Percy saw the boy he'd been at fifteen, confused and angry when his father liquidated a successful business to buy a rundown diner in the desert. "Whatever happened twelve years ago—was it just a mistake?"
Percy poured himself coffee with hands that wanted to shake. This was the conversation he'd avoided for over a decade, the one that explained why Larry had grown up in exile, why his mother had left, why perfect eggs would never be enough to fix what was broken.
"Sit down," Percy said.
They sat in the booth closest to the kitchen, the one with the best view of the parking lot. Percy needed to see who was coming.
"I was trimming trees at the Blackwood estate in the Hollywood Hills," he began. "Big job—they had these massive oaks that were dying from oak root fungus. Required climbing, careful cuts. Technical work."
Larry listened with the focused attention he usually reserved for temperature readings.
"I was up in the biggest oak, maybe forty feet, working on a diseased branch. Perfect view of the pool area. That's when I saw Harrison Blackwood and his son Richard come out of the pool house with..." Percy's voice caught. "With a girl. Maybe sixteen. Thin. Scared."
"Dad—"
"She'd been trying to escape. Got as far as the pool before they caught her. She was begging in accented English. Russian, maybe Ukrainian. Saying 'please' over and over."
Percy stared into his coffee, seeing again what he'd tried so hard to forget.
"Harrison Blackwood held her under the water while Richard timed it with a stopwatch. Like they were conducting an experiment. Like she was..." He couldn't finish.
"Jesus," Larry breathed.
"I stayed frozen in that tree for three hours after they left. Couldn't move. Couldn't think. Just kept seeing her face under the water, the way she fought at first, then..." Percy shook his head. "I finally climbed down and drove straight to the police."
"You reported it?"
"Filed a complete report. Told them everything. The detective said they'd investigate." Percy laughed, bitter as burned coffee. "Two days later, my biggest clients started canceling contracts. A week later, the bank called in my business loans early. Two weeks later, the detective called to say there was no evidence of any crime."
Larry's face had gone pale beneath the flush. "They covered it up."
"Money doesn't just talk, son. It erases. It rewrites. It makes sure tree surgeons who see too much suddenly can't get work, can't get loans, can't get anyone to believe them."
"So you ran."
"I ran." Percy met his son's eyes. "Sold everything, bought this place because it was far from anything that mattered. Your mother tried to understand, but I couldn't tell her what I'd seen. Couldn't put that weight on her. Couldn't risk them coming after her or you."
"But she left anyway."
"She left because I was already gone. The man she married, the one who built a business, who had ambition and dreams—he died in that tree. What came home was just a ghost who made eggs."
They sat in silence, the weight of twelve years settling between them like desert dust. Outside, heat shimmer rose from the asphalt, making the world look unstable, uncertain.
"Richard Blackwood," Larry said finally. "The man from last night. He was there?"
"Twenty-one years old, holding a stopwatch, learning the family business." Percy's hands tightened on his coffee cup. "Now he's back to finish what his father started. To make sure the only witness to their crime disappears one way or another."
"We could still go to the police—"
"With what? My word against his? Twelve years later? The girl's body was never found. There's no evidence she ever existed except in my memory, and memory doesn't hold up in court against men like Blackwood."
Larry stood abruptly, walked to the window. His shoulders were shaking, and Percy realized his son was crying—not dramatically, just the quiet tears of someone processing a loss they'd never understood until now.
"All these years," Larry said to the window, "I thought you were weak. Thought you gave up because business got hard or the divorce got ugly. But you were trying to protect us."
"I failed at that too. Your mother left. You grew up in this nowhere place. All I managed to do was delay the inevitable."
"No." Larry turned back, and his round face showed a strength Percy had never seen before. "You built this place. Gave people like The Train somewhere to belong. Created something Blackwood has to destroy because its very existence proves he's wrong."
Before Percy could respond, the bell chimed. Molly entered, but something was different. Her usual careful movements were rushed, her composure cracked. She went straight to the back booth and sat facing the door, her hand never leaving her apron pocket.
"Molly?" Percy approached carefully. "What's wrong?"
She looked up, and he saw she'd been crying. Her tough-girl mask had slipped, revealing someone younger, more frightened than he'd imagined.
"I need to make a call," she said. "But I need you to know... what you might hear... it's not who I am now."
Percy nodded, gave her space. But in the quiet diner, voices carried.
"Mama?" Molly's Texas accent was thick now, all pretense abandoned. "It's me... I know, I know I shouldn't call... No, I'm okay... Mama, listen, I need to know—is Wesley really dead?"
Percy busied himself wiping tables, but he heard the sharp intake of breath, the quiet "Oh God" that followed whatever answer she received.
"How?" A pause. "When?... Yesterday? Mama, yesterday?" Her voice broke. "But I just... we just talked last week... No, I can't come home for the funeral... Because they'll be watching... The same people who—Mama, I have to go... Tell Daddy I'm sorry... For everything... I love you too."
She ended the call and sat staring at the phone like it might bite her. Percy approached with coffee and his quietest manner.
"Wesley," she said without looking up. "My ex. We were in business together back in Texas. Rattlesnake venom extraction. Except the venom wasn't really for medical research like we told the universities."
She took a shaking sip of coffee.
"It was for synthesizing compounds. Exotic poisons. The kind that don't show up on standard tox screens. We were young and stupid and thought we were just making good money from people who collected rare things." She laughed, short and bitter. "Turns out we were helping kill people. Politicians in Mexico. Journalists who asked too many questions. Anyone who got in the wrong way."
"You got out?"
"I tried to. When I found out what the venom was really for, I wanted to stop. But Wesley was in too deep. Owed money to the wrong people. So I ran." She finally looked up, eyes red-rimmed but determined. "Been running for three years. Waiting tables, staying invisible, sending money home when I could. And now Wesley's dead. Rattlesnake bite, Mama said. While milking venom."
"Molly—"
"Nobody gets bitten while milking anymore. Not if they're careful. Not if they've been doing it for years." Her hand was steady on the coffee cup, but Percy could see the effort it took. "They killed him as a message. To me. To anyone else who might think about running."
The diner felt smaller suddenly, the walls pressing in. Percy understood now why she'd recognized Blackwood's type so quickly, why she knew how men like him operated. She'd been swimming with similar sharks and barely escaped with her life.
"I'm sorry," Percy said, meaning it. "About Wesley. About all of it."
"He wasn't a bad man. Just weak. Couldn't say no to easy money, couldn't walk away when things got dark." She wiped her eyes with precise movements. "Kind of like this place, isn't it? We all came here thinking we could hide from what we'd done or seen or been. But the past has GPS now. It finds you eventually."
Through the window, Percy could see The Train returning to their trucks. Earl sat in his cab, papers spread on the dashboard—bills, probably, calculating how long he could last without loads. Jake was on his phone, his face grim as he talked to dispatchers who had no work for him. Wally paced beside his truck, anxiety radiating from every nervous gesture. Pond sat on his running board, head in his hands, looking smaller and paler than ever in the harsh afternoon light.
"They're all going to lose their trucks," Percy said. "Without loads, they can't make payments. Without trucks, they're just old men with nowhere to go."
"Unless you take the money."
"Two million, split among them, might buy some time. Get them started somewhere else."
"Percy." Molly's voice was gentle but firm. "Men like Blackwood don't pay two million for real estate. They pay it for silence. You take that money, you're agreeing that what you saw never happened. That girl never existed. That her death was worth exactly two million dollars."
"I know."
"And you'll take it anyway."
It wasn't a question. Percy didn't answer because they both knew the truth. Heroes stood on principle. Heroes fought injustice. Heroes didn't let witnesses be bought.
But Percy wasn't a hero. He was a tired man who made scrambled eggs and had failed to save anyone who mattered. The girl in the pool, his marriage, and now the four men in the parking lot who'd trusted him to keep their sanctuary safe.
The afternoon sun slanted through the windows, painting everything gold. It would have been beautiful if it wasn't a spotlight on their collective defeat.
The bell chimed again. Percy looked up, expecting a customer, but the diner remained empty. Just the bell moving in the wind through the door someone had failed to close properly. Or maybe it was just the ghost of all the mornings The Train had entered together, already starting to haunt a place that wasn't dead yet but had started dying.
"I need to prep for dinner," Larry said from the kitchen doorway. "In case anyone shows up."
They all knew no one would. But Larry needed his rituals, needed to pretend that precision and preparation could hold back the inevitable. So Percy let him go, let him arrange his tools and measure his portions and maintain his standards in a kitchen that would be closed in forty-eight hours.
Molly returned to wiping tables that were already clean. Percy went back to his booth to wait for whatever came next. Outside, The Train sat in their separate trucks, together but alone, like freight cars that had been uncoupled and left on a siding to rust.
The coffee grew cold. The shadows grew long. And somewhere in the Hollywood Hills, Richard Blackwood was probably checking his watch, timing Percy's resistance like his father had timed a young girl's last breaths.
Some lessons, Percy thought, run in families.
Some sins too.


Chapter 4: Saturday Evening - The Mercy of Monsters
The dinner rush was three people: a trucker Percy didn't recognize who ordered coffee and pie, left a twenty for a five-dollar check, and never looked up from his phone; old Cooper, maintaining his vigil with a fresh newspaper that was only two days old; and Martha Gonzalez, who came in around seven, took one look at Percy's face, and ordered a whiskey that they didn't serve.
"Coffee'll have to do," Percy said.
"Then make it strong enough to strip paint." She settled at the counter, her weathered face showing the kind of concern that came from understanding how the world worked. "Heard about the health notice."
"News travels."
"Bad news travels faster." She studied him with eyes that had seen too many good places die. "You taking his money?"
Percy didn't answer, which was answer enough.
"Can't say I blame you." Martha stirred sugar into her coffee with deliberate slowness. "Sometimes the only choice is which way you lose."
By eight o'clock, she was gone, Cooper was pretending to sleep over his newspaper, and Percy was alone with the sound of Larry obsessively cleaning equipment in the kitchen. The boy had been prepping all afternoon for customers who wouldn't come, arranging ingredients for orders that wouldn't be placed, maintaining standards for a restaurant that was already dead.
The bell chimed at 8:17.
Richard Blackwood entered alone, carrying a leather wine carrier and an air of absolute confidence. No bodyguards, no briefcase full of threats. He wore casual clothes—khakis and a polo shirt that probably cost more than Percy made in a week—like a neighbor dropping by for a social visit.
"Mr. Jameson. I hope you don't mind." He lifted the wine carrier slightly. "I brought a peace offering. Château Margaux, 1996. I've been saving it for a special occasion."
Percy watched from behind the counter as Blackwood chose a booth—not the corner where The Train sat, but a middle table, neutral ground. He produced two wine glasses from the carrier, crystal that caught the fluorescent light and transformed it into something almost beautiful.
"I don't drink," Percy said.
"Neither do I, usually. But some conversations require... lubrication." Blackwood opened the wine with practiced ease, the cork sliding out with a soft sigh. "Will you sit? I promise, no threats tonight. Just two men discussing the future."
Percy found himself moving toward the booth, drawn by curiosity or exhaustion or the simple human need to understand the monster across from him. He sat carefully, like the vinyl might be mined.
Blackwood poured two glasses, the wine dark as old blood. "My father loved this vintage. Said it tasted like success."
"Your father murdered children."
"Yes." Blackwood's agreement was calm, matter-of-fact. "He was a monster. Efficient, profitable, but undeniably monstrous." He lifted his glass, studied the wine's color. "I watched him drown that girl, you know. October 15th, 2012. 3:47 PM. She lasted two minutes and thirty-seven seconds."
Percy's hand clenched around his untouched glass.
"You were in the oak tree. Northwestern corner of the property, about forty feet up. Frozen. I saw you when I looked up to check the house windows." Blackwood sipped his wine. "I've always wondered—why didn't you try to stop it?"
"I couldn't move."
"Fear?"
"Shock. Disbelief. Cowardice." Percy's voice was hollow. "Pick one."
"Human nature," Blackwood corrected gently. "The same paralysis that keeps most people from intervening in tragedies. The bystander effect, psychologists call it. You're not unique in your failure, Mr. Jameson. Just uniquely positioned to witness it."
"Is this supposed to make me feel better?"
"It's supposed to make you understand." Blackwood pulled out his phone, swiped through some files. "Let me show you something."
He turned the screen toward Percy. Financial documents. Bank statements. Legal papers. All familiar names.
"Earl Donovan. Truck note past due. Repossession ordered for Monday morning. Jake Hendricks. Medicare supplemental insurance cancelled for non-payment. Needs back surgery that will now cost forty-seven thousand dollars out of pocket. Wallace Morrison. Three arrests for possession being reviewed by the DA. Any one conviction means he loses his CDL permanently. Paul Anderson. Disability review scheduled. Given his ability to work—driving trucks, no less—his benefits will almost certainly be terminated."
Percy stared at the documents, watching his friends' lives dissected into numbers and consequences.
"This isn't about them," he said.
"Everything is about leverage, Mr. Jameson. My father taught me that. Also taught me that crude threats are inefficient. Why break someone's legs when you can break their life with paperwork?"
Blackwood put his phone away, took another sip of wine.
"But here's what my father never understood—cruelty for its own sake is bad business. It creates martyrs, motivates revenge, leaves loose ends. I prefer solutions where everyone benefits."
"How does destroying their lives benefit anyone?"
"It doesn't. Which is why I'm prepared to solve their problems." Blackwood produced a folder from beside him on the booth seat. "Earl's truck—paid off, free and clear. Jake's surgery—covered, best surgeon in Los Angeles. Wallace's record—expunged through a very good lawyer I keep on retainer. Paul's disability—a doctor's report confirming his need for continued support."
Each document was real, Percy could see. Legal. Binding. Salvation typed up in triplicate.
"All this requires is your signature on a real estate transfer and a simple non-disclosure agreement. You sell me the diner, promise to never discuss certain events from 2012, and your friends wake up Monday morning with their problems solved."
"And if I refuse?"
Blackwood's expression didn't change. "Then Monday morning arrives anyway. Earl loses his truck and his livelihood. Jake lives with deteriorating pain until he can't drive at all. Wallace goes to jail for a crime he actually committed. Paul ends up on the street because albinos don't fare well in homeless encampments." He paused. "And you still lose the diner, just through health department violations instead of a clean sale."
The wine sat between them, untouched by Percy, nearly finished by Blackwood. Cooper continued his performance of sleeping over his newspaper. From the kitchen came the sound of Larry scrubbing something that was already clean.
"You know what fascinates me about this place?" Blackwood asked, his tone conversational. "It's exactly what my father would have hated. Inefficient. Unprofitable. Catering to people who contribute nothing to society's bottom line. And yet..."
He gestured around the empty diner.
"It works. It serves a function. These people you've collected—they're broken, but they still move freight. Still contribute. Still exist. My father would have seen them as waste to be eliminated. I see them as assets being poorly managed."
"They're people."
"Yes. People with specific monetary values attached to their problems." Blackwood finished his wine. "Forty thousand for Earl's truck. Forty-seven thousand for Jake's surgery. Fifteen thousand for Wallace's lawyer. Five thousand to maintain Paul's disability. Call it a hundred and seven thousand dollars in humanitarian aid, on top of the two million for your silence."
"My silence about a murder."
"Your silence about an event that can't be proven, involving a victim who doesn't exist in any database, witnessed by a man whose credibility has been thoroughly destroyed." Blackwood's voice remained gentle, almost sympathetic. "I'm offering you the chance to help the living at the cost of abandoning the dead. Some would call that pragmatism."
Percy finally took a sip of the wine. It tasted expensive and bitter and like the death of principles he'd thought were non-negotiable.
"When?" he asked.
"Tomorrow night. Come to my office—the address is in the folder. Sign the papers. Monday morning, the money transfers. Your friends' problems disappear. You disappear to wherever two million dollars takes you. The diner becomes... something else. Everyone wins."
"Everyone except the girls you'll traffic through here."
Blackwood stood, leaving the wine and glasses on the table. "Mr. Jameson, I traffic in real estate and development opportunities. What my tenants do with their properties is their business, as long as the rent arrives on time."
He moved toward the door, then paused.
"That girl in the pool? Anya Volkov. Fifteen years old. From a village outside Donetsk. Her parents sold her for three thousand dollars and a promise that she'd have a better life in America." He turned back, his expression unreadable. "I remember her name because I'm not my father. I don't enjoy the cruelty. But I understand the economics. Three thousand dollars there. Fifty thousand here. The mathematics of human commerce."
"You're still a monster."
"Yes. But I'm a monster offering to save your friends. My father would have simply destroyed them and taken what he wanted." Blackwood smiled sadly. "Evolution, Mr. Jameson. Even monsters evolve."
After he left, Percy sat with the wine that cost more than most people made in a month. Cooper finally stopped pretending to sleep and shuffled out, leaving a five-dollar bill for coffee he'd nursed all evening. Larry emerged from the kitchen, dish towel in hand, his round face showing he'd heard everything.
"Dad?"
"Call The Train," Percy said quietly. "Tell them to come in tomorrow morning. One last breakfast together."
"You're going to take it."
It wasn't a question. Percy looked at his son—perfectionist, anxious, loyal Larry who'd spent three years in exile because of his father's choices—and nodded.
"He wins either way. At least this way, they get something more than destruction."
Larry sat down across from him, in the spot Blackwood had vacated. "What about you? What do you get?"
Percy poured the last of the wine into his still-full glass, watching the liquid catch the light.
"I get to be the coward I've always been. Just a richer one."
They sat together in the empty diner, father and son, surrounded by the ghost of everything they were about to lose. Outside, the desert night pressed against the windows, patient and eternal and utterly indifferent to the small tragedies of the people who tried to scratch out meaning on its surface.
Somewhere, The Train slept in their trucks, not knowing that salvation and damnation were being negotiated over wine that tasted like surrender. Somewhere, Molly mourned a man she'd run from but still loved. Somewhere, Richard Blackwood was probably sleeping peacefully, secure in the knowledge that he'd solved another problem with the precision his father had taught him.
And here, in a diner that served scrambled eggs no matter what you ordered, Percy finally understood that some fights were lost before they began. The only choice was how gracefully you accepted defeat.
Tomorrow would be their last day. Monday would bring new beginnings built on old sins. And Anya Volkov would remain fifteen years old forever, her name spoken once in a diner in the desert before being buried again under two million dollars and the weight of pragmatic choices.
The wine glass caught the light, fracturing it into rainbows that died against the yellowed walls. Beautiful and brief and ultimately meaningless.
Like everything else at Uncle Percy's.
Chapter 5: Sunday Morning - The Last Breakfast
Percy woke at 4 AM, though "woke" implied he'd slept. He'd spent the night in his apartment above the diner, listening to the building settle and creak like old bones accepting their fate. Every sound seemed final—the ice maker's cycle, the refrigerator's hum, the tick of the clock that had counted down fifteen years of mornings just like this one.
Except this wasn't like any other morning.
He descended the back stairs in darkness, not needing light to navigate steps he'd climbed ten thousand times. The kitchen welcomed him with its familiar smells—old grease, disinfectant, the ghost of yesterday's bacon. Larry had left everything perfect, of course. Surfaces gleaming, tools arranged with military precision, ingredients prepped and waiting for meals that might never be served.
Percy started the coffee first. Not the usual industrial-strength brew that could wake the dead, but something gentler. He'd been saving a bag of good Colombian beans for a special occasion. This seemed to qualify.
By 5:30, he'd sent Larry on an errand to Barstow for supplies they didn't need. His son had protested—Sunday was their busiest breakfast day, when families came in after church to judge each other over pancakes. But Percy had insisted, and Larry had seen something in his father's face that stopped further argument.
Percy wanted to cook this breakfast himself. Needed to.
The bell chimed at exactly 6 AM.
The Train entered, but their usual synchronization was off. Earl came first, as always, but he paused in the doorway, looking back as if to make sure the others were still there. Jake followed, moving slower than usual, his hand pressed to his lower back. Wally's smile flickered on and off like a bulb with a loose connection. Pond brought up the rear, huddled deeper into his jacket despite the morning warmth already building.
They arranged themselves in their booth with the careful movements of pallbearers settling a casket. Everything the same as always, but weighted with invisible significance.
"Morning, boys," Percy said, already pouring coffee. His hands were steady. That surprised him.
"Figured you'd be closed," Earl said. "What with tomorrow and all."
"One more day. Seemed important."
They nodded, understanding without explanation. Some things needed proper endings.
"I'm cooking this morning," Percy announced. "Larry's in Barstow. So you get my special touch today."
"Everything scrambled?" Jake asked with the ghost of a smile.
"Everything scrambled. But made with love."
It was a word he'd never used before in this context. Food was fuel, comfort, routine. But love? That seemed too large for eggs and toast. Yet this morning, in this light, with these men, it was the only word that fit.
Percy retreated to the kitchen, grateful for the familiar work. Eggs cracked with more care than necessary. Bacon arranged just so. Toast buttered edge to edge. He found himself taking the kind of care Larry always took, as if precision might somehow preserve this moment.
Through the pass-through window, he could hear The Train's quiet conversation.
"Remember when we first started coming here together?" Jake was saying. "Earl thought we were crazy, four strangers sharing a booth."
"Weren't strangers long," Earl replied. "Hard to stay strangers when you eat breakfast together every morning for five years."
"Four years, seven months," Pond corrected softly. "I keep track."
"Course you do," Wally said, but his teasing was gentle. "Remember when we figured out we were all running the same routes? That first time we convoyed up to Vegas?"
"Saved my ass," Jake admitted. "Breakdown in the middle of nowhere, hundred-degree heat. Would've died if you hadn't been there."
They were telling their origin story, Percy realized. The way people do when something's ending.
He plated their breakfast with unusual attention. Earl's eggs actually attempted to be over easy, though they still ended up scrambled. Jake's bacon achieved something close to extra crispy. Wally got wheat toast—Percy had sent Larry to buy some yesterday. And Pond's fruit cup contained actual fruit salad, not just a sad apple.
"Order up," Percy called, though Molly was already there to collect the plates.
She'd arrived quietly, slipping in the back door with her own key. This morning she'd left her hair down, and it softened her face, made her look younger. Or maybe just sadder.
"Smells good," she said, balancing the plates with practiced ease.
"Trying something new. Caring."
She almost smiled. "Dangerous precedent."
Percy watched through the window as she served The Train. They received their plates with the solemnity of communion. Earl stared at his eggs as if they might contain answers. Jake tested his bacon, nodded approval. Wally held up his wheat toast in silent salute. Pond actually smiled at his fruit salad.
"Percy," Earl called. "Get out here."
Percy emerged from the kitchen, wiping his hands on his apron.
"Sit," Jake commanded, pointing to the spot next to Pond.
"I've got work—"
"Sit," all four said in unison.
Percy sat. Molly appeared with coffee for him, then retreated to give them space. The morning light slanted through the windows, painting everything gold.
"My wife's name was Grace," Earl said suddenly. "Thirty-two years married. She died of ovarian cancer five years ago. Two months after she passed, I was sitting in my truck at a rest stop, trying to figure out reasons to keep breathing. Decided to get breakfast first, think about it on a full stomach. Ended up here."
He took a bite of scrambled eggs.
"Percy served me eggs that weren't what I ordered. Didn't apologize. Didn't explain. Just scrambled. And they were good. Real good. So I came back the next day. And the next. And somewhere along the way, I stopped thinking about reasons not to breathe."
The booth was silent except for the clink of forks on plates.
"Lower left side," Jake said quietly. "That's where the pain lives. Herniated disc pressing on the sciatic nerve. Doc says surgery might help, might not. Fifty-fifty chance I could end up worse. So I drive through it. Every mile hurts, but it's honest pain. Earned. Not like the other kind."
"What other kind?" Wally asked.
"The kind that comes from knowing your ex-wife was right. That you chose the road over her. That you loved movement more than staying still." Jake shifted, pressing his hand to his back. "This place is the only stillness I got left. Six AM to seven-thirty, five days a week. Only time I'm not moving but don't feel guilty about it."
Wally's fingers drummed the table in their nervous rhythm. "I smile because if I don't, people see the real me. The one who can't sleep without checking the locks seventeen times. Who counts his steps. Who takes pills to make his brain stop spinning long enough to drive." He looked at his wheat toast. "You know why this matters? The wheat toast? Because Percy remembered. Nobody remembers what I need. They just see the smile and think I'm fine."
"Paul," Pond said quietly.
They all turned to look at him.
"My name's Paul. Nobody's called me that since eighth grade, but it's still my name." He removed his thick glasses, cleaned them carefully. "These aren't just for sun protection. I'm legally blind without them. Shouldn't have a CDL at all, but I memorized the eye chart fifteen years ago. Every night I pray I wake up blind so I don't have to pretend anymore. But then who'd take care of my mother? She's ninety-three, thinks I'm a college professor. Been telling her that lie so long I almost believe it."
They ate in silence, four men who'd just handed each other pieces of themselves they'd been hoarding like precious stones. Percy felt the weight of what he was about to destroy.
The bell chimed. A woman entered—mid-thirties, professionally dressed, clearly not from around here. She looked nervous, scanning the diner until her eyes found Percy.
"Excuse me, are you Percy? The owner?"
Percy nodded, started to rise, but she waved him back down.
"I'm sorry to interrupt. I just... I needed to come by." She took a breath. "Two years ago, I was in the back of a truck. Being moved from one bad place to another. The driver stopped here, said I could use the bathroom, get some food. I thought it was a trick."
Percy remembered her now. Bruised face, hollow eyes, flinching at every sound.
"You gave me the key to the storage room. Said I could rest as long as I needed. When I woke up, there was food waiting, and a woman—" she looked at Molly "—helped me clean up, gave me clothes that didn't mark me as property."
Molly stood very still, recognition dawning.
"The driver who was supposed to take me to LA just... left. And another driver offered to take me to San Francisco instead. To a shelter that didn't ask questions." The woman's eyes filled with tears. "I have a job now. An apartment. A life. Because this place existed. Because you didn't ask questions, just helped."
She pulled out an envelope. "I've been saving up. It's not much, but I wanted to pay you back—"
"No," Percy said firmly. "Absolutely not."
"Please. I need to—"
"You need to live your life. Be happy. That's payment enough."
The woman nodded, wiping her eyes. "I heard about... about tomorrow. I'm so sorry. Places like this shouldn't disappear."
After she left, the diner felt smaller, as if her story had compressed the air. Percy looked at The Train, at Molly, at the building that had somehow become a lighthouse for people lost in darkness.
"I need to tell you something," Percy said.
They waited, patient as always.
"Blackwood came back last night. He made another offer. Not just for the diner." Percy's throat felt full of sand. "He knows about your troubles. All of them. Earl's truck. Jake's surgery. Wally's record. Pond's—Paul's—disability review."
"Son of a bitch," Earl growled.
"He's offering to fix it all. Pay off the truck. Cover the surgery. Clear the arrests. Guarantee the disability. All of it." Percy made himself meet their eyes. "All I have to do is sign. Take the two million. Never speak about something I saw twelve years ago."
"What did you see?" Molly asked quietly.
So Percy told them. All of it. The tree, the pool, the girl named Anya Volkov who died while he watched, frozen by cowardice. His voice was steady, which surprised him. Maybe confession got easier with practice.
When he finished, the booth was silent.
"I'm signing tonight," Percy said. "I wanted you to know. Wanted you to understand. He wins either way, but this way you get something more than destruction."
"Percy," Earl started.
"No. It's done. I've been running from that moment for twelve years. At least this way, my cowardice helps someone."
"That ain't cowardice," Jake said firmly. "That's love."
"Same thing, sometimes."
They finished their breakfast in silence, each lost in their own thoughts. The morning regulars began filtering in—families heading to church, workers grabbing coffee before shifts. Normal people living normal lives, unaware they were witnessing the last real morning at Uncle Percy's Diner.
"We'll be here tonight," Earl said finally. "When you get back from signing. We'll be here."
Percy nodded, not trusting his voice.
"One more round?" Wally asked, holding up his empty coffee cup.
Percy fetched the pot, made the rounds one last time. Four cups, each prepared exactly right. Small acts of care that had added up to something larger than any of them had expected when they'd first started sharing breakfast.
As he poured, Percy realized this was what he'd really be signing away tonight. Not just a building or silence about a crime. He'd be signing away the possibility that broken places could shelter broken people, that scrambled eggs could be enough, that love could be as simple as remembering someone took wheat toast.
But that was the choice, wasn't it? Save the living by betraying the dead. Help his friends by helping a monster. Love through surrender.
The coffee was perfect this morning. Rich, smooth, with just enough bite to remind you it was real.
Like everything else at Uncle Percy's, imperfect but honest.
One more day of that.
Then nothing but rich silence.
ACT III: The Resolution
Chapter 6: Sunday Night - The Signing
The office building squatted on the edge of Barstow like a mistake someone had tried to correct with beige stucco and tinted windows. Three stories of the kind of anonymous architecture that could have been medical suites or insurance offices or the regional headquarters of companies that existed only on paper. The parking lot was empty except for a black Mercedes and the security lights that turned everything the color of old bones.
Percy sat in his truck for five minutes, engine running, air conditioning fighting the residual heat that rose from the asphalt like the ghost of the day's brutality. The folder Blackwood had left lay on the passenger seat—papers that would transform two lives lost into two million dollars gained. Simple math, if you didn't think about it too hard.
The building's lobby was aggressively normal. Fake plants that fooled no one, abstract art that meant nothing, a directory of businesses with names like "Sunset Holdings" and "Desert Partners LLC." Blackwood's office was listed simply as "R.B. Enterprises, Suite 300."
The elevator played a version of "The Girl from Ipanema" that had been tortured into submission. Percy rode up alone, watching the numbers change, feeling like he was descending despite the upward motion.
Suite 300's door was already open. Blackwood sat behind a desk that was all clean lines and empty surfaces, like an altar to efficiency. The office could have belonged to anyone—no personal photos, no diplomas, nothing that suggested a human being spent time here rather than a very sophisticated machine.
"Mr. Jameson. Punctual. I appreciate that." Blackwood gestured to a chair across from the desk. "Can I offer you anything? Water? Coffee?"
"Let's just do this."
"Of course." Blackwood opened a leather portfolio, revealing documents marked with color-coded tabs. "I've kept it simple. Real estate transfer here, non-disclosure agreement here, payment authorization here. Your attorney can review—"
"I don't have an attorney."
"Then I'll walk you through each document. I want you to understand exactly what you're signing."
And that was the worst part—Blackwood's genuine desire for clarity. No tricks, no fine print, just straightforward language that transformed horror into legalese. Percy signed where indicated, initialed where required, participated in his own moral demolition with the mechanical precision of someone endorsing a check.
"The young woman's name," Percy said as he signed the last page. "Mai. What was her full name?"
Blackwood paused, perhaps surprised by the question. "Mai Nguyen. From a village outside Da Nang. Her family sold her to traffickers for eight hundred American dollars."
"Eight hundred." Percy set down the pen. "She died for eight hundred dollars."
"She died because she tried to escape. The money was just the initial transaction." Blackwood collected the signed documents with practiced efficiency. "If it helps, that particular pipeline was shut down shortly after. My father's sloppiness attracted unwanted attention."
"It doesn't help."
"No, I suppose it wouldn't." Blackwood opened a laptop, typed briefly. "The transfers are initiated. Your personal account will show the deposit by morning. The payments for your friends' situations have already been processed."
"That fast?"
"Money moves at the speed of electricity now, Mr. Jameson. Suffering is analog. Solutions are digital." He closed the laptop. "The diner closes tomorrow as scheduled. You have thirty days to vacate. After that, renovation begins."
"What kind of renovation?"
"Does it matter?"
Percy stood. It was done. Twelve years of running had ended not with capture but with capitulation. He'd sold his silence for the price of four men's temporary salvation and his own permanent damnation.
"One more thing," Blackwood said as Percy reached the door. "The non-disclosure agreement is comprehensive. Any violation, any attempt to speak about what you witnessed, and the payments reverse. Your friends' problems return with interest. Are we clear?"
"Clear."
Percy left the office, rode the tortured elevator down, walked through the aggressively normal lobby into air that still tasted like heated metal. The desert night stretched endless in all directions, but for the first time in twelve years, Percy had nowhere to run.
The drive back to the diner took thirty-seven minutes. He knew because he watched every digit change on the clock, measuring the distance between who he'd been and who he'd become in increments of time that felt both too fast and too slow.
The diner's lights were on, warm against the darkness. Through the windows, he could see The Train in their booth. They'd waited. Of course they'd waited.
Percy parked but didn't get out immediately. Inside, he could see Larry behind the grill, cooking something. Earl was reading a newspaper. Jake held a cup of coffee like it was anchoring him to the world. Wally's fingers drummed their nervous rhythm. Pond had removed his thick glasses, cleaning them with the edge of his shirt.
Normal. Everything looked normal. Except for the way they all turned when he finally entered, the bell chiming its familiar wheeze. Except for the way the silence felt different—not empty but full, like a held breath finally released.
"It's done," Percy said.
Nobody asked what "it" was. They knew.
"Coffee's fresh," Larry said from the kitchen. "And I made eggs. Scrambled for you, over easy for Earl, actually over easy this time."
Percy almost smiled. "You finally got them right?"
"Figured it was time." Larry's voice was steady, but Percy could see the emotion his son was holding back. "Last chance and all."
Percy took his usual spot behind the counter, poured himself coffee from the pot that had been brewing since the morning of the world. The Train sat in their booth, not talking, just being present for this wake they were holding for a place that wasn't dead yet but would be by morning.
Earl's phone buzzed. He looked at it, his massive frame going still.
"Truck payment went through," he said quietly. "Paid in full. Title's clear."
Jake's phone was next. "Email from Cedars-Sinai. Surgery scheduled for next month. Pre-authorized, no copay." His weathered face showed something that might have been hope or might have been grief for the price of his healing.
Wally checked his phone without it buzzing. "Court date cancelled. Case dismissed with prejudice. My lawyer says someone very expensive made it disappear."
Pond didn't have a phone, but Percy knew his disability review would be cancelled by morning, his benefits secured by medical documentation that would appear as if by magic.
"He kept his word," Earl said.
"Men like him always do," Percy replied. "That's what makes them dangerous. They're reliable."
Molly emerged from the back room where she'd been doing inventory no one needed. She moved through the diner touching things—tables, chairs, the coffee machine—like she was saying goodbye. Maybe she was. With the diner closing, she'd need to run again, find another place to hide from the ghosts of rattlesnakes and dead men who'd loved the wrong people.
"I made a pie," she said. "Apple. Seemed appropriate."
"Why?" Wally asked.
"American as apple pie. Corrupted as everything else American." She cut slices with surgical precision. "Besides, Mai would have been twenty-six today."
The math hit Percy like a physical blow. Fourteen years old, twelve years ago. October 15th would have been her birthday.
"How do you know that?"
Molly's smile was sharp as the knife she wielded. "I know a lot of things. Names, dates, details that powerful men think they've buried. That's why I run. That's why I'll keep running."
They ate pie in silence, five men and one woman marking the death of something that had mattered in ways they'd never fully articulated. The pie was good—sweet and tart and honest. Like everything at Uncle Percy's, it was exactly what it claimed to be.
"What will you do?" Jake asked Percy. "With the money?"
"I don't know. Haven't thought past tonight."
"Two million buys a lot of new starts," Earl said.
"Or one expensive ending." Percy set down his fork. "I'm sixty-three years old. I've been running or hiding for twelve years. Maybe it's time to stop."
"What about you?" he asked Larry. "Your share could set you up anywhere. Restaurant of your own. Fresh start."
Larry laughed, short and bitter. "With blood money? Every perfect egg I cooked would taste like that girl's death."
"The money's already bloody," Wally pointed out with his talent for uncomfortable truths. "Not taking it won't bring her back."
"No, but taking it makes us accomplices." Larry's round face was flushed with more than kitchen heat. "He wins because we let him. Because our problems mattered more than justice."
"Justice?" Earl's voice carried the weight of his sixty-seven years. "Son, justice is a luxury for people who can afford lawyers. Rest of us just try to survive."
"Then what's the point?" Larry's voice cracked. "We just give up? Let men like Blackwood turn everything into transactions?"
"The point," Pond said softly, "is that we're here. Together. At the end." He looked around the table with pink eyes that saw more than people expected. "How many people get to say goodbye properly? How many just wake up to find their world already gone?"
Outside, a coyote howled, long and mournful. Others joined it, a chorus of wild grief that the desert translated into music. Inside, six people sat with coffee and pie and the knowledge that by morning, their sanctuary would be just another casualty of progress.
"I want to cook breakfast," Larry announced. "One more time. For everyone."
"It's ten o'clock at night," Percy pointed out.
"So? You always said we serve breakfast all day." Larry stood with sudden energy. "Besides, when has time ever mattered here?"
And so they had breakfast for dinner on the last night of Uncle Percy's Diner. Larry cooked with the precision he'd perfected over three years, each order exactly as requested. Eggs over easy that were actually over easy. Bacon crisp but not burned. Toast golden edge to edge.
Percy watched his son work and felt something that might have been pride if it wasn't so tangled with grief. Larry had become the cook he'd always wanted to be, had achieved the perfection he'd chased so desperately. And tomorrow it would all be inventory in a closed restaurant.
They ate slowly, savoring what they'd taken for granted all those mornings when they'd thought there would always be another. Earl's eggs were flawless. Jake's bacon shattered at the perfect point between crisp and carbon. Wally's wheat toast was a small miracle of gluten and heat. Pond's fruit salad was a work of art.
"You did it," Percy told his son. "You achieved perfection."
"Yeah," Larry said, looking at his empty plate. "And it doesn't mean a damn thing, does it?"
"It means everything," Molly said firmly. "It means you cared enough to get it right. Even at the end. Especially at the end."
The clock above the door ticked toward midnight. Soon it would be Monday, and the health department notice would take effect. Uncle Percy's would officially close, and all of this—the cracked vinyl booths, the coffee-stained walls, the grill that knew the weight of a million eggs—would become archaeology.
"I should go," Molly said, but didn't move.
"Got somewhere to be?" Earl asked.
"Not yet. But by dawn, yeah. Somewhere else. Somewhere new." She looked at Percy. "That's the difference between us. You sold your past. I'm still trying to outrun mine."
"Maybe that's what the money's for," Percy said. "To stop running."
"Men like Blackwood don't pay you to stop running. They pay you to stop talking. The running never ends." She stood, finally, moving toward the door. "Take care of each other. What you had here... it mattered."
She left without looking back, the bell chiming goodbye.
One by one, The Train began to disperse. Jake first, needing to rest his back. Wally next, anxiety medication calling. Pond helped Earl clean up, the two of them working in companionable silence.
Finally, it was just Percy and Larry, father and son, surrounded by the debris of their last meal.
"I'm sorry," Percy said.
"For which part?"
"All of it. Dragging you here. Making you spend three years in exile for my sins. Selling out just when you'd found your rhythm."
Larry was quiet for a long moment, then: "You know what I realized tonight? When I was cooking those perfect eggs?"
"What?"
"That perfect is lonely. Your scrambled eggs brought people together. My perfect eggs just proved I could follow directions." He laughed, short and sad. "Maybe that's what I'll miss most. The excuse to be imperfect."
They cleaned in silence, putting everything in order for a morning that wouldn't come. The grill gleamed. The coffee pots stood ready. The tables waited for customers who would find a locked door and a government notice.
By the time they finished, it was nearly 2 AM. The diner looked better than it had in years, like a body prepared for viewing.
"You want to stay?" Percy asked. "One last night?"
"No," Larry said. "It's already gone. We're just haunting it now."
They locked up together, checking doors and windows out of habit rather than necessity. Who would break into a condemned diner? What was left to steal except memories and the lingering smell of bacon grease?
In the parking lot, they stood by their vehicles, reluctant to complete the leaving.
"What time will you come by tomorrow?" Larry asked. "To start packing?"
"I don't know. Maybe I won't. Maybe I'll just let them throw it all away."
"Dad—"
"Go home, son. Get some sleep. Tomorrow we start figuring out how to live with what we've done."
Larry hugged him then, surprising them both. They weren't a hugging family, hadn't been since Larry was small. But tonight called for gestures that exceeded their usual emotional vocabulary.
"It mattered," Larry said into his father's shoulder. "All of it. Even the scrambled eggs. Especially the scrambled eggs."
After Larry drove away, Percy stood alone in the parking lot, looking at the diner that had been his purgatory and his salvation. The sign flickered, some bulbs burnt out so it read "U CL P RCY'S." Even broken, even partial, it was still true.
He thought about Mai Nguyen, forever fourteen, whose death had driven him here. He thought about all the women who'd passed through, heading toward safety or at least toward hope. He thought about The Train, four broken men who'd found wholeness in shared breakfast. He thought about Larry, who'd learned that perfection was just another kind of prison.
Two million dollars. Blood money, his son had called it.
But wasn't all money bloody when you traced it back far enough? Wasn't survival always a negotiation with monsters, the only choice being which monsters and what terms?
Percy got in his truck, started the engine, drove toward his trailer and whatever came next. Behind him, Uncle Percy's Diner sat empty in the desert night, already becoming memory.
By morning, it would be Monday.
By morning, everything would be different.
By morning, the scrambled eggs would exist only in the hearts of people who'd tasted them and understood that imperfection, served with love, was its own kind of perfection.
The desert swallowed his taillights, patient and eternal and utterly indifferent to the small tragedies of people who'd tried to build something meaningful on its unforgiving surface.
But they'd tried.
For a while, they'd succeeded.
And maybe, in the end, that was enough.
Chapter 7: Monday Morning - The Aftermath
The health department notice looked smaller in daylight. A piece of paper taped to glass, edges already curling in the desert heat. Percy stood in the parking lot at 6 AM, not because he needed to be there but because his body didn't know where else to go. Fifteen years of routine didn't die easy.
The Train's trucks were already there. Of course they were.
Earl sat on his running board, massive frame hunched forward, studying his hands like they belonged to someone else. Jake leaned against his cab, pressing his spine against the metal, eyes closed. Wally paced between the rigs, his nervous energy with nowhere to discharge. Pond stood in Earl's shadow, letting the bigger man shield him from the morning sun.
Nobody spoke. What was there to say?
Cars slowed on Highway 247, drivers rubbernecking at the closed diner like they were passing an accident. Some pulled in, read the notice, drove away. A few took pictures. By noon, Uncle Percy's closure would be Facebook news, tomorrow's chip wrapper, a minor footnote in the desert's long history of failed enterprises.
"Figured you'd be packing," Percy said finally.
"Packing what?" Earl's voice was hollow. "The booth we sat in? The coffee cups that knew our hands?"
"Your truck's paid off. You're free."
"Free." Earl tested the word like suspicious food. "Sixty-seven years old with a clear title and nowhere to deliver. That's not freedom, Percy. That's just well-funded emptiness."
A rental truck pulled up. Two men in matching shirts emerged, began unloading industrial cleaning equipment. They moved with the efficiency of people who gutted dreams for a living.
"That was fast," Jake observed.
"Money moves at the speed of electricity," Percy quoted. "Destruction's even faster."
One of the cleaners approached, clipboard in hand. "Mr. Jameson? We're here for the initial assessment. Mr. Blackwood wants the space ready for renovation by Wednesday."
"It's Monday."
"Yes sir. We work quick."
Percy handed over the keys he'd carried for fifteen years. They felt heavier than they should, like they'd absorbed the weight of every morning they'd unlocked possibility.
The cleaner tested the door, made notes. Through the window, Percy could see the booth where The Train had held court. Larry's pristine kitchen. The counter where Molly had served coffee with watchful eyes. Already it looked like archaeology.
"We'll need you to remove any personal items by end of day," the cleaner said. "Anything left gets disposed of."
"Define personal," Wally said. "Is fifteen years of conversation personal? What about the smell of bacon that's soaked into the walls? You disposing of that too?"
The cleaner, young and just doing his job, looked uncomfortable. "Sir, I just—"
"You just nothing," Earl interrupted, standing to his full height. "You're erasing something that mattered. Least you can do is look us in the eye while you do it."
The cleaner retreated to his truck. The second man began photographing the exterior, documenting what existed before transformation.
"Where's Larry?" Pond asked quietly.
"Home. Said he couldn't watch."
"Smart kid."
They stood in the parking lot like mourners who'd missed the funeral, watching strangers catalog their sanctuary for demolition. The sun climbed higher, and with it the temperature. Soon it would be too hot for standing around feeling sorry for themselves. But not yet.
"My surgery's in three weeks," Jake said suddenly. "Best specialist in California. Ninety percent chance I walk away better than before."
"That's good," Percy said.
"Is it? Fix my back so I can drive loads that don't exist? So I can sit straight in booths I'm not welcome in?"
"There are other diners—"
"No." Jake's voice was firm. "There are other places that serve eggs. But there was only one place that served us."
A Mercedes pulled into the lot. Not black—silver, newer model. Richard Blackwood emerged, dressed down in khakis and a button-down that probably cost more than most people's suits. He surveyed the scene with the satisfaction of someone whose plans were proceeding on schedule.
"Gentlemen. I didn't expect a welcoming committee."
"We're not welcoming," Earl growled.
"No, I suppose not." Blackwood approached with the confidence of someone who'd already won. "I wanted to check on the transition. Ensure everything's proceeding smoothly."
"Smooth as murder," Wally said.
Blackwood's expression didn't change. "I understand this is difficult. Change always is. But I hope in time you'll see this was the best outcome for everyone."
"Everyone?" Pond stepped out of Earl's shadow, his pink eyes fierce behind thick glasses. "What about the women who used our trucks? The ones running from men like you? Where's their best outcome?"
"I'm not responsible for what independent contractors do with their vehicles."
"Independent contractors." Jake laughed, bitter as burnt coffee. "That what we were? Not people who ate breakfast together for five years? Not friends who showed up when someone broke down? Just independent contractors in your supply chain?"
Blackwood checked his phone, already losing interest in their grief. "The funds transferred successfully, I trust? Your medical procedures arranged?"
"Blood money spends the same as any other kind," Earl said. "That what you want to hear?"
"What I want is for you to take advantage of the opportunities you've been given. Move forward. Find new... ventures."
"New ventures." Percy spoke for the first time since Blackwood's arrival. "That's what this is to you? A venture?"
"Everything's a venture, Mr. Jameson. The only variation is profit margin."
Percy studied the younger man's face, searching for some sign of the twenty-one-year-old who'd timed a child's drowning. It was there in the eyes—not cruelty, exactly, but an absence where empathy should live.
"Your father would be proud," Percy said.
For the first time, Blackwood's composure cracked. Just a flicker, there and gone. "My father died alone in a federal prison. I'm nothing like him."
"No," Percy agreed. "He enjoyed it. You just see it as necessary."
Blackwood turned to go, then paused. "The woman—Molly. She left town?"
Percy's stomach clenched. "Why?"
"Loose ends make me nervous. But I suppose she has her own reasons for staying invisible." He smiled, cold as morning frost. "Give her my regards if you see her. Tell her the rattlesnake business is under new management."
He left them with that, driving away in his silver Mercedes while the cleaners continued their inventory of everything that would soon cease to exist.
"He knows about Molly," Pond said. "About her past."
"He knows about all of us," Percy replied. "That's how men like him win. Information is just another weapon."
They watched the cleaners work for another hour, bearing witness to the beginning of erasure. Finally, the heat became unbearable, even for grief. One by one, The Train began to disperse.
"I'm heading north," Jake announced. "After the surgery. Got a cousin in Oregon says it rains there. Might be nice to see rain."
"Phoenix for me," Wally said. "Big city. Easy to disappear. Lots of loads if I can get hired on somewhere."
"What about you?" Percy asked Earl.
The big man stroked his mustache thoughtfully. "Been thinking about that mustache competition circuit. Travel around, teach workshops. Spread the gospel of proper facial hair maintenance."
Despite everything, Percy almost smiled. "That's a plan?"
"That's survival. Find something ridiculous to care about and care about it completely." Earl looked at the diner one last time. "Worked here, didn't it?"
Pond was the last to speak. "I'm staying. Someone needs to remember what was here. Someone needs to tell the story."
They looked at him—pale, fragile Pond who'd found strength in their shared breakfasts.
"What story?" Percy asked.
"About a place where scrambled eggs meant acceptance. Where broken people found wholeness. Where the coffee was bitter as burnt hope but we drank it anyway because it was ours."
The cleaners emerged with boxes of supplies, loading their truck with the tools of transformation. By Wednesday, Uncle Percy's Diner would be gutted. By month's end, something new would rise in its place. Something efficient. Something profitable. Something that had never heard of Mai Nguyen or known that some eggs were better scrambled.
"Will you take the money?" Earl asked Percy. "Your share?"
Percy thought about the two million dollars sitting in his account. Blood money, conscience cash, thirty pieces of silver adjusted for inflation.
"I don't know. Maybe I'll donate it. Maybe I'll burn it. Maybe I'll use it to start another diner somewhere else."
"That's not the same."
"No. Nothing's ever the same twice."
They stood together one last time in the parking lot of Uncle Percy's Diner. Four truckers and a failed tree surgeon, bound by nothing now but memory and the ghost of morning coffee. The Train was uncoupled, each car ready to roll in different directions.
"Same time tomorrow?" Wally asked, and for a moment they all forgot. Then remembrance hit like cold water. There was no tomorrow here. Tomorrow, this would be a construction site.
"Take care of yourselves," Percy said, inadequate words for an inadequate moment.
They exchanged handshakes, phone numbers, promises to keep in touch that everyone knew were lies told to make leaving easier. Then The Train dispersed for the last time, four trucks pulling out in four directions, leaving Percy alone with the remnants of his dream.
He walked to the door one last time, peered through the glass at the interior. The cleaners had already begun their work, stripping away fifteen years of accumulated meaning. The booth where The Train sat was just vinyl and wood. The grill where Larry had pursued perfection was just metal and heat. The counter where Molly had served bitter hope was just formica and memory.
Just. Such a small word for such large destruction.
Percy turned away, walked to his truck, drove toward whatever came next. In his pocket, Blackwood's business card felt heavier than keys ever had. He pulled it out at a stop sign, studied the expensive paper, the embossed letters, the phone number that connected to a man who'd learned to make necessary murders look like good business.
He thought about calling. About asking what happened to girls like Mai after they were murdered. About demanding to know how men like Blackwood slept with so many ghosts in their beds.
Instead, he tore the card into pieces and let them flutter out the window like snow in the desert. They scattered on the wind, meaningless now, just paper returning to dust.
Behind him, Uncle Percy's Diner sat empty in the morning sun, already becoming something else. The sign still read "U CL P RCY'S," but soon that too would be gone, replaced by something that spelled everything correctly and meant nothing at all.
The scrambled eggs were finished.
The bitter hope was swallowed.
The sanctuary was closed.
But somewhere—in Oregon rain, Phoenix traffic, traveling mustache competitions, and the memory of an albino man who'd promised to remember—Uncle Percy's Diner would persist. Not as a place but as a possibility. Not as something that was, but as proof that something like it could be.
Sometimes that's all the victory you get: the knowledge that for a while, in a hard place, during a hard time, something soft existed. Something that accepted imperfection. Something that served breakfast all day to people who needed more than food.
Something that mattered precisely because it wasn't supposed to.
Percy drove toward his trailer, toward Larry, toward whatever shape survival would take now. The desert stretched endless in all directions, patient and brutal and utterly indifferent to human hopes.
But they'd tried.
And in trying, they'd succeeded.
And in succeeding, even temporarily, they'd proven that not everything valuable could be bought, sold, or murdered into silence.
Somewhere, Mai Nguyen was still fourteen years old, forever drowning in a Hollywood Hills pool. But somewhere else, women still ran toward safety in trucks driven by men who asked no questions. Somewhere, scrambled eggs still meant acceptance.
Somewhere, the bitter hope survived.
Just not here.
Not anymore.
