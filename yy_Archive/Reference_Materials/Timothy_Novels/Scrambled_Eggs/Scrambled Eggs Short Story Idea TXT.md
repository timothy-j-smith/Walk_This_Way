"Scrambled Eggs”
Short Story 
Timothy J. Smith
		
	The 2 X 2 X 2 was always the best deal at Uncle Percy's.  Then again there weren't a lot of fancy breakfast items on the one page laminated menu.  Sometimes, if you were lucky, you could order breakfast all day long.  That's if Percy was on the grill, and not his son Larry.  Larry was the better cook, always preparing the eggs exactly to your specification, the toast nicely browned and buttered, and the bacon, ham, or sausage sizzled to perfection, but he was easily flustered by such things as the breakfast-to-lunch menu transition at 11:30 AM.  He would not accept any breakfast orders after 11:20AM, claiming that the longest item to cook, the Eggstavangaza Omelet, took eleven minutes to perfect, and therefore trying to be fair to all customers, he cut breakfast off at 11:20AM.  
	Larry was bald by the age of 27, and his face was always beet red.  He would have perspired while reading the newspaper, although he claimed he never had time for reading.  Percy was the exact opposite, he still cooked on the weekends and a couple nights during the week, and he had a healthy head of speckled grey hair.  All the local customers knew that he would serve them breakfast at any time, although the over-easy eggs might be over-hard, and the over-medium eggs always came out scrambled.  This kind of pissed Larry off, so he tried avoiding the diner whenever the old man was on the grill.  Seeing eggs cooked improperly, or in the afternoon, was way too upsetting.  He never said anything to his father though, knowing that sooner or later he'd be able to do all things his way.

	Larry had a hard time relating with his father even as he got older.  He loved and respected him, but always remembered the uneasiness that lurked while growing up in Malibu Canyon, two miles from the Pacific ocean.  He feared his father.  His father had changed since then, now easy going, but the part of the fear remained attached to Larry's brain.  Larry remembered being up in his room and hearing his father's footsteps approaching, always hoping that the footsteps would soften and lead away, not to his room, not to come in and give a long winded explanation on how Larry had cut the lawn incorrectly--you're supposed to cut with the natural "lay" of the grass, not against it.  Larry had a tough time with affection, when his father tried to comfort him, he would feel uncomfortable, hot, dizzy, unable to think properly, and he would squirm out from underneath his father's arm.  He didn't want to hear any more words of wisdom about growing up, about becoming a man, or about and some of the safety issues to be aware of as he headed off  to college.  Larry studied at the California Culinary Institute because he loved cooking and creating, and because he wanted to have his own business someday, like his father, a successful tree surgeon.  Larry just never thought his father would own the business that he'd be managing.
	Uncle Percy's probably started to manifest itself when Larry first noticed that his parents no longer slept in one bed.  They had purchased two separate single beds, placed side by side, separated by sheets, but connected by one large comforter.  Larry's younger sister, Patricia, had first noticed this, and thinking it peculiar, asked her mother about it.  The mother claimed that with two beds neither of them would "sink" to the middle, and besides their old mattress was a hand me down from Percy's father, who had passed down the tree surgeon business, and it had to be replaced.  Larry's parents were separated at a fairly young age, by mattresses, and sheets, but were connected by a comforter.  What a funny name, Larry thought, a comforter, to comfort, although it didn't seem to be doing them any good in their marriage, nevertheless he liked the feel of it.  Soft to touch like silk, he let his fingertips run through the ribbed ends, it had the puffiness of down feathers, but it was surely synthetic, as his mother had become allergic to all animals byproducts.  That's why the dog, Skipper, had to be put to sleep, besides she was old and suffering from bronchitis.  When their cat, Muffy, was run over, accidentally, by Bull, the worker, of Mexican decent, a new was not bought to take its place.  His mother's allergies had come on late in life, and Larry wondered what she wasn't allergic to.  
	His mother was thirty-six, and not only were these strange allergies kicking up, but she began to suck on her thumb for no apparent reason.  She thought long and hard while she sucked, perhaps regressing into her childhood.  Larry half expected her to take up crawling as a means of transportation, to hell with walking.  
	She had only shown one previous sign of her "illness", as his father, Larry, now described it, and that was during Christmas when she would repeatedly buy each of "the men" a gift pack of Brut shaving lotion, after shave, splash on, deodorant spray, and body spray.  Percy always read out loud the instruction on the back of the bottle, "spray it on your arms, legs, back, and chest for that great smell of Brut that women love" and then he would look at Larry as if they, the men, shared a secret.  The gift was thoughtful, and kind of funny, but year after year she would buy the exact same present.  Larry always wondered if she knew that this was the exact same gift as the years before, or if this was truly a family joke, neither of the men used the damn stuff, so bottles of Brut compounded in the top closet of the men's bathroom.  One time in college, Larry tried using a little bit of the after shave.  The girl, who he was taking out for the first time, remarked that he smelt like her father, and that was the last time that Larry ever used the stuff.  The last thing he wanted was to smell like a father--that was too old of a smell for him.  
	Brut was the type of present that was nice to give, and the recipient would always say, "this will be put to good use", but he wasn't sure if anyone ever used the stuff, or if all men's top bathroom cabinet were filled with it.  The best part was if you left the bottles in their original carton, then you could send it off as a gift to somebody else.  Larry thought of a world of revolving Brut bottles, that never got used, but just traded hands over and over.  He now considered himself a man's man and decided he would never go near such "girlie" cologne type products.  He had his moments of weakness though, breaking down, but he had come to realize that there was more to life than smelling good, impressing women, and sex.  There was money, and there was the feeling of producing something with your own hands, be it nicely prepared food, or a rebuilt engine, that people would pay money for, that gave him his satisfaction.

	There was a pleasant smell of fried dough and cinnamon as you entered Uncle Percy's.   If it wasn't the french toast or pancakes in the morning, it was the warm sweet smell of deli sandwiches on homemade bread  in the afternoon.  The light sand from the desert nicely blended in with pancake and waffle mix that covered the floor.  It took a true connoisseur to be able to tell the difference between the two, and Larry knew the difference--definitely determined by what he declared was "the grit factor".  The higher the grit, the more likely it's true desert sand.  Larry despised grit, and was not so hot about the pancake mix on the floor, but Percy thought that their was nothing wrong with maintaining the current "atmosphere"--that way you don't have to worry so much about keeping the floors so clean and spotless.  The marriage had truly taken a piece of his father, Larry was sure.  This couldn't be the same guy that had him cut the grass again because he had mowed it in the wrong direction.   
	"You want cream?"   The chesty waitress asked one of the men in the corner booth.  The name "Molly" was stitched into her khaki working uniform.  Her nicely tanned arms and legs blended in with her uniform, giving the appearance of a long sleeve dress.  She had only been working at Percy's since the beginning of the summer.  
	Molly's first suggestion to Larry, the official manager, was to change the menus so that they read "Breakfast served: 5AM 'till 11:20AM", not "'till 11:30AM", since most non-local customers that came in during the week didn't understand why they couldn't be served breakfast after 11:20AM.  The customers would always point at the writing on the menu, "Breakfast served: 5AM 'till 11:30AM" as if Molly had never noticed it before.  Larry thought that this idea was pretty stupid.  For one thing, he said, when Percy was cooking breakfast was served practically all day, and another thing, the breakfast menus hadn't changed since 1972 when his father bought the place, and the menus added character--these words made him sound like his father, but he said them anyway.  
	Character doesn't bring me tips, Molly thought to herself, especially with unforgiving breakfast wanting customers.  Larry then mentioned that she should be more concerned about her waitressing, pointing out that she had mixed up a few orders recently.
	Eat my shorts, is all Molly could think at the moment, and before she could walk away Larry was in her face asking her out on a date.  He did so while running his fingers over the top of his head as if he had forgotten that he had no hair to put back into place.  Molly smiled and rightfully declined, she said that she was, "taken".  In truth the last time she was "taken" was four years prior, in Texas, to a rattlesnake hunter.  The venom of the captured rattlesnakes was being used in a research experiment that Texas Tech was doing for the government.  Unfortunately, greed got the best of her beau, Wesley.  He died from an apparent "self inflicted wound". 
	While trying to extract a portion of the venom on his own, to sell to another research lab, the needle slipped and jabbed into his wrist, killing him within six hours.  That's when Molly started her trek north.

	"You'd think they'd know you don't like god damn cream."  Earl stated to no one in particular, although he was making sure that the new girl could hear.  He didn't like her, but he wasn't sure exactly why.  Maybe it was because all the rest of the guys had set their eye on her and had not let up, but he wouldn't fall for it, not this time.  "We spend enough god damn time and money in this place."        
	"Today is a brand spanking new day."  Wally smiled, exposing coffee stained teeth.  He was a part time truck driver, as were the other men at the table, picking up any type of work, or any excess loads that needed to be distributed.  Legal or illegal, it didn't matter.  Wally's best connection worked for Coca Cola, and during the summer, when consumption was up, there was almost always work.  The Coca Cola connection would pay Wally on the sly.  This way both sides win, Wally thought, that's how life should work.  Win-Win.  Wally knew the desert like the back of his hand, just like the rest of his friends.     	
	"Saw The Sinister's dog in the middle of the road this morning."  Pond chirped in.  Pond was an albino.  He preferred wearing a hat, a long sleeve shirt, and long pants even in the heat of the summer.  He had cool pink eyes, the right one slightly crossed.

	"It wasn't totally dead."

	"Yep, every day."  Pond agreed.
	"Ain't no friend of mine."  Earl snarled.  "No way."
	"Who's that?"  Jake asked.  
	"Curly."  Earl stated as if everyone should know.
 	"Curly?"  Pond asked.  He looked at Wally, "Who was talking about Curly?"

	"Doing it?"  Wally asked.
	"Yep, doing it."  Earl answered.  Earl always liked the hard facts.  He liked cutting to the chase.  No story was worth holding back.
	"Always thought he might."  Jake added.
	"Lady Joe caught 'em cold."  Earl sliced his sausage into eight small pieces.  "Bastard had it comin' all right."  
	"With who?"  Wally asked.

	"The Fox?"  Wally smiled, and licked his syrupy fingers.
	"Foxy Lady, Foxy Lady ... "  Pond sung.

	"No more bacon left, you want ham?"  Molly asked, returning innocently to the booth.
	"I didn't order bacon, sweetie."  Jake responded.
	"Okay, who ordered the bacon?"  Molly said out loud, trying to finger for the correct make ticket in her pocket.
	"I ordered bacon."  Earl stated.  Molly looked up.  "But it's soggy."  He picked a piece off his plate and held it up like well cooked spaghetti.
	"I ordered bacon.  Over here."  A red headed man across the diner waved his hand like a politician.  The politician looked at the four men at their table, and then centered his tie as Molly approached.  He decided on the sausages instead.
	"Think that's Sonny."  Pond said.  
	"Seymour's boy?"  Jake asked.
	"His wife died, you know."  Earl added.  "Tuberculosis."

	"Scrambled."  Molly replied

	Jake left the booth and wiped the small piece of egg white from the corner of his mouth. "Poor bastards," he said, "a bunch of poor bastards."
	Percy came out from the kitchen to survey the morning scene.  He gave the impression that life had been kind to him throughout the years.  Calm and cool.  
	Molly scurried about trying to match customer's meals.  Percy liked Molly.  She wasn't the most adept at waitressing, but he that she too had a good story or two within her.  Sometimes, he thought, she comes on to me, but I'm to dumb to acknowledge, and probably too nice now to take advantage.  It was twelve years ago now, that Percy had left his wife because she wanted a divorce, and because she was going insane.  To hell with marriage, to hell with women, Percy had said at the time.  He had been married for twenty-four years when his wife found the young Post Office clerk, with the constant face stubble, had more to bring to her sexual plate than Larry could conjure up in a months time.  Percy wasn't the best at such shenanigans, certainly not as he had gotten older,  preferring a hard day of satisfying work to any experimental sexual activities that his wife of twenty-four years had suddenly found appealing.  He would have been happy to experiment when he was younger, but he had become accustomed to his generic life style.  He wanted to keep things in their place, in order, repetitive patterns of behavior were settling to him.  He was beginning to realize now, that perhaps he had indeed been like his son Larry, the neurotic.  
	There's something about her legs, Percy said to himself, staring at Molly's long browned calves, that did more for his insides than any other women had.  Perhaps this is what his former wife of twenty four years had been feeling restless about.  Molly's legs reminded him of those small cubed caramel candies.  Bronzed to perfection, and nice and smooth and sweet.  He wanted to rub his lips all over them.  Percy liked watching her too, just like most of the men in the diner.  He called the group of men in the booth in front of him, "the train"--always coming into the diner as if they were hooked together, and whenever the leader decided it was time to leave, they would all hook up, and take off together, never one of them staying behind for an extra cup of coffee, or to finish an article in the paper, it was just, "Boom: chug-a-lug-a-lug"--and they were all gone, leaving behind a tip of course.  Little work meant smaller tips, but when work was plentiful, larger tips.  This was understood by all, although never spoken.  Percy could always keep tabs on the guy's lives by the amount of the tip that they left behind.  Kind of like that Kodak developing guy, he thought, who must have a pretty good picture of what some of our lives are like, or at least where we go on vacation.
	"Who's a poor bastard?"  Percy asked.
	The men looked up from the booth.  "Uncle Percy."  They said in unison, greeting Percy.

	"I can't wait till the spring."  Earl swallowed the last forkful of his food.

Percy looked around to make sure that the women in the restaurant were not looking.  "He says, sex, sex, sex."  Percy paused for effect.  "My own son, but I think he was just trying to get back at me for something.  Money is his vice."

	"Kinda like riboflavin."  Pond put in.
	"It seems like such a violating word."  Jake added returning to the booth, thumbs down.    
	"I mean having it ain't so bad, it's just ... "  Percy's voice trailed off.
	"A family ain't worth shit, if you can't keep it together."  Wally declared.
	Ain't that the truth, Percy thought.  There was no way to truly keep a family together, for he had tried hard, but his wife became so overbearing and he had snapped before it was over.  The whole notion of snapping, losing it all, was foreign to him now, and he was surprised that he had.  He was now settled nicely into the restaurant business and a fairly successful owner, who would eventually pass off this business to his son.  That's what families are for, passing off from generation to generation.  His father had left him a tree repair empire in Southern California, covering from San Luis Obispo all the way down past San Pedro.  At one time the account base stretched close to San Diego, but believe it or not that was the least profitable zone.  Trees were a tricky business, his father had to own tree insurance because he had once miss diagnosed a Hollywood star's favorite tree, a gift from the Queen of England, injecting it with nutrients that it simply didn't need, and when the tree passed away he was sued for forestry malpractice.  Nevertheless, the tree business was a profitable one, but one that he had to sell off as he left Malibu, half of the money going toward alimony that he didn't think that he should have to pay, and the other half buying him a new business.  He was now at ease with himself though, cooking eggs, learning on the job, having his son beneath him, teaching him the tricks of the trade, how to run your own business, how to create repeat clientele, like the train that keeps coming back for more, and how to keep everyone happy.  Larry had a lot to learn, he thought.  
	Percy had thought about getting his liquor license for the evenings, having the diner cross as a bar, but the place had become too much of a trucker stop for coffee and a smoke, and the fear of liquor being mixed with driving didn't go over well with Percy, his father dying in an alcohol related automobile accident.  Larry thought that alcohol was a good idea because they would inevitably make more money, but while Percy ultimately ran the show, there would be no alcohol, and Molly would continue to waitress, mistakes and all.

	Larry looked out at the dining room floor that evening.  The early crowd had emptied, and the afternoon crowd was trickling in.  These were the times that he liked the best.  Being by himself, in charge, talking the orders and cooking himself, preparing the meals.    The soup was his favorite, using the recipes to the exact specifications.  The soups were always quite good, only lacking perhaps a little variety.  Sunday's clam chowder tasted a lot like Wednesday's corn chowder, and Saturday's pea soup, not too much different than Tuesday's vegetable.  The broth is what counts, Larry thought, that is where the nutrients from all the ingredients combine.  He liked watching the customers soak the homemade bread into the soup, absorbing the broth like a sponge, and then watching their reaction as bread melted in their mouths.  This was satisfying.
 
	Wally was driving his truck to Las Vegas, transporting a load of Lay's Potato Chips to the South.  The Lay's drivers had been on strike for almost a month and some of the gamblers were fed up with the generic brand of potato and corn chip being supplied.   Wally munched on a nacho cheese flavored chip, and took a sip of his coke.  This was another connection he had made from his younger days on the bowling circuit.  He hooked up his buddies from Uncle Percy's too, who were now completing the other illegal runs.   The take on this job was quite good, because of what was known as the "risk factor" in the underground trucking community.  If the job was life threatening, the pay was first rate.  But Wally didn't think much about the danger, this didn't affect him, he knew that his time would come eventually, but until then there was no point possessing fear.  He had learned this on the bowling circuit, how to react under pressure.  As a kid he was a nationally ranked bowler, hitting the magical number of 300 in practice often, but when it came time for competition he would inevitably bowl in the low 200's.  This happened on numerous occasions, and he could tell exactly when it was happening, his blood vessels constricting, the blood pumping faster, his breaths, shorter and faster, and he would loose his natural rhythm.  He would loose his flow.  Over time he learned how to control these senses, blocking everything out, and did quite well for three years on the tour before he called it quits, his right wrist killing him, and he didn't feel much like having the necessary surgery to repair the tendons that had been torn from a demonstration tournament in Chico, where he was bowling strikes backwards with a blind fold.  It was his only God given talent and now he would make a living picking up work from anyone and everyone that he had met.  Wally had an easy way about him, and it was hard not to give him work.  Now, during his drive, in the night, he was feeling tired, and decided to rest, but his idea of rest was to close one eye, while the other one was awake and doing the navigation.  The sleeping eye unconscious, or semi-conscious, floating off on it's own, like a potato in a large bowl of vegetable soup, acknowledging or challenging the ideas in the air waves, the nonsense thoughts, the other eye alert and aware of the surroundings of the real world, watching the white strip of paint down the centerline of the road, its sole responsibility.  This way he could get to Vegas before sun up, and make it back for an afternoon snack at Percy's.
