# Synopsis and Acts — Walk This Way

**Updated:** June 14, 2026 (Professor O + author — Draft_05 first full draft complete)  
**Supersedes:** Blueprint two-paragraph synopsis (Jan 14, 2026); keep Blueprint on shelf for reference only.

## Logline

On the last night of Boston rock radio, a veteran DJ tells the story of two boys who could have been the best band their town ever heard — and weren't — because every adult around them had already buried the thing they loved most, and called that burial *care*.

## Two-paragraph synopsis (Draft_05)

In the spring of 1979, two seventeen-year-olds from opposite sides of Sudbury, Massachusetts prepare for Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High's inaugural Battle of the Bands — a competition that becomes a proxy war for class, authenticity, and whatever rock and roll still means in a town divided by Hop Brook. Nick Johnson, working-class Aerosmith from his dead father's garage on Dutton Road, and Jackie Marsh, classically trained Queen from his psychiatrist father's house on Concord Road, were once best friends who made magic together: middle school belt buckles, shared headphones, a talent show mashup, a summer at music camp where the only currency was whether you could play. Dan Johnson's death in November 1977 changed everything. By senior year the friendship has thinned to forty feet of cafeteria linoleum and doors hinged at different walls. The buckles that started an argument now mark battle lines. WBCN sponsors the Battle; Maxanne Sartori, twenty-nine, judges what she helped sell. Music stops being a language and becomes a weapon — until, after the tie, they record one eleven-minute jam in Studio C (Saturday, May 19) that proves harmony was never fiction. They do not become a band again. Each chooses the people who already chose him. *The best band that never was* stays literally true.

Thirty years later, on August 11, 2009, Maxanne Sartori — now fifty-nine, carrying three decades of guilt and an unauthorized **cassette dub** she has kept in her purse since May 1979 — sits in WBCN's Studio B for the station's final broadcast before the frequency becomes Christian contemporary. She drew the short straw among reluctant returning DJs: the shift no one wanted, four hours until the dial goes dark. She tells the story of Nick and Jackie — how she witnessed what they were, how she helped turn their friendship into a product, and how she has carried the only portable copy of them playing together: eleven minutes, unheard on air until tonight. Near the end she plays the tape — loud, without permission. Then: *WBCN. The Rock of Boston. Goodnight.* The ON AIR light dies. Silence. At 12:01 AM the frequency becomes Christian contemporary. No viral rescue. No second act for the boys. She drives west with the radio off, imagining — or needing to imagine — Jackie at a piano somewhere, playing D minor once, never having heard the broadcast. The book does not argue its answer; it lets you hear the tape and sit in the silence after.

## The spine (not the plot)

**Surface plot:** Two gifted boys — working-class Nick Johnson and professional-class Jackie Marsh — meet through music, fracture through class and high school, compete in a Battle of the Bands, and fail to become the band they should have been.

**True subject:** *Inheritance is renunciation, not gift.* Dan seals his guitar in a toolbox. Dr. Marsh closes a practice-room Steinway. Eileen turns nine canvases to the wall. Bob Wentworth folds a Jordan Hall program into a drawer. Maxanne trades her ear for a blazer. The boys are not the first to give up what they loved; they are the latest in a chain of frightened people who decided music was a luxury they could not afford — and then handed that decision down as love.

**Thematic hinge (Nick, senior year):** *"It's not giving up. It's choosing the people who already chose us."* That sentence is the book.

**Promise to keep:** *The best band that never was* — must stay **literally true**. The ending earns elegy, not vindication. No viral resurrection.

## Dramatic engine — Greek tragedy (Draft_05)

The book uses **Greek tragedy structure** without the old Blueprint's viral coda.

- **Hamartia:** Maxanne believes she can game the system (Battle, tie clause). Marsh summons discovery then forbids the dream.  
- **Fate vs. choice:** Class, Hop Brook, and institutions **press**; Nick and Jackie **choose** loyalty — *the people who already chose us* — not hatred.  
- **Generational chain:** Inheritance is **renunciation** (toolbox, closed lid, Grace's fall). Boys are latest link, not first.  
- **Catharsis:** 2009 frame plays the tape; meters drop; **silence**. Reader renders verdict — Maxanne does not gloss the brick chapters.

**Not writing:** viral 2009 rediscovery; mashup-as-triumph; enemies-to-end arc; Aerosmith *Walk This Way* on air as sign-off (title = epitaph only).

## Frame

**August 11–12, 2009.** WBCN's final broadcast. Maxanne Sartori, **59**, four hours left. She holds a cassette labeled in two hands — the only complete recording of Nick and Jackie playing together (~11 min). She tells the story backward into 1974–1979 Sudbury. The booth is present tense, first person. The past is third person, **witness chapters**.

## Three acts (album titles)

Each act is named for a classic rock album. The subtitle is the book's **frequency metaphor** — signal, static, last transmission.

---

### Act I — *Toys in the Attic* (The Frequency)

**Chapters 1–15**

**What this act represents:** The moment before the crack becomes a canyon. Act I is **discovery and frequency** — two boys finding each other through Queen and Aerosmith, a town rendered as geography and smell, music as the one language that crosses Hop Brook before anyone admits Hop Brook exists. Adults bury their gifts in toolboxes, attics, and closed fallboards; the boys don't know that yet. They think the war is belt buckles and cafeteria doors.

The act ends when inheritance becomes physical: **Dan dies** (November 1977), the toolbox opens, and Nick becomes the man of the house while Jackie walks parallel hallways. Innocence doesn't explode here — it **thins**, like forty feet of linoleum you step over without looking down until years later it's a canyon. Act I's last note is landslide: what was buried surfaces, and the boys are still in the same town but no longer in the same world.

**Emotional register:** Wonder, comedy, first jam, first fracture. Load-bearing shown scenes. Maxanne's frame sets the clock but must not pre-chew the witness chapters.

---

### Act II — *A Night at the Opera* (The Static)

**Chapters 16–27**

**What this act represents:** **Static** — interference between signal and meaning. The town, the school, the parents, and WBCN all begin to **use** the boys' gift instead of nurturing it. The Battle of the Bands is conceived not as discovery but as **content**: revenue, rivalry, spectacle. Class stops being geography and becomes **machinery** — PTA chairs, diner booths, rubrics, tie-clauses, the machinery that lifts some kids and grinds others down.

Act II is the opera in the title: everyone performing their role — Grace gripping an oak chair in a fryer-smelling uniform, Dr. Marsh counting notes instead of hearing music, Maxanne writing the clause that makes a tie possible, Wentworth blowing one sagging F into an empty room. The boys still play; they play **at** each other. The bridge chapter (Ch 17) is the act's false hope — bodies almost remember friendship before institutions pull them back to their corners.

**Emotional register:** Pressure, comedy turning sharp, institutional guilt. Frame chapters confess; witness chapters show. Ends on the eve of battle — page turned, complicity acknowledged, no innocence left to protect.

---

### Act III — *In Through the Out Door* (The Signal)

**Chapters 28–41**

**What this act represents:** **Last signal** — what gets through when everything else is dying. Patty enters not as prize but as **conscience**: music that isn't a weapon. The Battle plays out hyper-local (Lincoln-Sudbury inaugural) while the larger world (WBCN, rock radio, the discovery era) ends in the 2009 booth above our heads.

Act III moves **in through the out door**: the famous exit is the secret entrance. The garage struggle session (Wed May 16 night) and the Studio C jam (**Sat May 19**, 4 PM) are not triumph — they are **the one unauthorized recording** Maxanne kept on cassette when she sold everything else. The boys don't reform the band. They play once in Studio C, truthfully, for eleven minutes — then choose their people. Nick: Army. Jackie: Juilliard path. Graduation, silence.

The frame closes: **cassette** on air (not the Aerosmith single); meters red then dead; midnight silence; **12:01 JOY FM**; Neighborhoods voicemail as early outside verdict; Jackie coda (**imagined**, does not hear broadcast); Volvo west, **radio off**. Walk This Way the **title** is epitaph, not encore.

**Emotional register:** Grief, restraint, physical detail under stress. Rhythmic hose allowed; thesis sentences forbidden. Ends quiet.

## Loss of innocence — four layers

| Layer | What it is | Strongest in |
| ----- | ---------- | ------------ |
| Personal | Nick ↔ Jackie friendship | Ch 5–7, 12–13, 17, Battle |
| Class / town | Hop Brook, four quarters, labor | Ch 4, 14, 21, 23 |
| Institutional | WBCN, Battle as product, Maxanne's clause | Ch 22, 27, frame |
| Cultural | Rock vs. disco, radio dying, "authenticity" | Frame (light touch); let objects carry |

**Fragile layer:** Institutional/cultural — survives when shown (Kowalski's soda-machine line, Globe obituary) and dies when Maxanne glosses meaning.

## What we are not writing

- Clean Battle winner or talent-show triumph lap as soul of book  
- 1976 conspiracy Battle; Kowalski tie-clause as main engine (if used: one boring sponsor line)  
- Viral 2009 rediscovery  
- Patty as romantic prize or deus ex machina  
- Parent biography chapters — parents live in **objects and one withheld fact each**
