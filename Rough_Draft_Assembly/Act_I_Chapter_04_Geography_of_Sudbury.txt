## CHAPTER 4: THE GEOGRAPHY OF SUDBURY

"Now," I continue on air, checking the clock—three hours and twenty-seven minutes left of WBCN—"let me tell you about the battlefield."

I fade up James Taylor's "Sweet Baby James"—not rock and roll, I know, but James understood Massachusetts in a way that mattered. He understood how geography could divide more than land.

"Sudbury, Massachusetts," I tell my dwindling late-night audience, "wasn't just a town in 1979. It was a perfect microcosm of everything America was becoming—a place where the same water ran through different worlds, where twelve minutes could take you from one version of the American dream to another."

The Hop Brook ran through Sudbury like God had drawn a line with his finger, saying 'This side for the comfortable, that side for the strivers.' But water doesn't respect property values. Fish don't check bank accounts before taking bait. And in 1979, kids from both sides of that brook would stand knee-deep in the same water, casting for the same bass, pretending for a few hours that the border didn't exist.

"But the real border," I continue, lighting another cigarette because some stories require continuous combustion, "wasn't the water. It was what happened in the woods on either side."

The conservation land between the two Sudburys should have been neutral territory—hundred acres of pine and oak, old growth that remembered when all of Massachusetts was forest. But by 1979, those woods bore scars that told the real story.

On Nick's side, dirt bike trails wound through the trees like varicose veins. The mini-bikes and dirt bikes had carved paths so deep that grass would never grow there again. Every weekend, the air filled with two-stroke exhaust and the angry hornet sound of engines, kids in hand-me-down helmets finding freedom in speed and mud.

On Jackie's side, those same trails were seen as wounds. The parents in those colonial houses wrote letters to the town council about noise ordinances and environmental damage. They put up "No Motorized Vehicles" signs that were routinely torn down. They talked about property values the way other people talked about virtue.

"Here's what you need to understand," I tell my dwindling audience, "those mini-bike trails weren't just about recreation. They were about different definitions of freedom."

For kids like Nick Johnson, those bikes meant mobility that didn't depend on parents who worked double shifts. They meant access to parts of town that public transportation didn't reach. They meant the kind of dangerous joy that rich kids could buy with skiing trips to Vermont but poor kids had to find in their own backyards.

For kids like Jackie Marsh, the woods were supposed to be pristine, a buffer between their careful world and the chaos beyond. The bikes were intrusions, reminders that borders could be crossed, that nowhere was really safe from the sound of people who didn't know their place.

"The four quadrants," I continue, organizing my next sequence of albums with obsessive attention to detail, "were Sudbury's way of organizing the chaos of class into something manageable, something that could be discussed at town meetings without actually addressing the underlying inequality."

The northwest quadrant—the Rampers—was where the money lived. Colonial houses with perfect lawns, kids who went to private schools or got into Harvard, families who summered on the Cape and wintered in Florida. This was Jackie Marsh's world, where classical music lessons were as essential as orthodontics.

The northeast quadrant—the Rats—was where the working class lived. Modest houses with well-maintained yards, kids who worked part-time jobs, families who counted pennies between paychecks. This was Nick Johnson's world, where garage bands were born and dreams were deferred but not forgotten.

The southwest quadrant—the Regs—was where the professors and professionals lived. Academic families who valued education but not necessarily wealth, kids who were expected to excel but not necessarily to inherit. This was the middle ground, the buffer zone between the extremes.

The southeast quadrant—the Rollers—was where the rural and mechanical lived. Families who worked with their hands, kids who understood engines and tools, people who found freedom in movement and noise. This was where the dirt bike trails began, where the working class found their voice.

"But here's the thing about quadrants," I tell my audience, "they're not just geographic divisions. They're social divisions. They're economic divisions. They're divisions of expectation and opportunity."

When Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh met in that middle school cafeteria in 1974, they were crossing more than a room. They were crossing the invisible lines that Sudbury had drawn to keep its children properly separated, to ensure that the right kids ended up in the right places, to maintain the order that made the comfortable comfortable and the strivers striving.

"The Battle of the Bands," I continue, "wasn't just a music competition. It was a referendum on whether those lines could be crossed, whether the frequency that connected two thirteen-year-old boys could survive the static of adult expectations and economic reality."

I check the clock: three hours and twelve minutes left.

"Coming up," I tell the darkness beyond the booth, "the moment when those invisible lines became visible, when two boys discovered that friendship could bridge any gap if both voices were willing to listen rather than simply perform."
