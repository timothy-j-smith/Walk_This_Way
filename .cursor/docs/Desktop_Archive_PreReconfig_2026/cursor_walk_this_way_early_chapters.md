I'm going to tell you about Sudbury's geography, but not the kind you'd find in a Chamber of Commerce brochure. This is the geography that mattered in March 1979—the invisible lines that determined which kids could afford guitars and which could afford guitar lessons, which kids practiced in garages and which kids performed in living rooms with rented Steinways.

At the Dump, postal codes didn't matter. Everyone's garbage stank equally. Everyone's children learned the same lesson about American consumption: we create more waste than we can hide, but burial is always an option.
The kids would sit in the cars while their fathers—always their fathers, this was 1979—would back up to the edge of the pit where bulldozers waited like mechanical vultures. Glass in one area, paper in another, everything else into the pit that would be covered with six inches of dirt and forgotten until the EPA decided forgetting wasn't an option anymore.
I take a sip of bourbon, feeling the warmth that helps me remember details that might otherwise stay buried under forty-five years of accumulated experience.

The trails they carved would outlast the bikes, outlast the kids, outlast everything except the earth's memory of where children once believed they could go anywhere.
These were the boundaries that mattered in March 1979, when two boys who lived less than a mile apart discovered that distance isn't measured in feet but in expectations, opportunities, and the particular American talent for maintaining divisions while preaching unity.
The town center sat like a colonial film set, complete with white churches, a green where sheep once grazed, and the stocks where colonial justice once embarrassed sin into submission. By 1979, the stocks were gone but the impulse remained—just more subtle, more efficient at keeping people in their proper places.
This was the geography that would shape a Battle of the Bands, that would turn a music competition into a class war, that would prove—once again—that in America, even harmony has to fight for its right to exist.
And somewhere in this divided landscape, three kids who'd once made music together were about to remember what they'd forgotten, or forget what they'd remembered, depending on which side of which line they'd learned to call home.
