The lake water was still cold in early July, even with the sun beating down on the dock. Nick sat with his legs dangling over the edge, his guitar case propped beside him like a second person. The wood planks were warm under his palms, rough with splinters that caught on his calluses. He'd been practicing the opening riff to "Walk This Way" all morning, and his fingers ached in that good way that meant he was getting it right.

"Hey."

Nick turned. Jackie stood at the edge of the dock, his own case—black, leather, expensive-looking—held carefully in both hands. He'd changed out of his camp t-shirt into a button-down, which was stupid because they were at music camp, not a recital. But that was Jackie. Always prepared, always proper.

"Hey," Nick said. He scooted over to make room, and Jackie sat down, setting his case between them like a barrier.

They'd been at Camp Winnipesaukee for three days. Three days since winning the middle school talent show. Three days since their mashup of "Walk This Way" and "Bohemian Rhapsody" had made the whole auditorium stand up and cheer. Three days since the principal had handed them the envelope with the camp scholarship, and Nick's mom had cried, and Jackie's dad had nodded like it was expected.

The camp was different from Sudbury. Different from middle school. Here, nobody knew which side of Hop Brook you came from. Here, the only thing that mattered was whether you could play.

"Did you see the schedule for today?" Jackie asked. He didn't look at Nick. He was watching the water, where a motorboat was cutting a white line across the lake.

"Yeah. Master class at two."

"Master class." Jackie said it like it was a test he was already failing. "I've never played in front of real musicians before."

"You played in front of the whole school."

"That was different." Jackie's fingers tapped against his case. "That was us. This is... this is real."

Nick understood. The camp instructors were actual working musicians. Some had played with bands Nick had only heard on WBCN. Some had records. Real records, in stores. Not demos in their garages.

A voice called from the shore. "Are you two going to sit there all day, or are you going to come help set up?"

They both turned. A girl stood on the beach, one hand shading her eyes, the other holding a stack of sheet music. She wore cut-off jeans and a t-shirt with the sleeves cut off, and her hair was pulled back in a ponytail that swung when she moved.

Nick recognized her. Not from camp—from school. From the halls at Curtis Middle School, where she'd been just another face. Just another girl who didn't matter because girls didn't matter yet.

But here, in the sunlight, with the lake behind her and music in her hands, she mattered.

"That's Patty Lally," Jackie said quietly. "From Sudbury. She's in our grade."

"I know." Nick stood up, brushing splinters from his palms. "I've seen her around."

They walked down the dock together, their cases bumping against their legs. Patty was already organizing music stands when they reached the beach. She looked up and smiled, and Nick felt something shift in his chest. Not butterflies—that was stupid. But something. A recognition.

"You're Nick and Jackie," she said. "From the talent show. That was incredible."

"Thanks," Nick said. Jackie said nothing, just nodded.

Patty set down the music. "I'm Patty. I've seen you both at school, but we've never really talked. Funny how that works, right? You can see someone every day for years and never actually see them."

Nick felt his face get hot. He'd seen her. He'd noticed her. But noticing didn't count. Noticing was what you did when you were bored in math class, when you were waiting for the bell. Noticing wasn't the same as seeing.

"Are you here for the master class too?" Jackie asked. His voice was careful, measured. The way he talked when he was nervous.

"I am. Violin." Patty picked up her case—smaller than theirs, shaped differently. "But I also play piano. And guitar. A little bit of everything, really."

"Everything?" Nick said.

"Music is music." She shrugged. "The instrument's just the tool. The music's what matters."

Jackie's shoulders relaxed. "That's exactly what I think. The structure, the theory—that's just the foundation. The emotion, that's what makes it real."

"Emotion?" Nick felt his jaw tighten. "You mean like Aerosmith? Raw, honest, no bullshit?"

"I mean like Queen," Jackie said. "Complex, layered, technically perfect but still emotional."

Patty laughed. "Why does it have to be one or the other? Can't it be both?"

They both looked at her. She was still smiling, but there was something in her eyes. A challenge. Like she was testing them.

"Queen's better," Jackie said. "Objectively. The composition, the arrangements—"

"Objectively?" Nick cut in. "There's nothing objective about music. Aerosmith hits you in the gut. Queen hits you in the head. Which one matters more?"

"The head," Jackie said.

"The gut," Nick said.

They stared at each other. Not angry—not yet. But something was building. Something that hadn't been there before.

Patty watched them both. "You know what's funny? You two just won a talent show by combining both. You took Aerosmith and Queen and made something new. Something that was both. And now you're arguing about which one is better?"

She was right. They had. They'd taken "Walk This Way" and "Bohemian Rhapsody" and woven them together until you couldn't tell where one ended and the other began. Until it was just music. Just them.

But that was before. Before camp. Before Patty.

"Come on," Patty said. "Help me set up. The master class starts in an hour, and if we're late, we'll get stuck in the back where we can't hear anything."

They helped her. Nick carried music stands. Jackie organized the sheet music. Patty directed them, and they both listened. They both watched. They both noticed the way she moved, the way she talked, the way she made everything seem easy.

And they both noticed that the other was noticing.

---

The master class was held in the main hall, a converted barn with high ceilings and wooden beams. The instructors sat at a table in front, real musicians with real experience. One had played with J. Geils Band. Another had done session work for Aerosmith. A third had studied at Berklee.

Nick's palms were sweating. He wiped them on his jeans and tried to breathe.

"Relax," Jackie whispered. "You've got this."

Nick looked at him. Jackie's face was pale, but his hands were steady. Always steady. That was Jackie. Even when he was terrified, he looked calm.

"Thanks," Nick said. "You too."

They were called up together. Of course. They were always together. Nick and Jackie. The talent show winners. The duo.

They set up their instruments. Nick's guitar was secondhand, scuffed, with a strap his dad had made from an old belt. Jackie's keyboard was rented, expensive, with pedals and settings Nick didn't understand.

"Play something," the instructor said. "Whatever you want. Show us what you've got."

Nick looked at Jackie. Jackie nodded. They'd practiced this. They knew what to play.

But as Nick's fingers found the opening notes of "Walk This Way," he saw Patty in the audience. She was watching. Really watching. Not like the other kids, who were just waiting their turn. She was listening. She was seeing.

And Nick wanted her to see him. Really see him. Not just the music, but him.

So he played harder. Louder. He let the raw energy of Aerosmith fill the room, let it hit like a punch. Let it be honest, brutal, real.

Jackie came in on the keyboard, and Nick felt the shift. Jackie was playing Queen. Not the mashup they'd practiced. Just Queen. Complex, layered, technically perfect.

They weren't together anymore. They were competing.

The instructors were nodding. They were impressed. But Nick could feel it—the fracture. The moment when they stopped being one thing and became two.

When they finished, the room applauded. But Nick and Jackie didn't look at each other. They looked at Patty.

And Patty was watching them both.

---

That night, they sat around a campfire with the other campers. The stars were out, bright and clear away from the city lights. Someone had brought a guitar, and they were passing it around, taking turns playing songs.

Patty sat between Nick and Jackie. Not literally—there were other kids there, a whole circle. But in the way that mattered, she was between them.

"Tell me about high school," she said. "Lincoln-Sudbury. What's it like?"

Nick shrugged. "We don't know yet. We're going in the fall."

"But you've heard things," Jackie said. "Everyone's heard things."

Patty nodded. "The groups. Rampers, Rats, Regs, Rollers. Like you have to pick one."

"You do," a kid named Mark said. He was a year older, already in high school. "You pick, or you get picked for. There's no neutral ground."

"What if you don't fit?" Nick asked.

"Then you don't fit." Mark strummed the guitar. "Simple as that."

Patty was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "What if you fit in more than one? What if you're a little bit of everything?"

"Doesn't work that way," Mark said. "You pick a side. You stick with it."

Nick looked at Jackie. Jackie was staring into the fire, his face lit orange and gold. He looked older suddenly. Like he was already thinking about it. Already choosing.

"What about us?" Nick said. "Me and Jackie. We're a team. We won the talent show together. We're here together."

Mark laughed. "That was middle school. High school's different. You'll see."

The fire crackled. Someone played a few chords. A Queen song. "Somebody to Love."

Jackie's head came up. "That's Queen."

"I know," the kid playing said. "You got a problem with Queen?"

"No," Jackie said. "Queen's perfect. Technically perfect."

"Technically perfect?" Nick felt something hot in his chest. "What does that even mean? Music's not a math problem. It's feeling. It's—"

"It's both," Patty said. Her voice was quiet, but it cut through the argument. "It's always both. Feeling and technique. Gut and head. Aerosmith and Queen."

She looked at Nick. Then at Jackie. "You two proved that. At the talent show. You showed everyone that it could be both. Why are you trying to make it one or the other now?"

Nick opened his mouth. Closed it. She was right. Again. Always right.

"Because," Jackie said slowly, "maybe it can't be both. Maybe you have to choose. Maybe that's what growing up means. Choosing."

The fire popped. Sparks flew up into the dark.

"Maybe," Patty said. "Or maybe choosing is the mistake. Maybe the magic is in not choosing. In being both."

She stood up. "I'm going to bed. Master class again tomorrow. You two should get some sleep too."

She walked away, and Nick and Jackie both watched her go. Both of them. At the same time. Both of them wanting the same thing.

And both of them knowing, without saying it, that they couldn't both have it.

---

The last day of camp, they had a final performance. All the campers played for each other, for the instructors, for anyone who wanted to listen.

Nick and Jackie were supposed to play together. They'd planned it. They'd practiced it. The mashup. The thing that had won them the talent show.

But when Nick took the stage, he saw Patty in the front row. And he saw Jackie see her too.

And something broke.

Nick played Aerosmith. Just Aerosmith. Raw, loud, honest. No Queen. No mashup. Just him. Just his music. Just what he felt.

Jackie played Queen. Just Queen. Complex, layered, perfect. No Aerosmith. No mashup. Just him. Just his music. Just what he knew.

They weren't together anymore.

When they finished, the applause was polite. Not like the talent show. Not like when they were one thing.

After, Patty found them both. Separately, but they both knew she'd talked to the other.

"You know what's funny?" she said to Nick. "I've been the same person since grade school. Same friends. Same family. Same music. But nobody noticed me before. Not really. And now you both do. And Jackie does. And it's... it's weird. But also kind of nice."

"Nice?" Nick said.

"Cute," Patty said. "The way you're both trying so hard. The way you're both competing. It's cute."

Nick felt his face get hot. "We're not competing."

"Aren't you?" Patty smiled, but it wasn't mean. It was sad. "You know what I wonder? What happens when we go back? Back to Sudbury. Back to high school. Back to Rampers and Rats and Regs and Rollers. Do you think we'll still see each other? Or do you think we'll fall back into our patterns? Our routines?"

"I don't know," Nick said.

"Me either." Patty looked at him. Really looked. "But I hope we do. I hope we remember this. This moment. When we were just us. Just music. Just kids at camp."

She walked away. Nick watched her go. And he knew, without turning around, that Jackie was watching too.

---

On the bus ride home, Nick and Jackie sat together. They always sat together. But this time, it was different.

They didn't talk about music. They didn't talk about camp. They didn't talk about Patty.

They just sat. Side by side. Best friends. But something had shifted. Something had broken.

And neither of them knew how to fix it.

Neither of them knew if they wanted to.

The bus rolled through the Massachusetts countryside, past fields and farms and small towns. Past the life they'd left behind for a week. Past the life they were heading back to.

High school. Rampers, Rats, Regs, Rollers. Choices. Sides.

Nick looked out the window. Jackie looked at his hands.

And between them, in the space that had always been filled with music and laughter and easy friendship, there was silence.

Just silence.
