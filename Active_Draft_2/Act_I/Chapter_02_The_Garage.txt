CHAPTER 2: THE GARAGE

Winter 1974-75


Jackie Marsh had never been to the other side of Hop Brook on purpose.

He'd crossed it in a car plenty of times -- his father driving the Lincoln Continental on Route 20 toward Boston, the brook flashing under the bridge in a brown streak that lasted half a second. But walking across was different. Walking across meant you were going somewhere on the other side, and going somewhere on the other side meant admitting the other side existed in a way that driving over it didn't.

It was the first Saturday of December, three weeks after Thanksgiving, and the air was the kind of cold that New England produced in early winter -- not the deep freeze of January, but the raw, wet cold that got into your bones through your coat, through your sweater, through your skin, and sat there like a boarder who wasn't paying rent. Jackie's mother had tried to make him wear a scarf. He'd stuffed it in his coat pocket the second he got to the end of Concord Road.

Nick had drawn him a map on notebook paper during study hall. Turn left at the Stop & Shop. Follow Route 20 past the gas station. Cross Hop Brook at the footbridge -- not the road bridge, the footbridge, the one behind the package store where the high school kids bought beer with fake IDs. Then right on Dutton Road, third house on the left, the one with the garage.

"You can't miss the garage," Nick had said. "It's the one making noise."

Jackie found the footbridge. It was a plank bridge, six boards wide, no railing, spanning maybe fifteen feet of slow-moving water that was brown with tannin and cold enough to numb your feet if you fell in. The boards were slick with frost. He crossed it carefully, his guitar case banging against his thigh with each step.

On the other side, Dutton Road looked different from Concord Road the way a photograph looks different from a painting. Concord Road had houses with names -- the Whitmore Estate, the old Parker place -- and mailboxes that matched the shutters. Dutton Road had houses with numbers and mailboxes that were whatever color they'd been when someone put them up. Number 14 had a flag that didn't go all the way up. Number 18 had a chain-link fence with a dog behind it that barked at everything. Number 22 -- the Johnson house -- had a driveway with a crack running down the middle where a dandelion had pushed through in the summer and died in the fall and nobody had swept it away.

The house was small. Cape Cod style, which in Sudbury meant the builder ran out of money before he got to the second floor. The paint was yellow but fading, the kind of yellow that used to be cheerful and was now just tired. The front steps had a lean to them. The gutters held a season's worth of leaves.

But from the detached garage behind the house, Jackie could hear it.

A guitar. Electric. Playing the opening riff of "Sweet Emotion" -- that low, slinky bass line that Joe Perry made sound like it was sneaking up on you, followed by the rhythm guitar chopping chords like it was angry about something it couldn't name. The sound was distorted and loud and came out of the garage the way heat comes out of an oven when you open the door -- a wall of it that hits you all at once.

Jackie walked up the driveway. The crack in the concrete caught his shoe. He steadied himself and kept going.

The garage door was open about two feet from the ground, enough to let the sound escape but not enough to let the cold in. Jackie crouched down and looked under it. Inside: a single bare bulb hanging from the ceiling on a wire. A concrete floor with oil stains shaped like continents. A workbench along the back wall with tools arranged on a pegboard -- hammers, wrenches, screwdrivers, pliers, each one hanging on its own hook, each hook labeled in handwriting that was small and precise.

And Nick, sitting on an overturned milk crate, bent over a guitar that looked like it had been through a war. The body was scratched. The strap was held together with a safety pin. A strip of electrical tape ran along the neck where something had cracked and been patched rather than replaced.

Nick looked up. "You found it."

"You said I couldn't miss it."

"You bring your guitar?"

Jackie held up the case. It was black, hard-sided, latched, with his initials stamped on the handle in gold. His father had bought it at a music shop on Boylston Street in Boston, the kind of place where the guitars hung on the wall behind glass and the salespeople wore ties.

Nick looked at the case. Then he looked at his own guitar, with its electrical tape and its scratched body and its safety-pin strap. He didn't say anything about the difference. He just kicked another milk crate into position across from his own and said, "Sit down."

Jackie unlatched the case. Inside, his acoustic guitar lay in red velvet lining, the wood polished to a mirror finish, the strings new and bright. He lifted it out and sat on the milk crate. The crate wobbled on the uneven concrete. He adjusted his weight.

"Okay," Nick said. "You know 'Sweet Emotion'?"

"I know the chords. I don't know the riff."

"I'll play the riff. You play the chords. Try to keep up."

Nick started. The riff came out of his beaten-up guitar with a tone that was dirty and warm, the low notes buzzing on the third fret where the action was too high and the strings sat wrong. Any guitar teacher would have told him to fix the setup, adjust the truss rod, raise the nut. Nick just played through it. The buzz became part of the sound.

Jackie found the chord progression. His guitar sounded clean and bright where Nick's was dark and gritty, and for the first few bars they sounded like two different songs happening at the same time in the same room. Jackie's fingers found the transitions -- Am to G to F -- but his rhythm was off, a half beat behind, his classical training making him precise where the song needed him to be loose.

"Stop thinking about it," Nick said. He didn't stop playing. "You're playing the notes right but you're playing them like homework. Just -- feel where the beat goes and go there."

Jackie tried. It was like someone telling you to stop thinking about breathing. The more he tried not to think, the more he thought. His fingers kept landing on the right notes at the wrong time, too careful, too correct.

Nick stopped playing. "You know what your problem is?"

"My timing's off."

"Your timing's perfect. That's the problem. You're playing exactly on the beat. Aerosmith doesn't play on the beat. They play around it. Behind it, sometimes. Ahead of it sometimes. That's what makes it feel like something." Nick strummed a chord, letting it ring sloppy and long. "Like that. It's not wrong. It's just different from right."

Jackie thought about this. At his piano lessons, different from right was called a mistake. You got corrected. You practiced until the mistake went away. The idea that imperfection could be a choice, not an error, was so foreign to his training that it felt like hearing a new language for the first time.

He tried again. This time he relaxed his wrist, let his hand fall on the strings a fraction late, didn't fight the delay. The sound that came out wasn't clean anymore. It was rough at the edges, a little muddy, and it locked into Nick's riff like a puzzle piece that had been sanded down to fit.

Nick grinned. "There it is."

They played through "Sweet Emotion" twice. The second time was better than the first. By the third pass, Jackie wasn't thinking about the chords at all -- his fingers had memorized the pattern, and his mind was free to listen to what Nick was doing and respond to it, a conversation happening in sound instead of words.

"Now play me something," Nick said. "Play me the Queen thing."

Jackie shifted on the milk crate. He rested his guitar on his thigh and began picking out the arpeggiated introduction to "Bohemian Rhapsody" -- the piano part, translated for acoustic guitar, the notes falling like drops of water from a faucet that wouldn't stop dripping. It sounded thin without the piano's resonance, but the melody was there, clear and strange and beautiful in the cold garage.

Nick listened. His head tilted the way it did when he was thinking hard about something. When Jackie reached the section where the song shifts from ballad to opera, Nick picked up his guitar and found a low chord that sat underneath Jackie's melody like a floor underneath a dancer. He didn't know the part. He was making it up, feeling his way under Jackie's notes, finding a harmony by instinct that a classically trained musician would have needed sheet music to calculate.

Something happened.

The wrench on the pegboard rattled.

It was the nine-sixteenths, hanging on its hook next to the socket set, and when Nick hit the low E chord it vibrated against the metal hook and produced a buzzing rattle that cut through the guitar sound like a snare drum hit. Jackie stopped playing. Nick stopped playing. They both looked at the pegboard.

"Did that just --"

"Yeah." Nick was grinning. "That happens. My dad figured out it's 82 hertz. Low E. When you play it loud enough, the wrench vibrates."

"82 hertz?"

"The wrench is exactly the right weight and length to resonate at 82 hertz. It's physics. My dad showed me -- you can make different tools vibrate at different frequencies. The vise grip does it at A. The claw hammer is somewhere around D. It's like the whole garage is an instrument."

Jackie looked at the pegboard. Hammers, wrenches, screwdrivers, pliers. All hanging in order, all labeled in that careful handwriting. "Your dad knows about frequencies?"

"He reads. Everything. Mechanics magazines, science stuff, music theory -- he's got a whole shelf in the house. He says understanding how things work is the same whether it's a building or a song. It's all structure and vibration."

Jackie looked at the tools again. He'd never met a construction worker who talked about frequencies and vibration. The construction workers he'd seen were the men his father drove past on the way to Boston, guys in hard hats and orange vests who stood by the side of the highway holding signs. His father never said anything about them, which was its own kind of statement.

Nick hit the low E again. The wrench rattled. Jackie hit a chord on his guitar -- a C major, full and ringing -- and a screwdriver two hooks down from the wrench began to vibrate, slower, deeper, more like a hum than a rattle.

"See?" Nick said. "The whole garage plays along."

They went back to playing. "Bohemian Rhapsody" into "Dream On" into something that didn't have a name yet, the two guitars weaving in and out of each other like two people walking down the same hallway from opposite ends and somehow never colliding. Nick's style was raw and instinctive, his fingers finding notes by feel rather than theory, his sound rough and urgent. Jackie's was precise and structural, his training showing in the clean transitions and the careful chord voicings, his sound clear and almost fragile against Nick's distortion.

Together, they sounded like nothing either of them had heard before. Not Aerosmith. Not Queen. Something in between that belonged to both and neither.

Nick started humming. Jackie recognized the melody -- "Sweet Emotion" -- but then it shifted, and Nick was humming the vocal line from "Bohemian Rhapsody" over the Aerosmith chord progression. It shouldn't have worked. The songs were in different keys, different tempos, different universes. But Nick's voice found a way to thread the Mercury melody through the Tyler harmony, bending notes, adjusting the rhythm, making two things that didn't belong together sound like they'd been waiting for each other.

Jackie opened his mouth and sang. Not humming -- singing. The high harmony to Nick's lead, the classical training in his voice providing a counterpoint that was technically perfect but emotionally loose, following Nick's lead the way he'd learned to follow the rhythm instead of dictating it.

Their voices blended. Two twelve-year-olds in a cold garage, one voice rough and the other smooth, meeting somewhere in the middle where rough and smooth became the same thing. The wrench on the pegboard vibrated. The screwdriver hummed. A crescent wrench further down the wall joined in, adding its own metallic overtone to the sound.

The garage was singing.

They stopped at the same time, not because they planned to but because the thing they'd been playing reached its natural end the way a wave reaches shore -- it just arrives, and then it's foam and silence. They sat on their milk crates and looked at each other. The bare bulb swung slightly in a draft from under the garage door, and the shadows moved on the walls like something breathing.

"That was --" Jackie started.

"Yeah."

"We should --"

"Yeah."

"What would we even call it?"

Nick thought about this. "'Queen Smith'?"

Jackie laughed. A real laugh, the first one that felt genuine since his family had moved from Syracuse four months ago. "'Queen Smith.' That's terrible."

"'Aero Queen'?"

"Worse."

"We'll figure it out."

The garage door opened. Not the small gap at the bottom -- the whole thing, rolling up on its tracks with a metallic groan. Cold December air rushed in and the bare bulb swung harder. In the doorway, backlit by the gray afternoon sky, stood a man in a canvas work jacket and steel-toed boots caked with dried concrete. He had a lunch pail in one hand and a rolled-up newspaper under his arm. His face was weathered the way faces get when they spend eight hours a day outdoors in every season, and his hands were thick, the knuckles raw, the right thumb cracked from the cold and taped with a Band-Aid.

Dan Johnson looked at his son, then at the boy sitting across from his son, then at the two guitars, then at the pegboard where the wrench was still trembling on its hook.

He didn't say anything for a long moment. He set the lunch pail on the workbench, next to a can of WD-40 and a jar of wood screws. He unrolled the newspaper, glanced at the headline, set it down. Then he pulled a folding chair from behind the workbench, the kind with a woven seat that sagged in the middle, and opened it in the corner by the door.

He sat down and crossed his arms.

"Don't stop on my account," Dan said.

Nick looked at his father. Something passed between them -- a question and an answer that didn't need words, the kind of communication that happens between people who live in the same small space and know each other's rhythms.

"Dad, this is Jackie. He's from school."

Dan nodded at Jackie. "I heard you playing from the driveway. Sounded like you two found something."

Jackie didn't know what to say to that. Adults in his experience commented on technique -- "Your arpeggios are improving" or "Watch your tempo in the second movement." They didn't say you'd found something, as if music were a place you could arrive at rather than a skill you could perfect.

"Thank you, sir," Jackie said, because that's what you said to somebody's father when you didn't know what else to say.

Dan's mouth twitched. Not quite a smile. "You don't have to call me sir. I work for a living." He uncrossed his arms and leaned forward. "Play it again. The thing you were doing at the end. Where it was both songs at once."

Nick looked at Jackie. Jackie nodded. They started again.

Dan listened. He sat in his folding chair with his concrete-dusted boots flat on the floor and his thick hands resting on his knees, and he listened the way Jackie had only seen his piano teacher listen -- not politely, not passively, but actively, his head tracking the sound like he was following something moving through the air.

When they finished, Dan stood up. He walked to the pegboard and touched the wrench -- the nine-sixteenths that rattled at 82 hertz. He flicked it with his thumb and it rang like a small bell.

"You know why this wrench does that?"

"You told me," Nick said. "82 hertz. Resonant frequency."

"Right. But you know what that really means?" Dan looked at Jackie. "It means this wrench and that guitar string are the same note. Same fundamental. One's steel in the shape of a string. One's steel in the shape of a wrench. But when one sings, the other can't help but sing along. That's resonance. That's what I heard you two doing."

He picked up his lunch pail and his newspaper. At the door, he stopped.

"Your friend play chess?" he asked Nick.

"I don't know. Do you play chess?"

Jackie shook his head. "No."

"I'll teach you sometime. If you keep coming around." Dan looked at the two of them one more time, his eyes settling on something only he could see. Then he went into the house.

The garage was quiet. The bare bulb had stopped swinging. The wrench hung still on its hook. Outside, the December sky was going dark early the way it did in New England, the light draining out of the afternoon like water out of a sink.

Jackie began packing his guitar into its velvet-lined case. Nick leaned his battered guitar against the workbench, next to the WD-40 and the wood screws and his father's newspaper with its headline about something happening somewhere that had nothing to do with two boys in a garage in Sudbury, Massachusetts.

"Same time next Saturday?" Nick asked.

"Yeah."

"Bring your piano stuff too. If you want. My dad's got a keyboard he picked up at a yard sale. It's only got sixty-one keys and the B-flat sticks, but it works."

"A keyboard with a sticky B-flat."

"You're a classical pianist. You'll figure it out."

Jackie latched his case. He walked back down the driveway, past the crack in the concrete, past the fading yellow house, past the mailbox with no flag. He crossed Hop Brook on the plank bridge, his guitar case banging against his thigh, the boards slick with new frost.

On the other side, Concord Road waited. Houses with names. Mailboxes that matched. Lawns that would be green again in April.

He walked home in the dark. His fingers were cold. His ears were ringing from the guitars. And somewhere in his chest, in the same spot Nick had tapped with two fingers in the cafeteria three months ago, something was humming at a frequency he didn't have a name for yet.

Behind him, across the brook, the light in the Johnson garage went dark. Dan was showing Nick something on the workbench, and the sound of their voices -- too far away to hear the words -- drifted across the cold December air like the last note of a song that wasn't finished.
