CHAPTER 1: BELT BUCKLES AND DISCO SUCKS

September 1974


The cafeteria at Ephraim Curtis Middle School smelled like floor wax and government cheese. The pizza came out of the kitchen in aluminum trays, rectangles cut into squares that looked nothing like pizza and tasted like ketchup on wet cardboard. The milk arrived warm. It was always warm. The cartons sat in plastic crates that were supposed to be refrigerated, but the cooler had been broken since June and nobody had fixed it because fixing things cost money the school didn't have.

Nick Johnson carried his lunch tray to the back of the room, the way he did every day. Brown bag from home -- tuna sandwich on white bread, an apple, and a nickel taped to a note that said "get yourself a milk" in his mother's handwriting. He never bought the milk. He kept the nickel. Five cents a day added up to a quarter a week, and a quarter bought you a pack of guitar strings at Belmont Music if you didn't mind the off-brand ones.

He was twelve years old and already knew the economics of want.

The cafeteria was loud the way only a room full of twelve-year-olds could be loud -- forks scraping trays, chairs screeching on linoleum, somebody's transistor radio balanced on the windowsill pumping out the Bee Gees at a volume that made the speaker rattle. "Jive Talkin'" bounced off the cinder block walls and came back distorted, the bass line bleeding into something that sounded like a washing machine with a bad bearing.

Nick hated it. Not the noise. The song. The Bee Gees sang like someone had their parts in a vise grip, and the whole cafeteria bobbed their heads to it like that was normal, like falsetto and polyester were what music was supposed to sound like. His father played Aerosmith on the garage stereo, cranked loud enough to rattle the wrenches on the pegboard, and that was music -- guitars that sounded like they were angry about something real, drums that hit you in the chest, Steven Tyler's voice coming from somewhere deep and wild and not giving a damn if it cracked.

Nick sat down. The tray slid on the table's fake wood grain. He unwrapped his sandwich and took a bite.

Across the cafeteria, at a corner table near the emergency exit, a kid sat alone.

Nick had seen him before -- in Mr. Apfel's homeroom, second row, the one who raised his hand for every question and never got the answer wrong. New kid. Moved in over the summer from somewhere in New York. He sat with his back straight like somebody had told him posture mattered, and he was eating a sandwich that looked like it had been cut on the diagonal, which was the kind of detail Nick noticed because his mother cut sandwiches straight across and he'd never thought about it until now.

But the sandwich wasn't what caught Nick's eye.

The kid wore a belt buckle.

Not the kind you bought at Sears with your school clothes. This one was gold script on brown leather, the word QUEEN in that curling font from the album covers, the kind with the crest and the lion and the whole royal thing going on. It was real -- not painted plastic, not a sticker. Real metal. And it caught the fluorescent light every time the kid shifted in his seat, throwing a small gold flash across the cafeteria like a signal fire.

Nick looked down at his own belt. Black leather, his birthday present from his father last March. Chrome gothic letters: AEROSMITH. Steven Tyler's name wasn't on it, but you didn't need Steven Tyler's name when you had the Aerosmith wings in chrome that caught the light hard enough to blind somebody from across a room.

Which is exactly what happened.

The sun came through the cafeteria's east-facing windows at a low September angle, hit Nick's buckle, and bounced a reflection straight across thirty feet of linoleum and into the new kid's eyes. The kid flinched, threw up his forearm, squinted. When he lowered his arm and blinked the spots away, Nick was already walking toward him.

They noticed each other's buckles at the same time. Nick saw the gold Queen script. The kid saw the chrome Aerosmith wings. For a second they just stood there, two twelve-year-olds doing the math on what the other kid's belt buckle meant about who he was.

Nick pointed at the Queen buckle. "Queen? Seriously?"

The kid looked up. His eyes were brown and careful, the kind of eyes that measured things before responding. "What's wrong with Queen?"

"Nothing's wrong with Queen. Just -- Aerosmith's better."

The kid stood up. He was shorter than Nick by two inches, but he stood up like those two inches didn't matter. "Better? Have you even heard 'Bohemian Rhapsody'?"

"Have you heard 'Dream On'?"

"'Dream On' is one song. 'Bohemian Rhapsody' is an opera inside a rock song inside a piano ballad. It's six minutes of --"

"Six minutes is too long. 'Dream On' gets there in four."

"Gets where?"

"The part where you feel it. In your chest. Right here." Nick tapped his sternum with two fingers, the way his father did when he was making a point about something that mattered. "Aerosmith gets there faster because they don't waste time being fancy."

The kid opened his mouth to argue, then stopped. His head tilted. On the windowsill, the transistor radio had shifted from the Bee Gees to KC and the Sunshine Band. "That's the Way (I Like It)" blasted out with its dumb two-note bass line and its handclaps and its chorus that repeated the same six words like the songwriter had been paid by the word and only had a dollar fifty.

The kid gestured at the radio with his sandwich hand. "Can we at least agree that disco sucks?"

Nick felt something unlock in his chest. Like a door he didn't know was closed had just opened. His face broke into a grin he couldn't have stopped if he'd tried.

"Oh my God. Finally someone who gets it."

The kid grinned back. And that was it. That fast. One sentence about disco and they were allies.

"It's not even music," Nick said, sitting down across from him. "It's -- it's a beat with words on top. Where's the guitar? Where's the --"

"Where's the artistry?" The kid was leaning forward now, his diagonal sandwich forgotten. "Where's the composition? Disco is wallpaper. You put it on and forget it's there."

"My dad says disco is what happens when you let the record companies pick the music instead of the musicians."

"My dad doesn't care about music at all. He thinks it's a waste of time unless it's classical."

"Classical?"

"I play piano. Classical piano. Chopin, Bach, the whole thing. I've been playing since I was five." He said it the way you'd say you had to wear a retainer -- not bragging, just stating a fact about your life that you didn't necessarily choose.

"But you like Queen."

"I love Queen." The kid said it with a fierceness that surprised both of them. "Freddie Mercury can sing anything. Opera, rock, ballad -- he does all of it and it all sounds like he means it. My teacher says he has perfect pitch but uses it wrong. I think he uses it exactly right."

Nick unwrapped the rest of his sandwich. "My dad plays guitar. Not professionally or anything. He works construction. But he comes home and plays in the garage, and when he plays Aerosmith it's like --" He stopped, searching for the right words, which at twelve was like trying to grab smoke. "It's like the garage gets bigger. Like the walls move."

The kid was quiet for a second. "I've never heard anybody describe music like that."

"Like what?"

"Like a room changing shape."

They looked at each other. The cafeteria noise continued around them -- trays clattering, chairs squealing, disco playing, kids being kids -- but for a moment it all receded to background static, and what was left was two boys who'd found the same frequency.

Nick extended his hand across the table. "I'm Nick. Nick Johnson."

"Jackie. Jackie Marsh. My dad just moved us here from Syracuse."

"Syracuse? Where's that?"

"New York. Upstate."

"Why'd you move to Sudbury?"

"My dad got a job at BU. Teaching." Jackie paused. "It's fine. The house is nice. I just don't know anyone yet."

"Well, you know me now." Nick tapped his buckle with his index finger. "Aerosmith."

Jackie tapped his. "Queen."

"This is going to be a thing, isn't it."

"It's already a thing."

On the windowsill, the radio had moved to "The Hustle." Van McCoy's orchestral disco arrangement filled the cafeteria, and at least a dozen kids started doing the line dance between the tables, their sneakers squeaking on the linoleum, their arms pumping in synchronized mediocrity.

Nick watched them. Jackie watched them. They looked at each other and shook their heads at the same time, the way people do when they've known each other for years, not minutes.

"You play anything besides piano?" Nick asked.

"Guitar. A little. I'm learning."

"You any good?"

Jackie considered this honestly, the way he considered most things. "Not yet. I can play the chords to 'Bohemian Rhapsody' but my fingers aren't fast enough for the solo."

"Nobody's fingers are fast enough for that solo. That's why it's genius."

"Exactly."

"We should jam sometime. I've got a garage."

Jackie's eyebrows went up. "A real garage? With amps?"

"No amps. Just my dad's speakers from the living room that he lets me borrow when my mom's at work. And a concrete floor that echoes like crazy. And my dad's tools on the wall that rattle when you hit certain notes." Nick said this last part with a grin, like it was the best feature of the house.

"Tools that rattle?"

"The wrench vibrates against the vise grip when you play E minor. My dad figured out it's 82 hertz. He used to be an engineer before he went into construction -- well, not an engineer like college. But he knows how things work. He says everything has a frequency."

Jackie was staring at him. "Everything has a frequency."

"That's what he says."

The bell rang. The cafeteria erupted into motion -- trays sliding into the dish return, chairs slamming against tables, the disco radio mercifully clicking off as someone grabbed it and shoved it into their backpack. Nick and Jackie stood up at the same time.

"You live near here?" Jackie asked.

"Dutton Road. Off Route 20, past the dump."

"I'm on Concord Road."

Nick paused. He knew where Concord Road was. Everybody did. It was on the other side of Hop Brook, where the houses had shutters that matched and lawns that looked like somebody cared about them. It was a different neighborhood from Dutton Road in every way that mattered to adults and in no way that mattered to twelve-year-olds who'd just discovered they both hated disco.

"That's not far," Nick said.

"Walking distance?"

"Everything in Sudbury is walking distance if you don't mind the walk."

They left the cafeteria together, pushing through the double doors into the hallway where the fluorescent lights buzzed louder than the ones in the lunchroom and the lockers smelled like wet coats and Aqua Velva stolen from fathers' medicine cabinets. They walked side by side, still arguing -- Queen vs. Aerosmith, Freddie vs. Steven, opera rock vs. blues rock -- their voices rising and falling in a rhythm that already sounded like music.

Jackie's Queen buckle caught the hallway light and threw a gold flash on the ceiling.

Nick's Aerosmith buckle caught the light from the other side and threw chrome.

Two reflections crossing in the air above their heads, bright and brief, gone before anyone else noticed.

They didn't notice either. They were too busy arguing about whether "Killer Queen" or "Sweet Emotion" had the better opening riff, two twelve-year-old boys who'd found each other in a cafeteria that smelled like floor wax and government cheese, united by the only thing that mattered: they both believed music should be real.

The Bee Gees played on somewhere behind them, tinny and distant and already fading.

Everything else was just beginning.
