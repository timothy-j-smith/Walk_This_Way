CHAPTER 3: CAMP WINNIPESAUKEE

Summer 1975


The bus smelled like diesel and vinyl and the underarm deodorant of thirty teenagers who'd been told this was the summer that would change their lives. Nick sat by the window. Jackie sat next to him. Their acoustic guitars lay across their laps like sleeping animals, the necks pointing in opposite directions.

Nick's guitar had a new string. The high E had snapped during practice three days ago, and Grace had taped a dollar to the refrigerator with a note that said "for the guitar" in her careful handwriting. The new string was bright and silvery against the old ones, which had gone dull from months of sweat and use. It looked like a new tooth in a worn smile.

Jackie's guitar was in its black case on the overhead rack. He held his composition notebook instead -- staff paper, hand-drawn treble clefs, chord progressions he'd worked out at the kitchen table while his father graded papers in the next room. The notebook was full. He'd started another one in the back of his binder, writing smaller to fit more on each page.

The bus pulled out of the Ephraim Curtis Middle School parking lot at 7:15 on a Monday morning in June. Mrs. Landers, the chaperone, did a head count twice and came up with a different number each time. She gave up and sat down.

They wound north on Route 3, past the strip malls and the gas stations and the Friendly's where Grace worked, the sign visible from the highway -- that stupid smiling cone with its swirl of soft serve, grinning at traffic like everything was fine. Nick looked away from it. Jackie noticed him looking away but didn't say anything.

Past Nashua, the landscape changed. The strip malls thinned out. The trees got thicker, pressing in from both sides of the highway like they were trying to take back what the road had borrowed. By the time they crossed into the White Mountain foothills, the air coming through the cracked bus windows smelled different -- pine sap and lake water and cold rock, a smell that had nothing to do with fryer oil or floor wax or any of the other smells that defined their daily lives.

"It smells like a different country," Jackie said.

Nick nodded. He had his forehead pressed against the window, watching the mountains grow. His father had taken him to New Hampshire once, when Nick was eight. They'd hiked a trail that Dan said was easy but turned out to be two hours of switchbacks through birch forest. At the top, they could see three states. Dan had pointed north, east, south, and named every mountain range. He knew them the way he knew tools -- by shape and function and where they fit.

Nick didn't mention this to Jackie. He'd learned in the last few months that talking about his father came in two modes: either the words wouldn't come at all, or they came in a flood that left him feeling hollowed out. There was no middle setting.

Camp Winnipesaukee sat on the western shore of the lake, tucked into a cove where the water was calm enough to mirror the sky. The main building was a converted barn, weathered gray, with a porch that sagged in the middle and a screen door that slapped shut behind everyone who entered. The cabins radiated out from the dining hall like spokes on a wheel, each one a rectangle of unpainted pine with a green metal roof that popped and pinged in the rain.

Mr. Freedman, the camp music director, met them at the bus. He was thin in the way that people who've been thin their whole lives are thin -- not from dieting or exercise, but from some internal engine that burned fuel faster than he could put it in. His hair was white and too long, and he wore a T-shirt that said WOODSTOCK and had a coffee stain on the collar that was old enough to have its own history.

"Cabin 7," he told Nick and Jackie, pointing with a clipboard. "Music intensive. You'll be sharing with six other kids who care more about sound than sleep. If you don't like that arrangement, there's a nature cabin that studies birds." He paused. "Nobody's ever picked the birds."

Cabin 7 smelled like pine sap and mildew and something underneath both of those -- the ghost of a thousand summers of teenage boys who'd slept in these bunks and sweated into these mattresses and carved their initials into the bed frames with pocketknives they weren't supposed to have. Nick took a top bunk near the window. Jackie took the bottom bunk below him. They stashed their guitars against the wall.

The other six kids filtered in over the next hour. A drummer from Manchester who brought his own hi-hat. Twin brothers from Portland who played fiddle and banjo and spoke in a private language of inside jokes. A girl from Vermont who played upright bass and was taller than everyone in the cabin. A clarinet player from Boston whose case was covered in stickers from bands nobody had heard of. And a kid from Cambridge who played twelve-string guitar and didn't talk to anyone for the first two days.

That first night, after lights-out, Nick lay in his bunk and listened to the lake. The water made a sound against the dock pilings that was slow and regular, like breathing. Through the screen window, he could see a sliver of moon sitting on the water's surface. The pine trees were black against a sky that had more stars than Sudbury ever showed, because Sudbury had streetlights and Boston's glow on the southern horizon, and up here there was nothing between you and the universe but air.

"Nick," Jackie said from the bunk below. His voice was quiet enough that only Nick could hear it.

"Yeah."

"What if we're not good enough?"

"For what?"

"For the talent show. For any of it. What if we get up there and we're just two kids from Sudbury who think they can play?"

Nick rolled onto his side and looked over the edge of the bunk. Jackie was lying on his back, staring at the underside of Nick's mattress, his face half-lit by the moonlight coming through the screen.

"We are two kids from Sudbury who think they can play."

"That's not reassuring."

"It's not supposed to be. It's just true."

Jackie was quiet for a while. Then: "My dad thinks this is a waste of time. He told my mom that music camp was fine as long as I didn't lose sight of what matters."

"What matters?"

"His words: 'disciplined preparation for a serious career.'"

"What does that mean?"

"Juilliard. Or Yale. Or becoming a doctor. Something with a diploma on the wall."

Nick thought about this. His mother hadn't said anything about what mattered when she signed the camp permission form. She'd just signed it, quietly, the way she did most things, and put the fifty-dollar deposit on the credit card that was already carrying a balance. The prize for winning the talent show at Curtis Middle -- the talent show they'd won, together, playing their Aerosmith-Queen mashup while Karen Bowers glared from the wings -- was a week at this camp, all expenses paid. Grace had been relieved. One week she didn't have to worry about feeding him.

"My mom thinks this is the best thing that's ever happened to me," Nick said.

"Your mom's right."

"Your dad's wrong."

Jackie laughed. Quietly, so he wouldn't wake the cabin. "You can't just say that."

"I just did."

The lake breathed against the pilings. Someone in the next cabin was playing harmonica, soft and bluesy, the notes drifting through the pine trees like smoke.

"Nick?"

"Yeah."

"Thanks for being here."

"Where else would I be?"


* * *


The talent show was Friday night.

They had five days to prepare, and they spent those five days in the music building -- a converted boathouse at the lake's edge, with tall windows that let in the water light and a wooden floor that had been warped by decades of humidity into gentle hills and valleys that made you feel slightly off-balance, which Mr. Freedman said was good for creativity.

They worked on three songs. "Dream On" -- Nick's choice, because Steven Tyler's voice was built for that song and Nick's voice, at thirteen, had found a register that came close. "Somebody to Love" -- Jackie's choice, because Freddie Mercury's vocal range was inhuman but the chord structure was something Jackie could build a piano arrangement around. And the mashup -- the "Sweet Emotion" / "Somebody to Love" collision they'd discovered in the garage, which had no name and no clear structure but had a sound that made people stop what they were doing and listen.

Mr. Freedman heard them rehearsing on Tuesday afternoon and stood in the boathouse doorway for the entire run-through without saying a word. When they finished, he walked over, pulled up a chair, and sat down backwards on it with his arms crossed on the backrest.

"How long have you two been playing together?"

"Since September," Nick said.

"Eight months."

"Sounds like eight years." Freedman scratched his chin. He had the kind of stubble that suggested shaving was something he did when he remembered, which was not often. "The mashup thing at the end. Where'd that come from?"

"The garage," Nick said.

"His dad's garage," Jackie added. "The tools vibrate when we play certain chords."

Freedman looked at them like he was deciding whether they were serious. "Tools vibrate."

"At 82 hertz," Jackie said. "The wrench does it on low E. The vise grip on A. It's resonance. Nick's dad figured it out."

"Nick's dad figured out resonant frequencies in his garage."

"He reads a lot," Nick said.

Freedman was quiet for a moment. Then he stood up, put the chair back where he'd found it, and said: "Don't change anything. Play it exactly like that on Friday. And whatever you do, don't think about it too much. The second you start thinking, you'll kill whatever that is."

He left. Nick and Jackie looked at each other.

"What do you think he meant?" Jackie asked.

"I think he meant stop talking about it and play."


* * *


The talent show took place in the dining hall, which had been cleared of its long wooden tables and set up with folding chairs in rows. The stage was a platform of plywood sheets on cinder blocks, raised about a foot off the floor, with a PA system that consisted of two speakers on stands and a mixing board that Mr. Freedman operated from a card table in the back.

Thirteen acts. Eight soloists, two duos, one trio, one full band, and one kid who'd built a percussion instrument out of kitchen utensils and played it while reciting Emily Dickinson.

Nick and Jackie were eleventh.

They stood backstage -- which was behind a curtain made from a bedsheet -- and listened to the acts before them. A girl from New Hampshire played violin so beautifully that the room went silent in a way that felt like church. The twins from Portland played a bluegrass number that got people clapping along. The twelve-string kid from Cambridge, who had barely spoken all week, sat on a stool and played Joni Mitchell's "A Case of You" in a voice so quiet you had to lean forward to hear it, which made it impossible to ignore.

"These kids are good," Nick whispered.

"We're good too," Jackie said. But his hands were shaking. He put them in his pockets.

"You nervous?"

"No."

"Your hands are in your pockets."

"I'm cold."

"It's June."

"Shut up."

Nick grinned. He pulled his guitar strap over his head and adjusted it. The pawnshop guitar with its electrical tape and safety-pin strap and its one bright new string. He touched the Aerosmith buckle with his thumb -- a habit, like a prayer, something he did before every performance because the buckle was a gift from his father and touching it meant Dan was there.

"Ready?" Nick said.

Jackie took his hands out of his pockets. They were still shaking. He picked up his guitar. "Ready."

They walked out. The plywood stage flexed slightly under their weight. The PA speakers hummed with static. The audience was maybe sixty people -- campers, counselors, Mr. Freedman at his card table, and a handful of parents who'd driven up for the show.

They started with "Dream On." Nick's voice was raw in a way that thirteen-year-old voices shouldn't be -- rough from too much singing in a cold garage, cracking on the high notes not because he couldn't hit them but because the song asked for more than his body could give and he gave it anyway. Jackie played the accompaniment on acoustic guitar, his fingers clean and sure, providing the foundation that let Nick's voice be unstable without falling.

The room shifted. People sat up. Someone in the back stopped talking.

They moved into "Somebody to Love" without a pause, Jackie's voice taking the lead this time -- Freddie Mercury's melody, technically demanding, each note placed with the precision of a pianist who'd been training since he was five. Nick dropped into the harmony, his voice dark against Jackie's brightness, the two of them finding the space between rough and smooth where something new lived.

Then the mashup. No introduction, no explanation. Nick started the "Sweet Emotion" riff -- low and dirty, the rhythm guitar part that Joe Perry played like a threat. Jackie layered the "Somebody to Love" piano chords over it, translated for guitar, the two songs from different worlds colliding in a way that felt like an argument resolving into agreement.

They sang together. Nick took the Tyler lines. Jackie took the Mercury lines. The melodies crossed and tangled and separated and came back together, and somewhere in the middle of it the two songs stopped being two songs and became one thing that didn't have a name.

The room was silent when they finished. Not the polite silence that followed the other acts. A different kind. The kind where people are still hearing what just ended, the way you keep seeing a camera flash after it's gone.

Then the applause came, and it came the way the big waves at Nauset Beach come -- building offshore where you can't see it, gathering speed, hitting all at once. The folding chairs rattled. Someone stood up. Then more people stood up. Mr. Freedman, at his card table in the back, was nodding slowly, the way people nod when something they suspected has been confirmed.

Nick and Jackie stood on the plywood stage and looked at each other. Not grinning. Not celebrating. Just looking, the way you look at someone when something has happened that neither of you expected and both of you understand.

They walked off the stage. Behind the bedsheet curtain, they put down their guitars and sat on the floor with their backs against the wall.

"That was --" Jackie started.

"Yeah."

"Did you hear them?"

"Yeah."

"I think we --"

"Yeah."

They sat there. The twelfth act went on -- the kid with the kitchen utensils and the Emily Dickinson -- but they couldn't hear it through whatever was still ringing in their ears.


* * *


After the show, when the folding chairs had been stacked and the plywood stage was being dismantled and Mr. Freedman was winding cables around his elbow in the way that sound engineers do, Nick and Jackie walked down to the lake.

The water was black and still. The moon was higher now, a half-circle that laid a stripe of white across the surface like a road you could walk on. The dock creaked under their feet. They sat at the end, their legs hanging over, their sneakers almost touching the water.

"I didn't know we could do that," Jackie said.

"Do what?"

"That. What happened up there. I've performed a hundred times. Recitals, competitions, juries. I've never felt that."

"Felt what?"

Jackie thought about it. He was good at thinking about things, at finding the precise word. But this time the word didn't come. "Like the room was listening. Not just hearing. Listening."

Nick picked up a pebble from the dock and threw it into the lake. It broke the moon's reflection into shivering pieces that took a long time to settle back into place.

"My dad says that's the difference between playing and performing. Playing is what you do in the garage. Performing is when other people feel what you feel."

"When did he tell you that?"

"Last summer. We were listening to Aerosmith -- the 'Get Your Wings' album -- and I asked him why live recordings sound better than studio recordings even though the sound quality's worse. He said it's because on a live recording, you can hear the audience listening. He said the audience is the other instrument."

Jackie let that settle. On the other side of the lake, someone was playing guitar in one of the far cabins, a melody so faint it could have been imagined. The pine trees stood like an audience that had been listening for centuries.

A voice behind them said: "That was real."

They turned. A girl stood at the end of the dock, barefoot, her feet muddy from the path. She was their age -- thirteen, maybe fourteen. Her hair was somewhere between brown and blonde, the color of wheat in September, and she wore a camp T-shirt that was too big for her, the sleeves hanging past her elbows. She stood with her weight on one hip, relaxed, like someone who was used to being outdoors and had stopped worrying about how she looked a long time ago.

"I'm Patty," she said. "Patty Lally. I was in the audience."

"Nick. This is Jackie."

"I know who you are. Everybody in camp knows who you are after tonight." She walked to the end of the dock and sat down next to them, cross-legged, like she'd been invited. "That thing you did at the end. The two-songs-at-once thing. Where did that come from?"

"The garage," Nick said. It was becoming their standard answer.

"Whose garage?"

"Mine. My dad's."

Patty nodded. She didn't ask follow-up questions about the garage. She looked out at the lake instead, at the moon's reflection that had almost finished reassembling itself. "I'm from Sudbury too."

Nick turned to look at her. "You're from Sudbury?"

"Southeast side. Near Cavicchio."

"The pig farm?"

Patty looked at him evenly. "The greenhouse and pig farm, yeah. My family's been there since before the Revolution. Or the pigs have. Same difference."

Jackie laughed. Patty didn't. She wasn't joking and she wasn't apologizing.

"I go to L-S for high school in the fall," she said. "Lincoln-Sudbury. Same as you, right?"

"Yeah," Nick said. "Freshman year."

"Me too."

They were quiet for a moment. The lake made its slow breathing sound against the pilings. Somewhere in the woods, a loon called -- a long, wavering note that sounded like a question nobody could answer.

"Can I tell you something?" Patty said.

"Sure."

"What you did up there tonight -- the way you played together -- that was the best thing I've ever heard. And I'm not saying that because I'm trying to be nice. I'm saying it because it's true."

Nick and Jackie didn't say anything. They weren't used to someone their age speaking with that kind of directness. Most thirteen-year-olds talked around things, hid their opinions inside jokes or sarcasm or the safe distance of not caring. Patty talked like an adult who'd decided life was too short for anything else.

"But I want to tell you something else," she said. "And you're not going to want to hear it."

"Okay," Nick said.

Patty pulled her knees up to her chest and wrapped her arms around them. She looked at the lake, not at them. "You guys are amazing together. But the world we're going back to isn't set up for this."

The words hung in the air. The loon called again, farther away this time.

"What do you mean?" Jackie asked.

"I mean Sudbury. I mean high school. I mean -- look, I live on the southeast side. My dad raises pigs. My mom works at the greenhouse. When I go to school, I sometimes smell like pig. You know what that gets you at Lincoln-Sudbury? It gets you a seat by yourself and a nickname you didn't ask for."

She turned to look at them. "Nick, you live on Dutton Road. Jackie, you live on Concord Road. I can see both your neighborhoods from the hill behind my house, and they're less than a mile apart, and they might as well be on different planets. Right now, up here, it doesn't matter. You're two kids who play music in a garage. But when we get back --" She stopped. Picked at the dock with her thumbnail. "When we get back, there's going to be a whole system of people and rules and invisible walls telling you that you don't belong in the same room."

"That's not true," Nick said. "Jackie and I --"

"I'm not saying you won't still be friends. I'm saying the world will make it harder. Every year, harder. You'll get sorted into groups. People will have opinions about who you should hang out with based on where you live and what your parents do and how much money's in the bank account. It happens every year. I've watched it happen to older kids from my neighborhood. The ones who had friends on the other side in middle school? By junior year, they don't talk anymore."

The dock creaked. A fish jumped somewhere in the darkness, a small splash that expanded into rings.

"So what do we do?" Nick asked.

Patty shrugged. "I don't know. I'm thirteen. I don't have answers. I just have eyes." She paused. "Hold on to it, I guess. What you have. Because it's real, and real things are harder to keep than fake things. Fake things -- the cliques, the groups, the who-sits-where stuff -- those are easy. They just happen. Real things take work."

Jackie was quiet. He was thinking about Concord Road and Dutton Road and the plank bridge over Hop Brook that he crossed every Saturday, his guitar case banging against his thigh. He was thinking about his father, who never asked where he went on Saturdays and never acknowledged that Jackie had a friend on the other side of the brook. He was thinking about how easy it had been, tonight, to play with Nick in front of people -- and how that ease had come from eight months of Saturdays in a cold garage, from his father's tools vibrating at frequencies that a construction worker had bothered to calculate.

"September always kills August," Patty said. She said it quietly, like she was talking to herself. "Every year. You have this perfect summer thing, and then September comes, and reality shows up, and the thing you had starts to shrink. Not because it wasn't real. Because everything around it is telling you it shouldn't be."

She stood up. She was shorter than both of them by a head, but standing on the dock in the moonlight with the lake behind her, she looked taller.

"I'm going to bed. Early hike tomorrow." She turned to go, then stopped. "For what it's worth -- I hope you prove me wrong. I really do."

She walked down the dock, her bare feet making no sound on the wood. At the shore, she disappeared into the trees.

Nick and Jackie sat on the dock for a long time after she left. The moon moved across the sky the way moons do -- slow enough that you can't see it happening, fast enough that when you finally look it's somewhere else.

"She's wrong," Nick said.

"Maybe."

"We're not going to stop being friends because of where we live."

"I know."

"We just played the best set of our lives. Together. People were standing up. Freedman was nodding."

"I know."

"So she's wrong."

Jackie didn't answer right away. He was looking at the lake, at the moon's reflection, which had been broken and reassembled and broken again by the fish jumping and the wind rippling and the dock shifting under their weight. Every time it came back together, it was the same shape but slightly different, reassembled from the same pieces but not quite in the same arrangement.

"She's not wrong," Jackie said. "She's just early."


* * *


The last morning of camp was a Sunday. The bus arrived at nine. Parents started showing up at eight.

Nick and Jackie packed their bunks. They stripped the mattresses and folded the sheets and left the cabin the way they'd found it, minus the initials they'd carved into the bed frame with Jackie's pocketknife -- NJ + JM + PL, surrounded by a circle, the letters rough and uneven because neither of them was good at carving.

Mr. Freedman found them at the bus and shook their hands. "You two keep playing," he said. "What you have is the kind of thing people spend their whole careers looking for. Don't waste it."

"We won't," Nick said.

"We won't," Jackie said.

Patty was waiting by the camp sign. She had her bag over one shoulder and a guitar case in her hand -- a battered case with duct tape on the hinges, not unlike Nick's guitar itself. She'd never mentioned that she played. She'd watched, and listened, and offered her opinion, but she hadn't played once all week.

"You play?" Nick said, looking at the case.

"Piano mostly. Some guitar. I'm not like you two. I just like music."

"That's all we are," Jackie said. "Kids who like music."

Patty smiled. It was the first time Nick had seen her really smile, and it changed her face -- softened it, made her look her age instead of the ageless, careful thing she usually projected.

"Stay in touch," she said. "We're going to the same high school. If nothing else, I can tell you where the good lunch table is."

"Is there a good lunch table at Lincoln-Sudbury?"

"There's one near the window. You can see the parking lot and the football field. It's neutral territory -- not Ramper, not Roller, not anything. Just a table."

"You've already mapped the cafeteria and you haven't started school yet."

"I have older cousins. They tell me things." Patty adjusted her bag on her shoulder. "Remember what I said. September kills August. Don't let it."

A horn honked. A green pickup truck with rust on the quarter panels and a livestock rack in the bed pulled up to the curb. A man in a John Deere cap leaned out the window. "Patty. Let's go. The sow's due."

"That's my dad," Patty said. She didn't explain what a due sow meant. She didn't need to.

She climbed into the truck. The door stuck and she had to pull it twice. Through the window, she waved once -- a small wave, just her fingers, the kind of wave that said more in less.

The truck pulled away. The livestock rack rattled over the gravel. And then she was gone.

Grace's car arrived fifteen minutes later -- the Pontiac Catalina with the dent in the passenger door from the time she'd backed into the mailbox during an ice storm. The engine idled rough. The muffler needed work. Nick could hear it from across the parking lot.

"That's my mom," Nick said.

"I know," Jackie said.

They stood there. The camp was emptying around them -- cars pulling away, kids waving from back seats, the dining hall being locked up for the season. Mr. Freedman was carrying the PA speakers across the grass, one under each arm, like a man carrying two children who'd fallen asleep.

Dr. Marsh's car arrived. A Lincoln Continental, dark blue, waxed to a mirror finish. It pulled into the lot and parked in the shade of an elm tree. Dr. Marsh got out wearing a polo shirt and pressed slacks, the kind of casual that cost more than other people's formal.

Nick looked at the Pontiac. He looked at the Lincoln. He didn't say anything about the difference, because at thirteen you can see these things without needing to name them, and naming them would make them real in a way that right now they were just shapes in a parking lot.

"Same time next Saturday?" Nick said. "The garage?"

"Yeah. I'll be there."

They didn't hug. Boys their age didn't. They punched each other's shoulders -- right hand to left shoulder, the way they'd seen baseball players do on TV -- and then they walked in opposite directions across the parking lot, toward their separate cars, toward their separate sides of the brook.

Nick got in the Pontiac. Grace looked at him. "How was it?"

"Good."

"Just good?"

"Really good."

She smiled. She put the car in reverse. The muffler coughed. They pulled out of the parking lot, and Nick watched the camp shrink in the side mirror -- the gray barn, the green roofs, the lake glittering behind the pines.

In the Lincoln, Jackie buckled his seatbelt. Dr. Marsh pulled out onto the highway without looking at the camp.

"Did you practice your Chopin?" his father asked.

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because we were playing rock and roll, Dad. All week."

Dr. Marsh's jaw tightened. He adjusted the rearview mirror, though it didn't need adjusting. "When you get home, two hours on the piano. The recital is in October."

"Fine."

"And Jackie?"

"What."

"Music camp was fine. As a break. But we can't lose sight of what matters."

Jackie looked out the window. The mountains were shrinking behind them. The strip malls were coming back. The highway was filling up with cars heading south toward the suburbs, toward the houses with names and the houses with numbers, toward the geography that decided who you were before you got a chance to decide for yourself.

September was two months away. Patty's words sat in his chest like a stone he'd swallowed.

He reached into his bag and pulled out his composition notebook. He opened it to the last page and wrote five words in the margin, small, where his father wouldn't see:

September always kills August.

Outside the car window, the landscape flattened into Massachusetts. Sudbury was an hour away. The plank bridge over Hop Brook was waiting, the same six boards, no railing, still connecting two sides that didn't know they needed connecting.

And somewhere behind them, getting smaller in the mirror, Camp Winnipesaukee sat on the edge of its lake, holding the echo of a sound that two boys had made together -- a sound that belonged to neither of them and to both of them, a sound that didn't know yet how fragile it was.
