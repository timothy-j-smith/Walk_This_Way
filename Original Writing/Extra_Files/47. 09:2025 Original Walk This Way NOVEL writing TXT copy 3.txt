 WALK THIS WAY
*A Novel*

---

## ACT I: THE SOUND OF DIVISION

### Chapter 1: The Sound of Suburban Ghosts

The thing about suburban Boston in 1979 was that it always sounded like it was holding its breath. Nick Johnson would think about this years later, when the memories had crystallized into something harder and more painful than nostalgia. He'd remember how the sounds of Sudbury drifted through the air like musical notes on an invisible staff — the metallic thwack of hockey sticks against pavement echoing Bobby Orr's legendary slapshots, the mechanical groan of garage doors opening to reveal the secret symphonies within, the distant collision of rock and roll bleeding from kitchen radios and basement stereos, each house a different movement in the suburban opus.

It was music, all of it. Even the silence between sounds had rhythm.

The spring of '79 carried revolution on its breath. Disco was dying its glittering death, suffocating under its own excess, while rock and roll rose from the ashes like some leather-clad phoenix. You could hear it in the streets — the battle between the Bee Gees' falsetto surrender and Aerosmith's gritty triumph, between Queen's operatic ambitions and Saturday Night Fever's last desperate spins. Every driveway basketball game had its own soundtrack bleeding from boom boxes, every lawn mower pushed to the rhythm of competing beats from neighboring yards.

On this particular March afternoon, with winter finally loosening its grip on New England and the first brave crocuses pushing through the still-cold earth, Nick stood in the Johnson family garage and felt the weight of four years pressing down on him like atmospheric pressure before a storm. The concrete floor beneath his Chuck Taylors was the same concrete that had witnessed the birth of something beautiful and its subsequent, inevitable death. The oil stains formed abstract patterns that looked, if you squinted, like musical notation — quarter notes of motor oil, whole notes of transmission fluid, the ghost of his father's truck still marking time even two years after the accident, before the drinking got worse, before everything went to hell in ways that working-class families understood but never talked about.

The pegboard walls still held the same Craftsman tools his father had hung there with the precision of a man who believed that order could keep chaos at bay. Each wrench had its outlined home, each screwdriver its designated hook, like instruments waiting for an orchestra that would never reassemble. But Nick's eyes weren't drawn to the tools or the oil stains or even the stack of amplifiers in the corner that he'd bought with money saved from loading trucks at the Sudbury Hardware store.

It was the corner where the magic had happened that held him now. The corner where two milk crates had once faced each other like thrones in a kingdom only he and Jackie Marsh had ever seen. One crate was still there, its red plastic faded to the color of dried blood, the latticed sides holding the weight of what had been. The other crate was gone — had been gone for two years now, taken the same day Jackie had walked out for the last time, his arms full of Queen albums and his eyes full of something Nick couldn't name but recognized as the end of everything.

Four years ago, he thought, and the number felt like a curse, like the time signatures in a song that never resolves.

He picked up his guitar — a Fender Stratocaster he'd bought third-hand from a pawn shop in Worcester, its sunburst finish scarred but its soul intact. The weight of it was familiar, comforting in the way that only constants can be when everything else has proven temporary. His fingers found the opening riff to "Walk This Way" without conscious thought, muscle memory taking over where emotion feared to tread.

The garage responded immediately, as it always did. The sound bounced off the concrete walls, raw and immediate, filling the space with something more honest than perfection. The tools on the pegboard vibrated in sympathy with the low E string, creating their own percussion section. Even the late afternoon light streaming through the single window seemed to pulse with the rhythm, dust motes dancing like tiny groupies in the amber rays.

But it wasn't enough. Not anymore.

Because the thing about playing alone was that you could hear all the spaces where other voices should be. The garage remembered Jackie's harmony the way an amputee remembers a phantom limb — always reaching for something that wasn't there, always surprised by the absence. Nick could play all the Aerosmith he wanted, could channel Steven Tyler until his throat was raw, but the magic required two voices, two perspectives, two kids who had once believed that rock and roll could bridge any divide.

"Backstage boogie set your pants on fire," he sang, his voice carrying the Massachusetts accent that Jackie used to tease him about — before Jackie had learned to modulate his own voice into something more refined, more suitable for the son of a Boston University professor. "Then you down to the peepshow trying to get the lowdown..."

The words echoed in the empty garage, and for a moment — just a moment — Nick could swear he heard another voice joining in, hitting the high notes he could never reach, adding the theatrical flair that had always been Jackie's gift. But when he stopped playing, there was only silence and the distant sound of the Weatherbys' dog barking at shadows.

He set the guitar down carefully, treating it with the reverence it deserved, and walked to the workbench where a yellowed piece of notebook paper was tacked to the wall. The setlist from their last performance together — September 15th, 1975, the Ephraim Curtis Middle School Fall Talent Show. Jackie's neat handwriting alternating with Nick's barely legible scrawl:

1. Bohemian Rhapsody / Sweet Emotion (Medley) — J&N
2. Somebody to Love — J (lead), N (harmony)  
3. Walk This Way — N (lead), J (harmony)
4. We Are The Champions / Dream On (Finale) — J&N

They'd won first place that night. The trophy was probably still somewhere in Jackie's house, gathering dust in a room full of things that mattered once.

### Chapter 2: When the Music Died (The First Time)

*Four Years Earlier — Spring 1975*

The cafeteria at Ephraim Curtis Middle School was what passed for purgatory when you were twelve years old in Sudbury. It was a space that managed to be simultaneously too big and too small — too big when you were the new kid looking for somewhere to sit, too small when you were trying to avoid someone who'd decided you were their particular project for the day. The fluorescent lights hummed at a frequency that made everyone look vaguely green, like they were all slightly seasick on dry land.

Jackie Marsh understood this particular circle of hell better than most. He'd been the new kid three times in seven years — Ithaca to Albany to Sudbury, following his father's academic career like a reluctant satellite orbiting an ambitious star. He'd learned the choreography of being new: the careful observation period, the strategic friend selection, the delicate balance between standing out enough to be noticed and blending in enough to avoid becoming a target.

But Sudbury was different. Sudbury had Nick Johnson.

The first time Jackie saw him, Nick was standing on a lunch table, arms spread wide like a classic rock messiah, singing "Sweet Emotion" to a cafeteria full of kids who were equal parts impressed and embarrassed. Someone's boom box was providing the backing track, tinny and distorted through its small speakers, but Nick didn't seem to care. He was lost in the performance, his whole body moving with the kind of unselfconscious abandon that Jackie envied and feared in equal measure.

"Sweet emotion," Nick sang, his voice cracking on the high notes but powering through with sheer determination. "Sweet emotion..."

It was terrible. It was magnificent. It was the most alive thing Jackie had seen since arriving in this suburb where everything seemed preserved in invisible amber.

And then Nick's eyes had found his across the crowded cafeteria, drawn perhaps by the fact that Jackie was the only one not laughing or looking away in secondhand embarrassment. For a moment, they just looked at each other — Nick still standing on the table, Jackie frozen with his lunch tray in his hands. Then Nick's gaze had dropped to Jackie's belt, where a gold Queen buckle caught the fluorescent light like a small sun.

Nick jumped down from the table in one fluid motion, crossing the cafeteria with the confidence of someone who'd never met a social situation he couldn't conquer through sheer force of personality. Up close, Jackie could see that Nick's Aerosmith belt buckle was actually held together with electrical tape on one side, that his jeans had been patched at the knee with fabric that didn't quite match, that his Black Sabbath t-shirt had been washed so many times the graphic was more suggestion than image.

"Queen?" Nick had said, pointing at Jackie's buckle with the kind of disdain usually reserved for disco. "Seriously?"

"What's wrong with Queen?" Jackie had responded, feeling his spine straighten the way it did when his father questioned his music choices.

"Nothing, if you like your rock and roll served with a side of theater kid," Nick said, but he was grinning, and there was something in his eyes that suggested this was a test rather than an attack.

"Better than thinking volume equals talent," Jackie shot back, surprising himself with the easy comeback.

Nick's grin widened. "Oh, this is going to be fun. I'm Nick. You're the new kid from New York, right?"

"Ithaca," Jackie corrected. "And it's Jackie."

"Jackie," Nick repeated, like he was tasting the name. "Yeah, okay. You play anything, Jackie from Ithaca?"

"Piano. Guitar. A little violin."

"Jesus," Nick laughed. "What are you, the von Trapp family?"

Jackie felt heat rise to his cheeks, but before he could formulate a response, the cafeteria speakers crackled to life with the opening notes of "Dancing Queen." Both boys winced simultaneously, like they'd been physically struck.

"Okay," Nick said, looking at Jackie with newfound respect. "Maybe we have more in common than belt buckles would suggest. You want to jam sometime?"

Jackie thought about his mother's carefully scheduled afternoon activities — piano lessons on Mondays, violin on Wednesdays, academic enrichment on Thursdays. Then he thought about the way Nick had looked on that table, completely free, completely himself.

"Yeah," Jackie said. "I'd like that."

### Chapter 3: The Harmony Discovery

*Two weeks later — Johnson Family Garage*

The garage was nothing like the music room in Jackie's house, with its acoustic panels and climate control and the baby grand piano that had been his grandmother's. The Johnson garage was pure function — a place where things got fixed or stored or forgotten. It smelled of motor oil and WD-40 and the ghost of cigarette smoke from a previous era. Extension cords snaked across the floor like veins, connecting amplifiers that Nick had assembled from parts scavenged from yard sales and the electronics recycling bin behind the Radio Shack.

But when Nick plugged in his guitar — a Sears Silvertone that looked like it had survived a war — and struck the first chord, the garage transformed.

"Okay," Nick said, adjusting an amp that was held together with duct tape and faith. "Here's how this works. We each play something we love, and we see if we can make them talk to each other."

Jackie was skeptical. In his world, music was about precision, about playing the notes as written, about honoring the composer's intent. But Nick approached music like a conversation with an old friend — casual, intuitive, ready to go wherever the moment took them.

Nick started with "Dream On," his fingers finding the ascending arpeggios with surprising delicacy. "Every time that I look in the mirror," he sang, his voice young but somehow already carrying weight, "all these lines on my face getting clearer..."

Jackie listened, his classically trained ear automatically cataloging the places where Nick's technique was rough, where his fingering was inefficient, where his voice strained instead of soared. But there was something else there too, something his piano teacher would never understand. Nick wasn't performing the song — he was living it, feeling it, bleeding it through his fingertips onto the strings.

"Your turn," Nick said, finishing with a flourish that was more enthusiasm than accuracy.

Jackie picked up Nick's acoustic guitar — a Yamaha that was actually in decent shape — and considered his options. He could play something technically complex, show off the years of training, establish his credentials. But something about the way Nick had played, so open and vulnerable, made him want to respond in kind.

He played the opening to "Bohemian Rhapsody," the gentle piano part translated to guitar, and began to sing. "Is this the real life? Is this just fantasy? Caught in a landslide, no escape from reality..."

His voice was clearer than Nick's, more controlled, hitting the notes with the precision of someone who'd been taught that mistakes were moral failures. But as he played, he found himself loosening, letting the garage's rough acoustics add texture to the sound instead of fighting against them.

"Open your eyes, look up to the skies and see," he continued, and then something extraordinary happened.

Nick joined in on the harmony.

Not the harmony as written, not the careful arrangement that Freddie Mercury had labored over in the studio. This was something else, something intuitive and wild and absolutely right. Nick's rougher voice wrapped around Jackie's clarity like sandpaper on silk, creating a texture that neither could achieve alone.

They looked at each other, eyes wide, both recognizing that something had shifted in the garage, in the air between them, in the very fabric of what they understood about music.

"Do that again," Nick said, his voice hushed like they were in church.

They did it again. And again. And again, until the sun was setting and Jackie's mother was calling on the phone and Nick's mother was coming home from her first job to grab dinner before her second.

"We could do something with this," Nick said as Jackie packed up to leave. "Like, really do something."

"Like what?" Jackie asked, though he already knew the answer.

"Like change everything," Nick said, and in that moment, in that garage that smelled of motor oil and possibility, Jackie believed him.

### Chapter 4: The Gravitational Pull of Class

*Fall 1975 — Ephraim Curtis Middle School*

By October, Jackie and Nick were inseparable, or as inseparable as two kids from different zip codes of the social universe could be. They had developed a routine: Jackie would take the late bus to Nick's neighborhood three times a week, telling his parents he was staying for chess club. Nick would bike to Jackie's house on weekends, when Dr. Marsh was at the university and Mrs. Marsh was at her book club, the two boys disappearing into the basement where Jackie had set up a makeshift studio.

But gravity has a way of asserting itself, especially in the carefully ordered solar system of middle school.

It started small. Comments from Jackie's other friends — the kids from the advanced classes, the ones whose parents all knew each other from the country club or the PTA fundraising committee.

"Why do you hang out with that Johnson kid?" Bradley Worthington had asked, his voice carrying the casual cruelty of someone who'd never had to question his place in the world. "My dad says his family is..."

Bradley had trailed off, but the implication hung in the air like a bad chord.

For Nick, it was more direct. The kids from his neighborhood, the ones whose older brothers worked at the auto shop or the grocery store, had their own opinions.

"Getting too good for us, Nicky?" Tony Russo had said, shoving Nick against the lockers just hard enough to make a point. "Hanging with the rich kids now?"

"He's not rich," Nick had protested, which was technically true. The Marshes were comfortable, not wealthy, but in the economics of middle school, anyone whose house had more than one bathroom might as well have been a Rockefeller.

The music kept them together, but the world kept trying to pull them apart.

### Chapter 5: The Talent Show Triumph

*September 15th, 1975 — Curtis Middle School Auditorium*

The night of the fall talent show arrived like a reckoning. The auditorium was packed — parents armed with cameras, teachers trying to maintain order, students vibrating with the particular energy that came from being out of the classroom after dark.

Jackie and Nick stood in the wings, watching act after act take the stage. There was Melissa Chen playing classical violin with technical perfection and emotional vacancy. The Thompson twins juggling with more enthusiasm than skill. Bradley Worthington performing a magic act that fooled no one but was met with polite applause because his father had donated the new auditorium curtains.

"We're going to destroy them," Nick whispered, and Jackie could hear both excitement and nerves in his voice.

"We're going to be good," Jackie corrected, but his hands were shaking as he adjusted his guitar strap.

They'd practiced their set a hundred times, but never in front of people. In the garage or Jackie's basement, they could create their own world, their own rules. Out here, under the lights, they would be judged by people who didn't understand what they were trying to do.

Mr. Peterson, the music teacher, introduced them with his usual dramatic flair. "Ladies and gentlemen, performing an original arrangement, please welcome Jackie Marsh and Nick Johnson!"

They walked onto the stage, and Jackie felt the lights hit him like a physical force. The audience was a dark mass beyond the footlights, full of judgment and expectation. He looked at Nick, who seemed smaller suddenly, less confident.

Then Nick grinned and mouthed, "Let's blow their minds."

They opened with the medley — "Bohemian Rhapsody" flowing into "Sweet Emotion" in a way that shouldn't have worked but did. Jackie's classical training provided the structure while Nick's raw energy provided the soul. When they hit the harmony on "Caught in a landslide, no escape from reality," Jackie heard someone in the audience gasp.

By the time they reached the finale — "We Are The Champions" morphing into "Dream On" — the auditorium was silent except for their voices, their guitars, and what felt like the heartbeat of everyone in the room.

"Dream until your dreams come true!" they sang together, Nick hitting the low notes while Jackie soared on the high ones, their voices meeting in the middle like hands clasping across a divide.

The last note hung in the air for a moment that felt eternal.

Then the auditorium exploded.

People were on their feet, applauding with the kind of enthusiasm usually reserved for professional performances. Jackie saw his mother wiping tears from her eyes, his father nodding with what might have been approval. Nick's mother was beaming, her exhaustion temporarily forgotten in the pride of the moment.

They won first place by unanimous decision.

Standing on the stage, holding the trophy between them, Jackie and Nick looked at each other with the kind of pure joy that only comes before you learn how easily joy can be taken away.

"We did it," Nick said, his voice full of wonder.

"We're just getting started," Jackie replied, and meant it.

But in the audience, forces were already in motion that would pull them apart. Dr. Marsh was thinking about the academic opportunities Jackie was missing while spending so much time on music. Nick's mother was wondering how she could afford to support her son's musical ambitions when she could barely keep the lights on. And somewhere between Queen and Aerosmith, between precision and passion, a fault line was forming that would eventually crack their world in half.

### Chapter 6: The Thanksgiving Fracture

*November 1975*

The end began, as most endings do, with the best of intentions.

Dr. Marsh had invited the Johnsons to Thanksgiving dinner. It was, he'd told his wife, the charitable thing to do. "The boy has been a good friend to Jackie," he'd said, in the tone of someone awarding points for adequate performance. "And from what I understand, their situation is... difficult."

Mary Johnson had accepted with the kind of dignity that comes from swallowing pride so often it becomes second nature. She'd made her famous apple pie from scratch, the one thing she knew she could do better than any country club caterer.

The dinner was a disaster of politeness.

The Marshes' dining room was like something from a magazine — matching china, real silver silverware, cloth napkins folded into precise triangles. Nick sat next to his mother, acutely aware of how his best school clothes looked shabby against the backdrop of casual elegance. Jackie sat across from him, wearing a sweater that probably cost more than Nick's entire wardrobe, looking miserable.

"So, Mrs. Johnson," Dr. Marsh said, carving the turkey with surgical precision, "what is it you do?"

"I work at Friendly's," Mary said, her chin lifting slightly. "And I do some house cleaning on the side."

"How... industrious," Mrs. Marsh said, in a tone that suggested industrious was a polite word for unfortunate.

The conversation limped along, punctuated by the clink of silverware and the occasional attempt by Jackie or Nick to inject something normal, something about music or school or anything that wasn't this careful dance of class consciousness.

Then Dr. Marsh made his move.

"Nicholas," he said, and Nick knew from the use of his full name that something bad was coming. "You're clearly a talented young man. Have you given thought to your future?"

"I want to be a musician," Nick said without hesitation.

Dr. Marsh's smile was the kind you'd give a child who said they wanted to be a dinosaur. "That's a fine dream. But what about something practical? Jackie here will be attending Phillips Academy next year. Early acceptance, of course. They have an excellent music program, but more importantly, they prepare young men for real futures. Harvard, Yale, that sort of thing."

The words landed like bombs in the careful détente of dinner.

"Phillips Academy?" Nick looked at Jackie, who was studying his mashed potatoes with intense focus. "That's... that's a boarding school. In Andover."

"The best in New England," Dr. Marsh said proudly. "Jackie's scores were exceptional. And of course, my connections didn't hurt."

"You're leaving?" Nick's voice cracked, and he hated himself for it.

"Not until next fall," Jackie said quietly. "I was going to tell you..."

"When?" Nick stood up, his chair scraping against the hardwood floor. "When were you going to tell me? After we planned our whole future? After we—"

"Nicholas," his mother said quietly, but Nick was beyond stopping.

"This is exactly what you people do," he said, looking at Dr. Marsh. "You come into people's lives, you make them believe things, and then you just leave when something better comes along."

"Now, young man," Dr. Marsh started, his face reddening.

"Dad, don't," Jackie said, standing too. "Nick, it's not like that—"

"Then what's it like?" Nick demanded. "We're partners, or we were. We were going to start a real band, make real music. But I guess that was just something to do until you could get into your fancy school with your fancy music program."

"That's not fair—"

"Fair?" Nick laughed, but there was no humor in it. "When has anything ever been fair? You get to leave. You get to go to your special school and become whatever your parents want you to be. I get to stay here and—"

"And what?" Dr. Marsh interrupted, standing now too. "Stay here and make something of yourself? That's what real people do, Nicholas. They don't run away to fancy schools. They work."

"Richard," Mrs. Marsh said quietly, but the damage was done.

Mary Johnson stood, placing her napkin carefully on the table. "Thank you for dinner," she said with quiet dignity. "Nicholas, we're leaving."

"Mom—"

"Now."

As they walked to the door, Jackie called after them. "Nick, wait. Please. We can figure this out."

Nick turned at the door, and Jackie would remember the look in his eyes for years afterward — betrayal and hurt and the death of innocence all at once.

"There's nothing to figure out," Nick said. "You're them, and I'm us. I just forgot for a while."

The door closed with a quiet click that echoed like a gunshot.

### Chapter 7: The Winter of Silence

*December 1975 - February 1976*

Winter in New England has a way of preserving things — grief, anger, the space between two people who used to be inseparable. The snow that year fell early and heavy, blanketing Sudbury in white that made everything look clean and new from a distance but was gray and dirty up close, especially on the side of town where the plows came last.

Jackie threw himself into his studies with the fervor of someone trying to outrun their own heart. Phillips Academy required excellence, his father reminded him daily, and excellence left no room for distractions. He practiced piano for hours, but only classical pieces now — Bach, Mozart, Chopin. The precision was soothing, the way each note had its place, its purpose, its predictable resolution.

But at night, when the house was quiet and his parents were asleep, he would sit at his window and watch the snow fall, and his fingers would unconsciously play "Dream On" on the windowsill, tapping out the rhythm of what they'd lost.

Nick, meanwhile, had started hanging with a different crowd — Tony Russo and the other kids who smoked behind the gymnasium and talked about cars and girls and how they couldn't wait to be done with school so real life could begin. He still played guitar, but now it was angry, aggressive — Black Sabbath, Deep Purple, Led Zeppelin at volumes that made the garage windows rattle.

His mother watched him change with the quiet heartbreak of a parent who could see the wound but couldn't heal it.

"You could call him," she suggested one night over dinner — mac and cheese again, the kind from a box because it was the last week before payday.

"Call who?" Nick asked, though they both knew.

"He's just a boy too, honey. Boys make mistakes."

"He's not like us, Mom. He never was."

Mary reached across the table and took her son's hand, rough from his guitar strings, calloused already at thirteen. "Music doesn't care about money, baby. It doesn't care about class or neighborhoods or any of that. You taught me that."

Nick pulled his hand away. "Yeah, well, maybe I was wrong."

At school, they became ghosts to each other. They had no classes together — Jackie was in all advanced placement, Nick in general education. When they passed in the hallways, they looked through each other with the practiced blindness of former friends who knew each other too well to pretend they were strangers but couldn't bear to acknowledge what they'd become.

The talent show trophy sat in the school's display case, their names engraved together in brass that would outlast their friendship by decades.

### Chapter 8: The Spring Collapse

*March 1976*

The letter from Phillips Academy arrived on a Tuesday that felt like spring might actually come that year. Jackie's acceptance was formal, official, complete with a scholarship that would cover most of the tuition — a necessity, as Dr. Marsh had privately admitted to his wife, given the cost.

"We're so proud of you," his mother said, holding the letter like it was made of gold.

"This is your future, son," his father added. "The beginning of everything."

Jackie nodded and smiled and said all the right things, but inside, something was dying. He walked to his room and picked up his guitar — the acoustic he'd played that night at the talent show. His fingers found "Somebody to Love" without thinking.

"Can anybody find me somebody to love?" he sang quietly, and his voice broke on the last note.

Two miles away, Nick was in the garage, teaching himself "Walk This Way" for the hundredth time. He'd figured out most of Joe Perry's guitar parts, but some of the nuances escaped him. There were parts that needed a second guitar, a harmony, a partner.

"Backstage boogie," he sang, but the words felt hollow without Jackie's higher harmony floating above them.

His mother knocked on the garage door. "Nick? There's someone here to see you."

For a moment, his heart jumped. But when he walked into the kitchen, it wasn't Jackie. It was Mr. Peterson, the music teacher, holding a folder and wearing his usual bow tie that made him look like he'd time-traveled from 1962.

"Nicholas," Mr. Peterson said, and his use of the full name made Nick wary. "I wanted to talk to you about next year."

"I'm not going to Phillips Academy if that's what this is about," Nick said defensively.

"No," Mr. Peterson said gently. "But Lincoln-Sudbury High School has a music program too. A good one. And I've spoken to the director there. They're very interested in having you."

"I don't do school music," Nick said. "All that classical stuff, it's not—"

"It's not Jackie's thing, you mean," Mr. Peterson interrupted. "But it could be yours. Music theory, composition, arrangement — these aren't just for classical musicians, Nicholas. Steven Tyler knows theory. Joe Perry understands composition. The difference between a good rock musician and a great one is understanding why the music works, not just how to play it."

Nick was quiet for a moment. "Jackie's really going away, isn't he?"

"Yes," Mr. Peterson said simply. "But that doesn't mean your music has to end."

"Our music already ended," Nick said, and the finality in his voice made Mr. Peterson's heart ache for the lost potential of two boys who could have been extraordinary together.

### Chapter 9: The Last Day

*June 1976 — Last Day of Eighth Grade*

The last day of middle school had the strange quality of being both an ending and a beginning, a threshold between who you were and who you were about to become. The hallways were chaos — yearbooks being signed, lockers being cleaned out, promises to stay in touch that everyone knew wouldn't be kept.

Jackie was surrounded by well-wishers. Everyone wanted to be remembered by the kid who was going to Phillips Academy, as if proximity to his potential might rub off somehow. He signed yearbooks with practiced messages: "Have a great summer!" "Stay cool!" "See you around!"

But his eyes kept searching for one face in the crowd.

He found Nick by his locker, alone, throwing things into a garbage bag with the efficiency of someone who'd learned not to be sentimental about objects.

"Nick."

Nick didn't turn around. "Jackie."

They stood there, the hallway chaos flowing around them like a river around two stones.

"I wanted to—"

"Don't," Nick said, still not turning. "Just don't."

"We could still play this summer. Before I leave."

Now Nick did turn, and his eyes were hard in a way that made him look older than fourteen. "Play what? The game where we pretend we're equals? Where we act like you're not leaving for bigger and better things while I'm stuck here?"

"It's not like that—"

"It's exactly like that." Nick slammed his locker shut. "You know what the worst part is? I actually believed it. When we were up there on that stage, when we were making that music, I actually believed that none of the rest of it mattered. But it does matter. It always mattered."

"The music was real," Jackie said quietly. "That was never a lie."

"Maybe," Nick said, picking up his bag. "But real isn't always enough, is it?"

He walked away, and Jackie let him go. What else could he do? He was fourteen years old and the world had already decided their trajectories, set them on paths that diverged like railroad tracks, parallel for a while but destined for different destinations.

### Chapter 10: The Audition That Changed Everything

*September 1976 — Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School*

High school was a different universe, and Nick entered it alone but determined. Lincoln-Sudbury Regional was bigger than Curtis Middle School, pulling kids from multiple towns, and in that bigness, he could reinvent himself. He wasn't the kid who'd lost his musical partner. He was just Nick Johnson, freshman, nobody special yet.

The music room was in the arts wing, a part of the school that always smelled faintly of paint and sawdust from the adjacent art and shop classes. Nick stood outside the door, listening to someone playing piano inside — a classical piece, all precision and no soul.

"You coming in or just enjoying the concert?"

Nick turned to find Mr. Wentworth, the high school music director. He was younger than Mr. Peterson, with a salt-and-pepper beard and a ponytail that suggested he'd been a hippie before becoming a teacher.

"I don't do classical," Nick said.

"Good," Mr. Wentworth said. "Neither do I. Well, not exclusively. Come on."

The music room was bigger than anything at Curtis, with practice rooms, a baby grand piano, and enough instruments to outfit a small orchestra. But what caught Nick's eye were the posters on the walls — not Bach and Beethoven, but The Who, Cream, Jimi Hendrix, and yes, Aerosmith.

"Mr. Peterson told me about you," Mr. Wentworth said, sitting at the piano. "Said you had a gift for harmony and arrangement."

"I had a partner for that," Nick said, the words coming out bitter.

"Had," Mr. Wentworth agreed. "Past tense. What about now?"

He played something on the piano — the opening to "Dream On," but arranged differently, fuller, more complex.

"That's not how it goes," Nick said.

"No," Mr. Wentworth agreed. "It's how it could go. With a full band. With proper arrangement. With understanding of why Tyler and Perry made the choices they did." He stood up. "I'm starting something new this year. A rock ensemble. Real rock, not watered-down school versions. But I need students who understand that rock and roll isn't just three chords and attitude. It's musicianship. It's theory. It's understanding the conversation between structure and rebellion."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because Mr. Peterson said you had the ear for it. And because I think you're angry enough to be great if you can channel it into something besides self-pity."

The words stung, but Nick recognized truth when he heard it.

"What would I have to do?"

"Learn. Practice. Be open to the idea that maybe, just maybe, understanding music theory won't make you less rock and roll. It might make you more."

Nick thought about Jackie, probably sitting in some wood-paneled room at Phillips Academy, learning from the best teachers money could buy. Then he thought about himself, standing in a public school music room with a teacher who had Hendrix posters on the walls.

"When do we start?"

Mr. Wentworth smiled. "How about now?"

### Chapter 11: Parallel Lives

*Fall 1976 - Spring 1977*

Miles and circumstance separated them now, but Jackie and Nick were living variations of the same story, like a song played in different keys.

At Phillips Academy, Jackie was drowning in excellence. Everything was competitive — grades, sports, even leisure time had to be optimized for college applications. He joined the choir because it was expected. He played piano for the school's chamber ensemble because it looked good on transcripts. But late at night, in his dorm room, he would put on headphones and listen to Queen's "A Night at the Opera," letting Freddie Mercury's voice remind him that rock and roll could be art, could be important, could be worth sacrificing for.

His roommate, Charles Worthington III (Bradley's cousin, because of course he was), caught him once air-guitaring to "Bohemian Rhapsody."

"Marsh, what the hell are you doing?"

"Nothing," Jackie said, quickly turning off the music.

"Is that Queen? Seriously?"

"It's... complex. The arrangements are sophisticated."

Charles laughed, not unkindly. "You can just say you like rock music, you know. Half the guys here have secret vinyl collections. Beckwith has every Stones album ever made."

But Jackie couldn't explain that it wasn't just about liking rock music. It was about a garage that smelled like motor oil and dreams, about harmonies discovered by accident, about a friendship that had made him feel more himself than any acceptance letter ever could.

Meanwhile, at Lincoln-Sudbury, Nick was discovering that he had a gift for arrangement that went beyond his collaboration with Jackie. Under Mr. Wentworth's guidance, he was learning to read music properly, to understand chord progressions not just by ear but by theory. He could take a simple song and reimagine it, layer it, make it bigger without losing its essential truth.

"You've got good instincts," Mr. Wentworth told him after he'd arranged a version of "Sweet Emotion" for the full rock ensemble. "Raw talent refined by understanding. That's a rare combination."

"My... friend used to say I had no discipline," Nick said, the word "friend" careful, protected.

"Your friend was wrong. You have a different kind of discipline. The kind that comes from having to work for everything, from having to prove yourself every day. That's its own kind of rigor."

Nick threw himself into the music with the dedication of someone who had something to prove — to the world, to Jackie, to himself. He formed a band with some of the other kids from Lincoln, the smaller and scrappier of the two towns that fed into the regional high school. There was Stephen Murphy on bass, a stocky kid with steady hands and a good ear. Dave Cassel on drums, skinny but powerful, with rhythm in his bones. Mark Pianka on keyboards, quiet but musically intuitive.

They practiced in Nick's garage, the same garage where everything had started and ended. But now it was different. Now it was his space, his sound, his vision.

### Chapter 12: The Song That Brought Them Back

*March 15, 1979 — Present Day*

Which brought Nick back to now, standing in that same garage four years later, seventeen and angry and accomplished and empty all at once. He'd heard Jackie was back from Phillips Academy, that he'd gotten early acceptance to Cornell but was deferring for a gap year. The rumor was that Dr. Marsh was furious, that there had been fights, that Jackie had moved back home but was barely speaking to his parents.

Nick picked up his guitar again, started playing something different. Not Aerosmith this time, but something of his own, something he'd been working on with the band. It was built on the bones of "Dream On" but transformed into something new, something that acknowledged the past while pushing toward the future.

"I've been lost in the memories," he sang, words he'd written late at night when the garage felt too empty. "Of the music that we made. Every harmony reminds me, of the price we both have paid."

"Not bad," a voice said from the doorway. "But the bridge needs work."

Nick spun around, his heart stopping for a moment that stretched like a held note.

Jackie stood in the doorway of the garage, looking older, taller, but with the same eyes that had found him across a middle school cafeteria a lifetime ago. He was wearing jeans and a simple t-shirt, not the preppy uniform Nick had imagined. And at his waist, catching the late afternoon light, was the same Queen belt buckle from seventh grade.

"What are you doing here?" Nick's voice came out rougher than he intended.

"I heard you playing," Jackie said, stepping into the garage cautiously, like someone entering a church. "From the street. I was just... walking."

"In my neighborhood?" Nick set down his guitar, defensive. "Phillips Academy boys don't usually slum it on this side of town."

"I'm not at Phillips anymore," Jackie said quietly. "I'm home."

"I heard. Daddy must be thrilled."

Jackie's jaw tightened, but he didn't take the bait. Instead, he looked around the garage, taking in the changes — more equipment, better amps, the posters of bands they'd discovered together now joined by newer ones.

"You've gotten better," Jackie said. "Your arrangement skills... that was really good."

"Yeah, well, some of us had to learn the hard way. We didn't all get private tutors and—"

"I missed it," Jackie interrupted. "I missed this. I missed..."

He trailed off, but the unsaid word hung between them like a suspended chord waiting for resolution.

"You missed what?" Nick challenged. "The garage? The wrong side of town? What exactly did the golden boy miss while he was off conquering the world?"

"You," Jackie said simply. "I missed you, Nick. I missed us. I missed what we could make together."

The words landed like a physical blow. Nick turned away, unable to look at Jackie's face, at the honesty there that threatened to crack open four years of carefully maintained anger.

"You left," Nick said. "You chose them."

"I was fourteen. My parents chose. I just... went along."

"You could have fought."

"Could I? Really? Could you have fought if your mom had the chance to give you something better?"

Nick wanted to argue, but he knew Jackie was right. At fourteen, you went where your parents pointed you. At fourteen, you didn't understand that some choices couldn't be undone.

"What do you want, Jackie?" Nick asked, exhaustion creeping into his voice. "Why are you here?"

Jackie reached into his pocket and pulled out a folded piece of paper. He handed it to Nick, who recognized it immediately — the setlist from their talent show, the one Nick had tacked to his wall.

"Where did you get this?"

"I've had it," Jackie said. "I've carried it with me for four years. Through every classical recital, every chamber performance, every moment when I was playing music I was supposed to love but felt nothing." He took a breath. "I want to play again. Real music. Our music. I want to see if... if there's anything left."

Nick looked at the setlist, at their handwriting intertwined, at the evidence of what they'd been capable of when they were too young to know it was impossible.

"There's a Battle of the Bands," Nick said slowly. "March 15th. At school."

"I know. I saw the posters."

"I have a band. We're entering."

"I know that too."

They stood there, two seventeen-year-olds carrying the weight of a friendship that had defined and destroyed them in equal measure.

"We could..." Jackie started.

"No," Nick said firmly. "I have my band. You want to enter, enter with your own. We're not partners anymore, Jackie. We're not friends. We're just... two guys who used to know each other."

Jackie nodded, something dying in his eyes but something else being born — determination, maybe, or acceptance.

"Okay," he said. "Then I'll see you at the battle."

He turned to leave, then stopped. "Nick? That song you were working on? The bridge should modulate up a minor third. It'll create the tension you're looking for before the resolution."

Nick wanted to tell him to go to hell, wanted to say he didn't need Jackie's advice, didn't need his perfect pitch and his theoretical knowledge. But Jackie was right. He was always right about the technical stuff.

"I'll think about it," Nick said.

Jackie smiled, small and sad. "See you around, Nick."

"Yeah," Nick said to the empty doorway. "See you around."

He picked up his guitar and played the bridge again, modulating up a minor third like Jackie had suggested. It was perfect. Of course it was.

In the distance, he could hear someone playing piano — classical, precise, but with an underlying sadness that suggested the player's heart wasn't entirely in it. The Marshes lived three miles away, too far for sound to actually carry, but Nick could hear it anyway. The same way he'd been hearing Jackie's harmony in every song he'd played for four years.

The Battle of the Bands was in five days. Five days to prove that he didn't need Jackie Marsh, didn't need his classical training or his perfect pitch or his ability to make Nick better just by being in the same room.

Five days to prove that some partnerships, once broken, couldn't be rebuilt.

Even if the music still remembered what they'd been together.

Even if every song still felt half-empty without the other voice.

Even if.

### Chapter 13: The Poster That Changes Everything

*March 16, 1979 — Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School*

Monday morning arrived like a power chord at 6 AM — jarring, inevitable, and impossible to ignore. The hallways of Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School hummed with the particular frequency of teenage anticipation, that electric current that runs through a school when something is about to happen, even if no one quite knows what yet.

Nick arrived early, which was unlike him, but sleep had been elusive after Jackie's visit. He'd spent the night playing and replaying their conversation, finding harmonies in the silences, discordance in the words. His fingers were raw from practicing until 3 AM, working through the bridge modulation Jackie had suggested, hating that it was perfect, hating more that Jackie could still hear what was missing in his music from just a few bars.

The music hallway was already busy. Someone was practicing scales in Room 105, the sound floating through the closed door like a ghost of classical ambition. Further down, the jazz band was setting up, their casual tuning creating an accidental symphony of prepared chaos.

But it was the crowd gathered around the main bulletin board that caught Nick's attention.

"What's going on?" he asked Sarah Chen, a junior who played violin in the orchestra but had bass player fingers — long, strong, practical.

"Wentworth just posted something," she said, standing on her toes to see over the crowd. "Something about a battle."

Nick pushed through, his height advantage letting him see over most of the gathered students. There, in the center of the board, was a poster that would change everything:

**BATTLE OF THE BANDS**
**March 15, 1979**
**Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School Auditorium**
**Open to All Regional High School Bands**
**Eight Bands Maximum**
**Grand Prize: $500 and Professional Recording Session at Longview Studios**
**Judged by: WBCN "The Rock of Boston" DJ Panel**
**Jerry "The Duke of Madness" Goodwin**
**Tami Heide**
**Carter Alan**
**Maxanne Satori**

But it was the bottom of the poster that made Nick's blood run cold and hot at the same time:

**Registration Deadline: March 19 — This Friday**
**Audition Recordings Due: March 21**

"Holy shit," someone said. "WBCN? The actual Rock of Boston?"

"Five hundred dollars," another voice added. "That's like... real money."

"Longview Studios recorded Aerosmith's first demo," a third voice chimed in, and Nick recognized Tommy Brooks, a drummer who understood that rhythm was everything. "This is legit."

Nick stared at the poster, his mind already racing. WBCN wasn't just any radio station — it was *the* station, the one that had broken Aerosmith, that had made The Cars, that understood that Boston rock and roll was its own particular breed of magic. Getting judged by their DJs was like getting blessed by the rock and roll gods themselves.

"You entering, Johnson?" Tommy asked.

"Yeah," Nick said, his voice steadier than his hands. "Yeah, we are."

"With who? Murphy and those guys?"

"My band," Nick said firmly. "We've been practicing for months."

What he didn't say was that they'd been practicing for this without knowing it was coming, the way you practice for a war during peacetime, keeping your skills sharp for a battle you hope never comes but know probably will.

The crowd was growing now, the energy palpable. Nick could already see the divisions forming — the serious musicians calculating their chances, the dreamers imagining fame, the realists counting the prize money.

Then the crowd parted like the Red Sea, and Jackie Marsh walked through.

He looked different in the harsh fluorescent light of the school hallway than he had in Nick's garage. More polished, despite the casual clothes. More Phillips Academy than Lincoln-Sudbury, even though he was standing in the same hallway as everyone else. The Queen belt buckle caught the light like a challenge.

Jackie studied the poster with the intensity of someone reading a declaration of war, which, Nick supposed, it was.

"Eight bands," Jackie said quietly, but his voice carried. "That's not many."

"It's enough," Nick said, and everyone turned to look at them, these two former friends now standing on opposite sides of the bulletin board like gunfighters at high noon.

"You entering?" Jackie asked, though they both knew the answer.

"With my band," Nick confirmed. "You?"

"I'll need to put one together," Jackie said, and there was something in his voice — not arrogance exactly, but confidence. The kind that came from years of being told you were exceptional and finally believing it.

"Good luck with that," Nick said, turning to leave.

"Nick," Jackie called after him, and Nick stopped but didn't turn. "May the best band win."

"We will," Nick said, and walked away, feeling the eyes of the entire hallway on him.

Behind him, he heard the whispers starting:
"Is that Jackie Marsh? I thought he was at Phillips Academy."
"Johnson and Marsh used to be tight, right?"
"This is going to be epic."

### Chapter 14: The Recruitment

*March 16, 1979 — Marsh Family Home — Evening*

Jackie sat at the piano in his family's living room, the same piano where he'd learned scales at age five, where he'd first played "Bohemian Rhapsody" at eleven, where he'd spent countless hours practicing for Phillips Academy auditions that had taken him away from everything that mattered. The keys felt familiar under his fingers, but the music that came out was different now — more complex, tinged with a sadness that classical training couldn't quite mask.

"You're playing angry," a voice said from the doorway.

Elizabeth stood there, thirteen now but tall for her age, all angles and attitude that their parents didn't quite know how to handle. She held her violin case like some kids held skateboards — casually, but ready for action.

"I'm not angry," Jackie said, but his fingers betrayed him, striking a discordant chord.

"Right," Elizabeth said, entering the room uninvited. "That's why you've been playing the same three bars of 'Somebody to Love' for an hour, each time more aggressively than the last."

She was right, of course. Elizabeth had always been able to read him like sheet music — every note, every rest, every dynamic marking.

"I saw the poster," she said, sitting beside him on the piano bench. "Battle of the Bands. You're entering."

It wasn't a question.

"I don't have a band," Jackie said.

"So make one." Elizabeth pulled out her violin, already tuned because she was always prepared. "Start with what you've got."

"Which is what? A washed-up Phillips Academy dropout who can't even—"

The slap of her bow against his shoulder was gentle but effective.

"Stop it," she said firmly. "You're Jackie Marsh. You made a middle school gymnasium cry with your voice. You got into Phillips Academy on talent, not just Dad's connections, whatever he tells his golf buddies. And you came back because music matters more to you than their approval."

"You don't understand—"

"I understand that Nick Johnson was in the music store yesterday buying new strings and looking like he was preparing for war," Elizabeth interrupted. "I understand that you two idiots are about to let pride destroy what's left of whatever you had. And I understand that if you're going to do this — and you are going to do this — you're going to need a band that can match whatever he's putting together."

Jackie looked at his little sister, really looked at her. When had she gotten so wise? When had she stopped being the kid who followed him around begging to learn guitar?

"You want in?" he asked.

"I want you to stop moping and start planning," she said. "Queen didn't become Queen by feeling sorry for themselves. They became Queen by being so undeniably good that no one could ignore them."

She was right. Again.

"Okay," Jackie said, turning back to the piano with new determination. "We'll need a drummer. A bassist. Maybe another guitar."

"I know a drummer," Elizabeth said. "Ace Callahan. Freshman, but he's been playing since he was six. Jazz trained but loves rock. Bit of a prodigy, actually."

"A freshman?" Jackie was skeptical.

"You were in eighth grade when you won that talent show," Elizabeth reminded him. "Age doesn't matter. Talent does."

"What about bass?"

"There's this junior, Max Wesslen. Quiet kid, but I've heard him in the practice rooms. He's... thorough. Methodical. The kind of bassist who makes everyone else sound better without anyone noticing he's doing it."

Jackie was impressed despite himself. "You've thought about this."

"I've been thinking about it since you came home from Phillips looking like someone had died," Elizabeth said quietly. "Music is the only time you look alive anymore, Jackie. So yeah, I've been thinking about how to keep you alive."

The words hit harder than any slap could have.

"Liz..."

"Don't," she said, standing up. "Just... fix this. Fix yourself. And if you happen to show Nick Johnson that he's not the only one who understands rock and roll, that's just a bonus."

She headed for the door, then turned back. "By the way, I've been working on an arrangement. 'Bohemian Rhapsody' but with strings. Not classical strings — rock strings. Like what the Electric Light Orchestra does, but... more."

"Play it," Jackie said.

Elizabeth smiled — the first real smile he'd seen from her in months. She set up her violin, took a breath, and began.

What came out was extraordinary. It was "Bohemian Rhapsody" but reimagined, the violin taking Mercury's vocal lines and making them soar even higher, adding harmonies that didn't exist in the original but felt like they'd always belonged there. It was classical training in service of rock and roll, and it was perfect.

"Holy shit, Liz," Jackie breathed.

"Language," she said primly, but she was grinning. "So? Are we doing this?"

Jackie stood up from the piano bench, and for the first time since coming home from Phillips, he felt something like hope.

"We're doing this," he said. "We're going to create something they've never seen before. Not just a Queen tribute — something that honors Queen but transcends it."

"Now you're talking," Elizabeth said. "I'll call Ace tonight. You work on Max. We should rehearse tomorrow, get a feel for the chemistry before we commit to the battle."

"Since when did you become the band manager?"

"Since my older brother needed someone to save him from himself," she said, and left before he could respond.

Jackie sat back down at the piano, but this time when he played "Somebody to Love," it wasn't angry. It was determined. He could hear it now — how the violin would weave through the vocals, how a jazz-trained drummer could add complexity to the rhythm, how a methodical bassist could ground the whole thing while he and Elizabeth soared above it.

They wouldn't just enter the Battle of the Bands. They would transform it.

### Chapter 15: The Other Side of Town

*Same evening — Johnson Garage*

While Jackie was planning his revolution with classical precision, Nick was in the garage with his band, the air thick with cigarette smoke and determination. Stephen Murphy, Mark Pianka, and David Cassel had been playing together for two years now, long enough to read each other's musical minds, to know when Stephen was about to walk up the bass or when Mark was going to throw in an unexpected keyboard flourish.

"So it's official," Nick said, passing around beers he'd lifted from his mom's fridge — Narragansett, because even their beer was working class. "Battle of the Bands. March 15th. WBCN judges."

"Shit," David said from behind his kit, a Frankenstein creation of different drum pieces he'd assembled over the years. "The actual Rock of Boston?"

"The actual Rock," Nick confirmed. "This is our shot, boys. Our chance to show them that real rock and roll doesn't come from fancy schools or classical training. It comes from here." He thumped his chest, then pointed at the garage walls covered in Aerosmith posters, ticket stubs, and setlists from every dive bar show they'd managed to sneak into.

"What's the competition look like?" Mark asked, his fingers unconsciously playing scales on his Farfisa organ, a vintage beast he'd rescued from a church demolition.

"Don't know yet," Nick lied. He knew exactly who one competitor would be, could feel Jackie's presence like a change in barometric pressure. "But it doesn't matter. We've got something they don't."

"Which is?" Stephen asked, though his grin suggested he already knew the answer.

"We've got hunger," Nick said. "We've got the need. They're playing for glory. We're playing for survival."

It was dramatic, maybe overly so, but it was also true. The five hundred dollars might be pocket change to kids from Jackie's neighborhood, but to Nick it was two months of his mom not working doubles. The recording session wasn't just an opportunity; it was a potential lifeline to something bigger.

"What are we playing?" David asked. "Got to be perfect. Three songs, probably, for the audition recording."

"Dream On," Nick said immediately. "It's our strongest. Shows range, emotion, and technical skill."

"Sweet Emotion for the second," Stephen suggested. "It's a crowd pleaser, and my bass line is tight as hell."

"Walk This Way to close," Mark added. "It's the song that puts asses in motion. Judges can't ignore it."

Nick nodded, but his mind was elsewhere, thinking about a garage session four years ago when two boys had discovered that "Walk This Way" and "Bohemian Rhapsody" could dance together, that Queen and Aerosmith weren't opposites but partners in the same rock and roll revolution.

"Earth to Nick," Stephen said, snapping his fingers. "You still with us?"

"Yeah," Nick said, shaking off the memory. "Let's run through the set. But first..." He grabbed his guitar, the Stratocaster that had become an extension of his body. "I've been working on something. An arrangement that's pure Aerosmith but... more. Fuller. Like we're not just copying them but channeling them, you know?"

He played the opening to "Dream On," but it was different — the guitar line was more complex, weaving in and out of spaces the original had left empty. Stephen's bass came in, intuitive as breathing, finding the pocket Nick had created. Mark's organ added color, not overwhelming but enhancing. David's drums were subtle at first, then building, creating dynamics that didn't exist in the original.

When Nick started singing, his voice had changed too. Four years of practice, of throwing himself against the walls of his limitations until they cracked, had given him range he didn't have before. He could hit the high notes now, not with Jackie's effortless clarity but with a roughness that felt more honest, more earned.

"Half my life's in books' written pages," he sang, and the words felt autobiographical even though they weren't his. "Lived and learned from fools and from sages..."

The garage transformed around them. This was the magic Nick remembered but different — not the innocent discovery of two kids but the intentional creation of young men who'd learned that music was both salvation and damnation, both the question and the answer.

When they finished, the silence was holy.

"Jesus Christ," Stephen breathed. "When did we get good? Like, really good?"

"We've always been good," Nick said. "We just needed a reason to prove it."

"This Jackie thing," David said carefully. "It's a reason?"

Nick's jaw tightened. They all knew the history, had heard the stories, had seen Nick's face whenever someone mentioned Jackie Marsh's name.

"It's not about him," Nick said, but even he didn't believe it. "It's about us. About showing everyone — the Rampers, the college-bound kids, the ones who think we're just townies with guitars — that we matter. That this matters."

"Right," Mark said diplomatically. "Not about Jackie at all."

"Look," Nick said, setting down his guitar. "I know what you're thinking. But this is bigger than ancient history. This is about the future. Our future. We win this, we get studio time. We get WBCN's attention. We get a shot at being more than guys who play covers in dive bars until we're forty and bitter."

"We're with you," Stephen said, speaking for all of them. "Whatever the reason, we're with you. But Nick? Don't let the past poison the present. We're too good for that now."

Nick nodded, but his mind was already racing ahead, imagining the battle, the stage, the moment when he and Jackie would inevitably face each other with guitars instead of words, with music instead of memories.

"From the top," he said. "And this time, let's make it perfect."

They played until midnight, until Nick's fingers bled and Stephen's were blistered and David's arms ached and Mark's back screamed from hunching over the organ. They played like their lives depended on it, because in a way, they did.

Three miles away, in a basement that had been professionally soundproofed, Jackie and his newly formed band were doing the same thing, chasing perfection with the dedication of the desperate and the skill of the trained.

The battle lines weren't just drawn. They were carved in stone and amplified through Marshall stacks.

### Chapter 16: The News Spreads

*March 17, 1979 — Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School*

By Tuesday, the Battle of the Bands had become the only thing anyone could talk about. The poster by the music room had been joined by others throughout the school, and the registration sheet was filling up fast.

The cafeteria at lunch was a buzz of speculation and side-taking that would have made the Montagues and Capulets proud. The social geography of the school, usually divided by the invisible lines of class and clique, was reorganizing itself around musical allegiances.

At the elevated section known as "The Ramp," where the college-bound kids held court, Jackie sat with his newly assembled band. Elizabeth had been right about Ace Callahan — the freshman looked like he was twelve but played drums like he'd been born with sticks in his hands. His parents were both jazz musicians who'd named him after Ace Cannon, and he'd inherited their rhythm and then some.

Max Wesslen was exactly as advertised — quiet to the point of invisibility, but when he picked up a bass, he became the foundation everyone else could build on. He was the kind of musician who would never be a star but without whom stars couldn't shine.

"We need a name," Ace said, spinning a drumstick with casual expertise. "Can't just be 'Jackie Marsh and band.'"

"Crown Jewels," Elizabeth suggested, and everyone turned to look at her. "What? It's Queen-inspired but not derivative. And it suggests we're the best without actually saying it."

"It's pretentious," Max said quietly, his first contribution to the conversation.

"It's confident," Jackie countered. "And in this case, confidence is going to matter."

From across the cafeteria, Nick watched them with his own crew. The Lincoln boys — that's what everyone called them, since Stephen, Mark, and David all came from the smaller, scrappier town that fed into the regional high school. They'd claimed a table near the windows, strategic position that let them see everything while maintaining their outsider status.

"Crown Jewels," Stephen read from the registration sheet someone had lifted from the music room. "Jesus, could they be more pompous?"

"We need a name too," David pointed out.

"Street Fighting Men," Nick said immediately. "It's Stones-inspired, but it's also who we are. We're not pretty. We're not polished. We're fighters."

"I like it," Mark said. "It's got attitude."

"It's got balls," Stephen corrected. "Which is what we're going to need going up against Mr. Phillips Academy and his classical training."

Nick bristled. "We're not going up against him. We're going up against everyone. He's just another band."

The looks his bandmates exchanged suggested they didn't believe him any more than he believed himself.

The registration sheet, when it was posted at the end of the day, told the story of a school divided:

1. Crown Jewels (Queen tribute) — Jackie Marsh, lead vocals/guitar/piano
2. Street Fighting Men (Aerosmith tribute) — Nick Johnson, lead vocals/guitar  
3. Detroit Rock City (KISS tribute) — Tom Patterson and the drama club kids
4. Sharp Dressed Men (ZZ Top tribute) — The auto shop crew
5. Truckin' (Grateful Dead tribute) — The art wing hippies
6. Zeppelin IV (Led Zeppelin tribute) — Senior metalheads
7. Boston Pride (Boston tribute) — The Wayland transfers

And at the bottom, added just before the deadline:

8. Rumours (Fleetwood Mac tribute) — Concord High School

"Who the hell is Rumours?" someone asked, looking at the unfamiliar name.

"Concord kids," someone else answered. "Don't know them."

"Fleetwood Mac?" a third voice scoffed. "That's not even real rock."

But Mr. Wentworth, standing in his office doorway, looked at that last name with interest. He'd gotten a call from his counterpart at Concord, Elizabeth Davidson, who'd said simply, "We're sending someone special. Don't underestimate them because they're quiet."

Quiet. In a battle of bands where volume often equated to victory, quiet was either a liability or a secret weapon.

### Chapter 17: The Preparation Begins

*March 18-20, 1979*

The three days between registration and recording submissions were a blur of rehearsal, rivalry, and rising tension that infected the entire school like a fever.

Crown Jewels rehearsed in the Marsh basement, a space that had been designed for music. Dr. Marsh had installed acoustic panels when Jackie was younger, back when he'd supported his son's musical ambitions as long as they remained properly contained within acceptable boundaries. Now those same panels contained something Dr. Marsh didn't quite understand — rock and roll with classical precision, rebellion with perfect pitch.

"From the top," Jackie commanded, his leadership style a mixture of his father's professorial authority and something harder, hungrier. "Ace, the tempo on 'Somebody to Love' needs to be exact. It's 76 BPM in the verse, 79 in the chorus."

"You want me to play with a metronome in my head?" Ace asked, but he was grinning. He loved the challenge.

"I want you to BE the metronome," Jackie said. "Elizabeth, your violin entrance needs to soar. You're not accompanying; you're declaring war."

Elizabeth nodded, her violin already under her chin. At thirteen, she had the focus of someone twice her age and the ambition of someone who'd grown up in the shadow of an older brother's talent and was determined to step into her own light.

They launched into "Somebody to Love," and it was transformative. Jackie's voice had matured at Phillips, trained by coaches who didn't know they were preparing him for rock and roll. He could hit Mercury's notes now, all of them, but with his own color, his own pain. Elizabeth's violin didn't just accompany; it conversed with his voice, sometimes supporting, sometimes challenging, always elevating. Ace's drums were precise but not mechanical, jazz-trained hands finding the heartbeat inside Queen's theatrical arrangements. Max's bass was the spine everything else hung on, invisible until you tried to imagine the song without it.

"Can anybody find me somebody to love?" Jackie sang, and the question felt personal, aimed across town at a garage where another band was preparing for war.

Meanwhile, in that garage, Street Fighting Men was taking a different approach. Where Crown Jewels sought perfection, Street Fighting Men sought truth. Where Jackie commanded, Nick collaborated.

"What if we dirty it up more?" Stephen suggested during a break. "I mean, we're good, but we're too clean. Aerosmith recorded in basements and beer-soaked studios. We're playing like we're afraid to make a mistake."

"He's right," David said from behind his kit. "We need more danger. More edge."

Nick thought about it, then smiled. "You know what? You're absolutely right. Forget perfect. Let's be real."

He cranked his amp until it was just below the feedback threshold, that sweet spot where the sound was dirty but controlled. Stephen detuned his bass slightly, just enough to add grit. Mark found the honky-tonk setting on his organ, the one that sounded like it had been through a bar fight. David loosened his snare, making it snap like a whip crack.

"Walk This Way," Nick called out. "And this time, play it like you're trying to get laid."

The transformation was immediate. The song became dangerous, sexual, alive in a way that would have made Steven Tyler proud. Nick's voice was rough, commanding, channeling every ounce of working-class defiance into three minutes of pure rock and roll.

"Backstage boogie set your pants on fire," he growled, and the garage walls seemed to sweat with the heat of it.

But it was in the quiet moments between songs that the real preparation happened. Jackie, sitting at his piano at 2 AM, playing the opening notes of "Dream On" and remembering a garage where that song had first shown him what harmony could be. Nick, lying on his bed, staring at the ceiling, hearing "Bohemian Rhapsody" in his head and hating that he could still sing every word.

### Chapter 18: The Recording

*March 21, 1979*

The school had set up a makeshift recording studio in the band room, using equipment borrowed from Berklee College of Music where Mr. Wentworth had connections. Each band had one hour to record three songs, no overdubs, no second chances. It was as close to live as you could get while still having the protection of walls between you and your audience.

Crown Jewels went first, by virtue of alphabetical order.

They set up with military precision, Elizabeth tuning her violin to the piano, Ace adjusting his kit with mathematical accuracy, Max silently ensuring every connection was secure. Jackie stood at the center, guitar in hand, looking every inch the rock star he was training himself to become.

"Rolling," Mr. Wentworth said from behind the board.

They opened with "Bohemian Rhapsody," the six-minute epic that most bands wouldn't dare attempt. But Crown Jewels didn't just attempt it; they reimagined it. Elizabeth's violin took the opera section and made it soar even higher. Ace's jazz training let him navigate the complex time signatures like he was strolling through a park. Max held it all together with a bass line that was both faithful to the original and somehow completely his own.

But it was Jackie's voice that stole the show. Four years of training, four years of pain, four years of discovering who he was when he wasn't trying to be what everyone expected — it all poured out in six minutes of controlled chaos.

"Mama, just killed a man," he sang, and every word carried weight.

When they finished, the control room was silent. Mr. Wentworth had been teaching for twenty years. He'd heard hundreds of student bands. He'd never heard anything quite like this.

"Next song?" he asked, his voice carefully neutral.

Street Fighting Men went fourth, after sitting through Detroit Rock City's enthusiastic but sloppy KISS tribute and Sharp Dressed Men's surprisingly competent ZZ Top covers.

Where Crown Jewels had set up with precision, Street Fighting Men set up with controlled chaos. Cables everywhere, amps at dangerous angles, David's kit looking like it might collapse at any moment but somehow holding together through sheer will.

"Ready when you are," Nick said, and there was something in his voice — not nervousness, but anticipation. Like a fighter before a bout, knowing pain was coming but welcoming it.

They opened with "Dream On," and from the first note, it was clear they weren't trying to be Aerosmith. They were trying to be themselves, filtered through Aerosmith's lens. Nick's voice had smoke in it, whiskey and cigarettes and late nights in bars where dreams went to die or be born. Stephen's bass walked through the song like a drunk man who knew exactly where he was going. Mark's organ added colors that didn't exist in the original — purples and blacks and the deep red of dried blood. David's drums were a heartbeat, irregular but vital.

"Dream until your dream comes true," Nick screamed, and it wasn't pretty, but it was real.

The other bands were good in their own ways. Detroit Rock City brought theatricality, Sharp Dressed Men brought humor, Truckin' brought surprisingly complex harmonies, Zeppelin IV brought volume that threatened the windows, Boston Pride brought hometown pride.

But everyone was waiting for the mystery.

Rumours arrived at 6 PM, when the sun was setting and the school was mostly empty except for the janitors and the dedicated musicians. Three people walked in — two girls and a guy, all from Concord, all unknown.

The girl who seemed to be the leader was small, with dirty blonde hair and eyes that looked like they'd seen things. She introduced herself simply as "Patty." The other girl, Robin, was taller, darker, with fingers that looked made for guitar strings. The guy, whose name no one caught, set up a minimal drum kit with the efficiency of someone who'd done this a thousand times.

"We're only doing one song," Patty said to Mr. Wentworth. "Is that okay?"

"Rules say three," Mr. Wentworth replied, but something in the girl's voice made him curious.

"One is all we need," she said, and there was no arrogance in it, just certainty.

They set up quickly, quietly. No amps cranked to eleven, no theatrical positioning. Patty sat at the piano, Robin picked up an acoustic guitar, the drummer settled behind his kit like he was sitting down to dinner.

"Rolling," Mr. Wentworth said.

Patty's fingers found the piano keys, and the opening notes of "Dreams" filled the room. But this wasn't the Fleetwood Mac version, not exactly. It was stripped down, rebuilt, reimagined as something more intimate but somehow bigger.

"Now here you go again, you say you want your freedom," Patty sang, and her voice was nothing like Stevie Nicks'. It was younger but older, cleaner but dirtier, simpler but more complex. It was the voice of someone who'd learned that music wasn't about perfection or power but about truth.

Robin's guitar wove through the piano like thread through fabric, binding everything together. The drums were subtle, suggesting rhythm rather than demanding it. And when they hit the chorus, when Patty's voice opened up and Robin added harmony that seemed to come from somewhere beyond the room, everyone in the control booth stopped breathing.

"Thunder only happens when it's raining," Patty sang, and outside, as if on cue, the first drops of spring rain began to fall.

They played for four minutes and thirteen seconds. When they finished, the room was silent except for the rain against the windows.

"Thank you," Patty said simply, and they began packing up.

"Wait," Mr. Wentworth said. "Don't you want to record more? You have time—"

"No," Patty said, smiling a smile that suggested she knew something no one else did. "One is enough."

They left as quietly as they'd come, leaving behind only the echo of a song that had somehow made everyone else's recordings sound like what they were — tributes, not originals, copies instead of creations.

### Chapter 19: The Listening Party

*March 22, 1979 — After School*

Mr. Wentworth had decided, in what he would later recognize as either genius or madness, to hold a listening party for all the bands. "You should hear your competition," he'd said. "Learn from each other. This isn't just about winning; it's about growing as musicians."

The music room was packed. Crown Jewels sat on one side, Street Fighting Men on the other, like the Sharks and Jets if they'd traded switchblades for Stratocasters. The other bands filled in the spaces between, everyone trying to look casual while vibrating with competitive energy.

Mr. Wentworth played the recordings in order.

Crown Jewels' "Bohemian Rhapsody" filled the room, and even Nick had to admit it was impressive. The technical skill was undeniable, the arrangement innovative, the performance polished to a shine that hurt to look at directly. Jackie's voice soared through the speakers, and Nick heard something in it he didn't remember from their garage days — pain, maybe, or longing, or the sound of someone trying to prove something to someone who wasn't in the room.

When Street Fighting Men's "Dream On" came through the speakers, Jackie found himself leaning forward despite himself. Nick's voice had changed, developed layers and textures that hadn't been there at twelve. The rawness was intentional now, controlled chaos rather than chaotic control. The band's chemistry was obvious — they played like a unit, like brothers, like what Jackie and Nick could have been if the world had been different.

Detroit Rock City got laughs with their KISS tribute, complete with Tom Patterson's attempts at Paul Stanley's stage patter captured on tape. Sharp Dressed Men surprised everyone with their competence. Truckin' revealed unexpected sophistication in their harmony work. Zeppelin IV was loud enough to make the windows rattle even on tape. Boston Pride was hometown proud and technically proficient.

Then came Rumours.

The room changed when Patty's voice came through the speakers. Conversations stopped. People who'd been checking their phones looked up. Even the janitor, pushing his cart past the open door, stopped to listen.

It wasn't that the song was technically perfect, though it was close. It wasn't that the arrangement was innovative, though it was. It was something else, something harder to define. It was the sound of musicians who weren't trying to be anyone but themselves, who weren't competing so much as simply existing in their truth.

When it ended, the room was quiet for a long moment.

"Well," Mr. Wentworth said finally. "I think we have a competition."

Jackie and Nick looked at each other across the room, and for the first time since Jackie's return, there was something besides anger and hurt between them. It was recognition — recognition that they weren't the only ones with something to prove, that the battle they'd been preparing for might not be the war that mattered.

"They're good," Stephen said quietly to Nick.

"Yeah," Nick agreed, his eyes still on Jackie. "They all are."

"May the best band win," Jackie said, loud enough for everyone to hear, but his eyes were on Nick when he said it.

"Yeah," Nick replied. "May the best band win."

### Chapter 20: The Town Takes Sides

*March 23-29, 1979*

What had started as a school competition quickly became a town event. The Sudbury Citizen ran a front-page story: "Local Bands to Battle for Recording Contract." The hardware store put up a "Street Fighting Men" banner. The bookstore countered with Crown Jewels propaganda. Even St. Michael's Catholic Church, where Patty occasionally played for services, got into the act with a subtle "Rumours" poster in their community bulletin board.

At Friendly's restaurant, where Mary Johnson worked, the staff had created a betting pool. The odds were constantly changing based on the latest rumors (no pun intended) about rehearsals, secret weapons, and surprise songs.

"My money's on your boy," Donna, another waitress, told Mary as they prepped for the lunch rush.

"He's not competing against anyone but himself," Mary said diplomatically, but she'd put twenty dollars on Street Fighting Men when no one was looking.

At Boston University, Dr. Marsh found himself in the unusual position of having colleagues ask about his son's "rock band." The words came out of his mouth like foreign objects.

"It's just a hobby," he told Professor Williams from the music department. "Something to get out of his system before Cornell."

"Cornell?" Williams asked. "I heard he was deferring."

Dr. Marsh's smile tightened. "Temporarily. This... music thing... it's just a phase."

But at home, he'd started listening to Queen albums when no one was around, trying to understand what his son heard in the theatrical bombast. One night, Helen caught him air-conducting to "We Are the Champions."

"Richard?"

He'd turned, embarrassed. "I was just... trying to understand."

"Maybe," Helen suggested gently, "you should try supporting instead."

Meanwhile, the bands themselves were deep in final preparations.

Crown Jewels had added choreography to their set, Elizabeth and Jackie moving in synchronized patterns that turned performance into theater. They'd rehearsed until every note, every movement, every breath was perfect.

Street Fighting Men had gone the opposite direction, stripping their sound down even more, making it rawer, more dangerous. They'd practiced in dive bars, playing for drunk crowds who didn't care who they were, only that they could make them feel something.

And Rumours? No one knew what Rumours was doing. They'd disappeared back to Concord after the recording session, leaving only mystery in their wake.

### Chapter 21: The Night Before

*March 14, 1979 — 11 PM*

The night before the battle, Jackie couldn't sleep. He stood at his bedroom window, guitar in hand, playing softly so as not to wake his parents. The song was "Love of My Life," the Queen ballad that had always reminded him of friendship lost.

Three miles away, Nick sat in the garage, the lights off, playing "No More No More" in the darkness. The Aerosmith deep cut had never been a hit, but something about its melancholy fit his mood.

Neither knew the other was awake, playing songs of loss and longing into the darkness. But if you'd been standing in the right spot, exactly between their houses, you might have heard both songs drifting through the night air, creating an accidental harmony that suggested what could have been.

At her house in Concord, Patty Lally sat at her piano, not playing but thinking. She'd heard about the rivalry between Crown Jewels and Street Fighting Men, about the history between Jackie and Nick. She'd recognized something in their recordings — two halves of something that had been broken, each trying to be whole on their own.

"Thunder only happens when it's raining," she sang softly to herself. "Players only love you when they're playing."

Tomorrow, eight bands would compete for a prize that seemed important but might not be the real victory. Tomorrow, friendships would be tested, rivalries would culminate, and music would decide things that words never could.

But tonight, in the quiet before the storm, three musicians in three different places were united by the same truth — that music was never really about winning or losing. It was about finding the part of yourself that only existed in song, the harmony that made you whole, the rhythm that kept you alive when everything else failed.

Tomorrow would bring battle.

But tonight, there was only music, drifting through the darkness like prayers from teenagers who didn't know they were praying, who only knew that the music was the most honest thing about them, the truest truth they had to offer.

### Chapter 22: Dawn of the Battle

*March 15, 1979 — 6 AM*

The morning of the Battle of the Bands arrived with the kind of crisp clarity that New England sometimes gifts its residents in early spring — the air clean and sharp, the sky a blue so perfect it looked fake, like a backdrop painted by someone who'd only heard descriptions of sky but had never seen one.

Jackie woke to the sound of Elizabeth already practicing, her violin singing scales through the walls. He lay in bed for a moment, feeling the weight of the day settling on him like armor he wasn't sure he wanted to wear. Somewhere across town, he knew Nick was waking up too, probably to the sound of his mother getting ready for her morning shift, the coffee maker gurgling its working-class symphony.

They'd been friends once. Best friends. The kind of friends who could communicate in looks and half-words, who could create magic from nothing but two guitars and the belief that music mattered more than anything else. Now they were generals of opposing armies, ready to wage war with instruments instead of weapons, with harmony instead of harm.

But wars have casualties, even musical ones, and Jackie wondered who would be left standing when the smoke cleared.

In the Johnson house, Nick stood in the shower, letting the hot water run over his shoulders, washing away doubt and fear and the dream he'd had where he and Jackie were twelve again, sitting in the garage, discovering that their voices fit together like pieces of a puzzle nobody knew was broken until they solved it.

"You okay, baby?" his mother called through the door.

"Yeah, Mom. Just thinking."

"Don't think too hard. Sometimes the heart knows things the head can't figure out."

Mary Johnson had a way of cutting through complexity with simple truth, a skill developed from years of not having time for anything but what mattered most.

By 7 AM, both bands were in motion. Crown Jewels met at the Marsh house for breakfast — a formal affair with fresh fruit and whole grain toast and organic juice that cost more than some families spent on dinner. They ate in relative silence, the weight of expectation sitting at the table like an uninvited guest.

"Remember," Dr. Marsh said, having taken the day off work for the first time in two years, "this is just a learning experience. Win or lose—"

"We're not going to lose," Jackie interrupted, and there was something in his voice that made his father stop mid-sentence.

"Of course not," Dr. Marsh said, but he looked at his son with something approaching concern. When had Jackie become this person — hard-edged, determined, slightly dangerous?

Street Fighting Men met at the garage, sharing gas station coffee and donuts that Stephen had bought with his last five dollars.

"Breakfast of champions," he said, raising a chocolate glazed in mock toast.

"Damn straight," Nick agreed, but his eyes were distant, already at the battlefield, already facing down the ghosts of what had been and what might have been.

By noon, the school was buzzing. Classes were technically in session, but nobody was paying attention. Teachers had given up trying to compete with the anticipation, and several had actually incorporated the battle into their lessons. Mr. Hammond in English was teaching "The Hero's Journey" using the competing bands as examples. Ms. Rodriguez in History was discussing the cultural importance of rock and roll in American society.

But in the music wing, where the real preparation was happening, the air was electric with possibility and dread in equal measure.
