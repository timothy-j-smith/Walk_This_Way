ACT III: FREQUENCY 

Chapter 17A: Monday Morning (V1)

"Seven forty-three AM," I tell my dwindling audience, my voice hoarse from hours of testimony but steady because some stories demand completion regardless of the toll they exact on their tellers. "Monday morning at Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School, and eight hundred teenagers walked through the same doors they'd walked through for months, but everything and nothing had changed."
The building looked identical—same Glass Corridors reeking of cigarette smoke, same elevated Ramp in the cafeteria, same territorial boundaries that had defined social interaction since September. But something invisible had shifted in the 60 hours since three teenagers proved that authentic vulnerability could cross any boundary if people were brave enough to risk it.
Bob Wentworth arrived at 7:15 AM carrying coffee and cautious optimism, wondering if the musical democracy he'd witnessed Friday night could survive the return of academic hierarchy that Monday morning imposed like gravity on teenage social experimentation. The music room looked the same—same piano with the stuck B-flat key, same guitar amplifiers held together with electrical tape and prayer, same bulletin board that had accidentally started a war.
But at 7:47 AM, something unprecedented happened. Sarah Morrison walked into the music room.
Not for any class—she was enrolled in Advanced Placement everything except music, her schedule calibrated for Ivy League admission rather than artistic exploration. Not for any official reason—her extracurriculars were student council, debate team, and activities that demonstrated leadership potential to admissions committees who measured character in resume bullets.
She walked into the music room because something in Friday night's recognition had convinced her that musical curiosity might be more important than musical credentials, that understanding what moved her was more valuable than maintaining what elevated her above others who were supposedly less worthy of elevation.
"Mr. Wentworth?" she said, her voice carrying uncertainty rather than the command authority that usually defined her interactions with faculty. "I was wondering... would it be possible to audit jazz band? I mean, I don't play an instrument properly, but I'd like to understand what I heard Friday night."
Wentworth looked at this girl who'd spent three years representing everything he'd assumed was wrong with privileged teenagers—achievement oriented, credential focused, more interested in performing excellence than experiencing it—and saw something that hadn't been visible before: genuine curiosity about music as expression rather than accomplishment.
"You could start with piano," he suggested carefully, understanding that whatever was happening required delicate handling. "Basic chord progressions, rhythm section fundamentals. The Steinway might be intimidating, but we have uprights that are more forgiving for beginners."
"I'd like that," Sarah said, and for the first time in three years of knowing her, Wentworth heard her voice carry want rather than should, preference rather than obligation, authentic interest rather than strategic advancement.
At 8:15 AM, the first period bell scattered students toward their assigned classrooms, but the usual migration patterns were subtly different. Kids who'd spent Friday night talking across class lines were maintaining eye contact in the hallways, acknowledging each other with nods or brief conversations that would have been unthinkable the previous week.
Tommy Morrison walked past the Ramp section of the cafeteria—elevated territory he'd been trained to avoid since freshman year—and Sarah Morrison looked up from her AP Government homework to wave. Not the careful acknowledgment that polite teenagers offer when social obligation requires recognition, but genuine greeting between two people who'd discovered they could have actual conversations when they stopped performing their assigned identities.
"How's the frequency this morning?" Sarah asked, referencing Patty's song in a way that created inclusion rather than exclusion, connection rather than demonstration of superior cultural memory.
"Little less static than usual," Tommy replied, adjusting his leather jacket with movements that felt less like armor and more like practical protection against New England March weather. "You?"
"Trying to tune in instead of just broadcasting," Sarah said, and both of them understood they were speaking a new language, using Friday night's vocabulary to navigate Monday morning's reality.
Lisa Henderson found herself in Chemistry lab partnered with Danny Kowalski, and instead of the careful politeness that usually characterized cross-class academic interaction, they discovered their Friday night conversation about music and mechanics could expand into broader territory about how things worked—molecular structures, engine timing, the rhythmic precision that made both chemistry and automotive repair forms of practical art.
"Been thinking about what you said," Danny told her while they calibrated their equipment for the day's experiment. "About static being interference that you can filter out. Makes me wonder what else we're filtering without knowing it."
Lisa, whose academic training was finally serving curiosity rather than performance, nodded with recognition that Friday night's insights were translating into practical understanding. "Like social assumptions. Like the idea that mechanical skills and academic skills are mutually exclusive instead of complementary."
But the most significant change was happening in spaces where teenagers gather when they think adults aren't observing their natural social behavior.
The Glass Corridors at 8:47 AM, between first and second periods, usually hosted exclusively Rats—working-class kids who'd claimed cigarette-fogged territory as their own, who'd developed protective territoriality against incursion from social superiors who might judge their choices, mock their limitations, or remind them of their assigned place in the school's hierarchy.
This Monday morning, the exclusive territory had become permeable. Not invaded—invasion implied conquest and replacement. Integrated, with kids from other quadrants who'd been granted access based on Friday night's recognition that authentic conversation could happen anywhere people were brave enough to risk it.
Nick Johnson stood near his usual spot by the windows, but instead of the protective isolation that had defined his relationship to academic success, he was surrounded by an unlikely group: Rampers who wanted to understand how he'd transformed working-class struggle into artistic triumph, Regs who were questioning their own assumptions about authentic expression versus academic preparation, Rollers who recognized his mechanical understanding of how sound worked.
"What I don't understand," said Jennifer Walsh, whose BMW dealer father had provided comfortable distance from economic anxiety but who was discovering that comfort could be its own form of limitation, "is how you made that song sound like hope instead of complaint. Like, when you sang about your mom serving coffee to people discussing your prospects, it didn't sound bitter. It sounded... determined."
Nick, who'd spent three years being reduced to symbol of authentic struggle by people who'd never struggled authentically, found himself explaining artistic choices rather than defending social positions. "Because bitterness doesn't change anything. Determination might. The song wasn't about being angry that the system exists—it was about proving the system doesn't define what's possible."
Meanwhile, Jackie Marsh was experiencing his own form of social recalibration. Crown Jewels had gathered in the music room during their free period, but instead of the protective isolation that classical training sometimes imposed between serious musicians and casual appreciators, they found themselves joined by kids who wanted to understand technical excellence not as intimidation but as one form of authentic expression among many.
"That original song you played," said Amy Kowalski, drummer whose mechanical precision had served garage band authenticity, "about pressure and expectations. How do you write something that personal and then perform it in front of eight hundred people?"
Jackie, who'd spent three years being reduced to symbol of privileged excellence by people who'd never examined what privilege actually cost, discovered he could discuss artistic vulnerability rather than defend social advantages. "Same way you play drums for Nick, I think. You focus on serving the music instead of protecting yourself. You risk connection instead of maintaining superiority."
Dr. Patricia Hoffman, the guidance counselor who specialized in college placement and crisis intervention in equal measure, arrived at her office at 8:52 AM to find something unprecedented: her appointment schedule included requests from students asking about "musical career paths" and "colleges with strong programs in both academics and performance arts."
These weren't her usual college-bound elite seeking strategic advantages or her typical crisis cases requiring immediate intervention. These were kids from all four quadrants who'd been affected by Friday night's recognition that authentic expression might be more important than assigned expectations, that following genuine interest might produce better outcomes than performing predetermined roles.
"Mrs. Hoffman?" Sarah Morrison stood in her doorway at 9:15 AM, but her usual command presence had been replaced by something more vulnerable, more genuinely curious. "I wanted to ask about schools that combine academic rigor with opportunities for musical exploration. Not necessarily performance-focused, but programs that understand music as part of broader education rather than separate from it."
Hoffman looked at this girl who'd spent three years calibrating every choice for maximum admissions advantage, whose transcript read like strategic planning rather than intellectual development, and saw something that hadn't been visible before: authentic interest in growth rather than strategic interest in achievement.
"Are you thinking about changing your major focus?" Hoffman asked carefully, understanding that whatever was happening required delicate handling.
"I'm thinking about becoming more than just my major focus," Sarah replied, her voice carrying want rather than should for the first time in their professional relationship. "Friday night taught me that excellence might be more interesting when it serves connection rather than separation."
By 10:30 AM, the transformation was visible to anyone who understood teenage social dynamics. The careful territorial boundaries that had defined Lincoln-Sudbury's hierarchy since September were still present but had become more permeable, more flexible, more conscious of their own artificial nature.
The cafeteria during lunch remained segregated—Rampers on their elevated territory, Rats in the Glass Corridors, Regs scattered throughout middle ground, Rollers near the parking lot exits. But the segregation felt chosen rather than imposed, maintained by preference rather than enforced by exclusion.
Kids were sitting in their familiar groups but acknowledging other groups with recognition rather than dismissal, understanding that different preferences for social organization didn't require mutual hostility, that choosing your tribe didn't necessitate rejecting everyone else's right to choose theirs.
Grace Johnson arrived at Friendly's for her Monday lunch shift at 11:47 AM, still processing Saturday night's explosion and Friday night's validation in approximately equal measure. The restaurant looked identical—same red vinyl booths, same black and white checkered floor, same counter where she'd served coffee to people discussing her child's limited prospects for twenty-three years.
But something had shifted in the 72 hours since she'd finally said what everyone knew but nobody wanted to acknowledge: that dignity didn't depend on job descriptions, that working-class dreams deserved respect rather than charity, that service work required skills and provided value that economic systems were designed to undervalue rather than recognize.
Dr. Richard Marsh entered Friendly's at 12:23 PM for his usual Monday faculty lunch meeting, but instead of claiming his regular booth with the assumption that his academic authority entitled him to premium seating and differential service, he approached Grace Johnson at the counter with something that looked like respect rather than polite tolerance.
"Mrs. Johnson," he said, his voice carrying acknowledgment rather than condescension, "I wanted to thank you for Friday night. For your son's performance, and for helping me understand what authentic excellence looks like when it's earned rather than inherited."
Grace looked at this university professor who'd spent years writing papers about working-class families without talking to working-class families, understanding that recognition could cross class lines when it was based on honest acknowledgment rather than academic analysis.
"And I wanted to thank you for raising a boy who could admit that privilege comes with its own kind of pressure," Grace replied, her voice carrying dignity that had been earned through twenty-three years of maintaining professional courtesy while being treated as invisible infrastructure. "Takes courage to risk connection instead of maintaining superiority."
They stood there for a moment—service worker and academic, single mother and university professor, representatives of incompatible worldviews who'd discovered that mutual respect was possible when it was based on recognition of shared humanity rather than comparative advantage.
By 2:47 PM, as the school day concluded and students scattered toward their after-school obligations, three things had become clear:
First, that Friday night's recognition hadn't dissolved the social boundaries that defined Lincoln-Sudbury's hierarchy, but it had made those boundaries more conscious, more chosen, more aware of their own artificial nature.
Second, that the conversations crossing class lines weren't transforming the fundamental economic realities that created different opportunities for different kids, but they were changing how those differences were understood—as circumstances to be navigated rather than identities to be defended.
Third, that the frequency Patty had sung about wasn't just metaphor but practical possibility that required daily choice rather than momentary recognition, sustained effort rather than temporary inspiration.
Monday morning had tested whether Friday night's democracy could survive the return of institutional hierarchy. The answer was complex: the hierarchy remained, but it had become more permeable, more conscious of its own constructed nature, more aware that excellence could emerge from any circumstances when it was supported rather than limited by community recognition.
The real test would come in the weeks and months that followed, when the immediate memory of shared vulnerability faded and the machinery of social expectation reasserted its gravitational pull toward familiar patterns of division, exclusion, and the comfortable assumption that some voices mattered more than others.
But for one Monday morning in March 1979, Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School had glimpsed what American education could be if it chose connection over competition, if it recognized that diverse forms of excellence could coexist without requiring anyone to fail, if it understood that the frequency where all voices mattered was more valuable than any system that required some voices to be silenced so others could be heard.

Chapter 17: The Day After (V1)

"March seventeenth, 1979," I announce, my voice carrying the weight of someone who's spent twelve hours excavating the truth from forty-five years of accumulated memory and mythology. "Forty-eight hours after the Battle of the Bands, and Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School was discovering that transformation is messier than recognition, that change requires sustained choice rather than momentary inspiration."

The weekend had provided buffer time for processing, for family conversations around dinner tables, for telephone discussions between parents who'd witnessed something they couldn't quite categorize using familiar frameworks for understanding teenage behavior and community dynamics. But Tuesday morning brought the real test: whether Friday night's democracy could survive two full days of institutional reinforcement of the hierarchies it had temporarily suspended.

I was there that Tuesday, having driven out from Boston to observe the aftermath of what I'd begun to understand was the most significant musical event I'd witnessed in twenty years of covering everything from Berklee College showcases to Boston Symphony debuts. Not because the performances themselves were technically superior to professional music—though they were remarkable for high school students—but because they'd accomplished something that professional music rarely achieved: they'd changed how a community understood itself.

The parking lot at 7:42 AM looked identical to any other Tuesday morning—same territorial clustering of transportation that reflected economic geography, same careful social boundaries that prevented interaction across class lines, same institutional momentum that processed eight hundred teenagers through educational routines without examining what those routines were designed to produce.

But something invisible had shifted. The boundaries were still present but more consciously maintained, more aware of their own constructed nature. The clustering remained but included subtle acknowledgments across territorial lines. The institutional momentum continued but carried conversations that hadn't existed the previous week.

Sarah Morrison arrived in her father's BMW, but instead of the automatic migration toward the elevated Ramp territory that usually defined her morning routine, she detoured through the Glass Corridors where Tommy Morrison was sharing a cigarette with the usual collection of Rats who'd claimed smoking-fogged space as their own.

"How's the frequency this morning?" she asked, using Friday night's vocabulary to navigate Tuesday morning's reality.

"Clearer than yesterday," Tommy replied, exhaling smoke that mixed with March morning air to create visibility patterns that looked like democracy in action. "You still thinking about learning piano?"

"Signed up for jazz band audit yesterday," Sarah said, her voice carrying genuine excitement rather than strategic achievement orientation. "Wentworth says I can start with basic chord progressions, work up to harmony theory if I can handle the commitment."

The conversation lasted ninety-seven seconds—I timed it later, when I was trying to understand how social change actually works at the granular level, how individual choices accumulate into community transformation. But what struck me most wasn't the duration but the naturalness, the lack of self-consciousness about cross-class interaction that would have been unthinkable the previous week.

Lisa Henderson found herself walking to first period with Danny Kowalski, their Friday night conversation about music and mechanics expanding into broader territory about how different kinds of intelligence could recognize each other instead of competing for scarce validation. Danny's understanding of engine timing and Lisa's grasp of molecular chemistry were discovering common ground in their appreciation for precision, rhythm, the way complex systems required all components to function harmoniously or the entire mechanism failed.

"Been thinking about what you said about filtering static," Danny told her as they navigated hallways that suddenly felt less like territorial battlegrounds and more like shared space where different kinds of excellence could coexist. "Makes me wonder about college. Like, maybe automotive engineering and academic achievement aren't mutually exclusive."

Lisa, whose academic training was finally serving curiosity rather than performance, nodded with recognition that Friday night's insights were producing practical applications. "There are programs that combine mechanical engineering with liberal arts requirements. Schools that understand different kinds of intelligence as complementary rather than competitive."

But the most significant development was happening in spaces where institutional authority tried to process what had occurred, how to contain it within existing frameworks for understanding student behavior and educational outcomes.

Principal Brad Sargent called an emergency faculty meeting at 7:15 AM, ostensibly to discuss "the musical event and its impact on school climate." The conference room filled with teachers, guidance counselors, and administrators who were trying to understand whether Friday night represented successful educational programming or potential disruption to carefully maintained social order.

"I'm receiving reports," Sargent announced to the assembled faculty, his voice carrying the administrative anxiety that accompanies unexpected deviation from predicted outcomes, "that students are exhibiting unusual social interaction patterns. Cross-demographic conversations, altered seating arrangements in common areas, requests for academic programming that doesn't align with established placement protocols."

Bob Wentworth, who'd spent the weekend processing his role in accidentally catalyzing community transformation, looked around the conference room at colleagues who seemed more concerned about maintaining institutional control than understanding what the music had actually accomplished.

"The students are talking to each other," Wentworth said, his voice carrying the wonder of someone who'd witnessed democracy in action. "Kids who haven't spoken in three years are discovering they have common interests, shared concerns, mutual respect. Isn't that what education is supposed to produce?"

Dr. Patricia Hoffman, guidance counselor who specialized in crisis intervention and college placement, consulted the notes she'd compiled over thirty-six hours of unprecedented student appointments. "I've had seventeen requests since Monday morning for information about colleges with strong music programs. Not from students with existing musical backgrounds, but from kids across all demographic categories who seem to have discovered artistic interests they'd never explored."

"Is this problematic?" asked Margaret Stevens, the English teacher who prided herself on maintaining classroom discipline through rigid adherence to established protocols. "Should we be encouraging students to deviate from their established academic trajectories based on a single evening's... entertainment?"

The pause after "entertainment" contained everything: the institutional inability to recognize that what had occurred transcended entertainment, the administrative preference for containment over exploration, the educational system's investment in predictable outcomes rather than authentic development.

"It wasn't entertainment," Wentworth said quietly, his voice carrying twenty-three years of teaching experience and the recognition that he'd witnessed something that justified his entire career in public education. "It was three teenagers teaching eight hundred people something about authenticity, vulnerability, and the possibility that excellence doesn't require anyone else to fail. If that's not educational, I don't know what is."

Meanwhile, in the cafeteria during first lunch period, the social geography that had defined Lincoln-Sudbury's hierarchy since September was undergoing subtle but significant reconfiguration. The elevated Ramp still hosted Rampers, the Glass Corridors still attracted Rats, the middle territories still accommodated Regs, the parking lot proximity still drew Rollers.

But the exclusivity had been replaced by permeability. The elevated territory acknowledged kids from other quadrants with recognition rather than dismissal. The Glass Corridors included conversations with academic achievers who'd previously been considered enemy combatants in the war between intellectual and authentic credentials. The middle ground became actual middle ground where different perspectives could coexist without requiring synthesis or conquest.

Nick Johnson sat with the Lincoln boys—Stephen, David, and Mark—processing victory in ways that transcended competition, understanding that his two-point margin mattered less than the mutual respect that all three performers had earned from each other and the community that had witnessed their courage.

"What happens now?" Stephen asked, his bass guitar permanently propped against the cafeteria wall since Friday night because carrying it had become statement rather than transportation. "I mean, we won something, but what do we do with winning?"

Nick looked around the cafeteria at conversations crossing class lines, at recognition happening between kids who'd spent three years categorizing each other as incompatible worldview representatives, at the possibility that Friday night's frequency might be more than temporary reception.
"Maybe winning wasn't about beating Jackie and Patty," Nick said, his voice carrying understanding that was still forming. "Maybe it was about proving that working-class authenticity and privileged excellence could recognize each other instead of competing for scarce validation. Maybe what we won was the right to be heard without requiring anyone else to be silenced."

Jackie Marsh sat with Crown Jewels at their usual table near the music room entrance, but instead of the protective isolation that classical training sometimes imposed between serious musicians and casual appreciators, they found themselves included in larger conversations about what music could accomplish when it served connection rather than separation.

"That handshake with Nick," Elizabeth said to her brother, her thirteen-year-old perspective providing adult insight, "that meant more than any trophy could mean. You proved that privileged kids could respect authentic struggle, and he proved that working-class kids could acknowledge technical excellence. You both won something that couldn't be scored."

Jackie nodded with recognition that defeat had taught him more about authenticity than victory could have taught him about superiority. "I keep thinking about what Patty said, about the frequency still existing. Maybe that's what we're all trying to find—the place where different kinds of excellence can coexist without requiring anyone to be diminished."

At 12:47 PM, something unprecedented occurred: Nick Johnson walked over to Jackie Marsh's table.

Not for any official reason—they weren't in the same classes, didn't share extracurricular obligations, had maintained careful distance since their friendship died at the Hop Brook bridge two years earlier. He walked over because something in Friday night's recognition had convinced him that mutual respect might be more important than territorial loyalty, that acknowledging former friendship might be more valuable than protecting current safety.

They didn't discuss their shared history or attempt to analyze what had gone wrong with their friendship. They talked about music, about what they'd learned from Friday night, about the possibility that different approaches to excellence could enhance rather than threaten each other.

"Your original song," Nick said quietly, his voice audible only to Jackie and the Crown Jewels members who were pretending not to eavesdrop. "About pressure and expectations. That was the bravest thing I've heard in my life. Made me understand that rich kids and working-class kids might face different pressures but the same need to be seen as human instead of symbol."

"And your 'Hop Brook Blues,'" Jackie replied, his voice carrying genuine admiration rather than competitive assessment. "That wasn't just autobiography. That was community testimony. You carried your mother's dignity, your neighborhood's dreams, everyone who'd been told their aspirations were unrealistic. That's not just musical skill—that's moral courage."

The conversation ended when the bell signaled transition to fifth period, but its impact extended far beyond individual reconciliation. Eight hundred teenagers had witnessed two former best friends choose recognition over resentment, understanding over division, the possibility that bridges could be rebuilt instead of permanently burned.

By 3:15 PM, as the school day concluded and students scattered toward their after-school obligations, certain patterns had emerged that would define how Friday night's recognition translated into sustained change rather than temporary inspiration:

First, that the social boundaries remained but had become more permeable, more conscious of their own constructed nature, more aware that exclusivity required constant maintenance rather than natural enforcement.

Second, that cross-class conversations were continuing but required individual courage rather than institutional support, personal risk rather than systemic encouragement, daily choice rather than policy mandate.

Third, that the frequency Patty had sung about was available but not automatic, required tuning rather than passive reception, demanded sustained effort rather than momentary inspiration.

"I propose," said Margaret Stevens, "that we implement structured activities designed to channel this musical interest into appropriate educational outcomes. Auditions for spring drama production, additional choir sections, music theory classes that align with existing curricular frameworks."

"You want to institutionalize what happened Friday night?" Wentworth asked, his voice carrying the recognition that some forms of transformation couldn't be contained within existing frameworks without losing their essential character. "You want to turn authentic expression into another form of academic achievement, musical democracy into another opportunity for competitive ranking?"

The silence that followed contained everything: the gap between educational theory and authentic learning, between institutional control and student development, between the machinery of school administration and the possibility that education could serve human flourishing rather than simply processing students through predetermined categories.

"What I want," Wentworth continued, his voice getting stronger as he found language for something he'd been feeling but couldn't express, "is to support what's already happening. Kids talking to each other across class lines. Students discovering interests they didn't know they had. Community members recognizing that excellence can emerge from any circumstances when it's encouraged rather than limited by assumptions about who deserves support."

At 4:23 PM, I interviewed Grace Johnson during her dinner break at Friendly's, trying to understand how Friday night's recognition was affecting the daily reality of working-class families who'd been validated by their children's courage but still faced the practical challenges that validation didn't automatically resolve.

"People look at me different now," Grace told me, her voice carrying surprise rather than satisfaction. "Saturday I had three customers apologize for things they'd said about Nick, about working-class kids in general. Sunday at church, Dr. Hoffman asked if I thought Nick might be interested in college music programs with financial aid."

She paused, stirring her coffee with movements that suggested deep thought rather than nervous habit.

"But here's the thing," Grace continued, her voice carrying the practical wisdom that comes from navigating economic reality without institutional support. "Recognition doesn't pay rent. Respect doesn't fix my car when it breaks down. What Nick accomplished Friday night was beautiful and important, but it doesn't change the fact that we still live in different worlds, face different challenges, have different resources for pursuing dreams."

"Maybe the conversation," Grace replied, her voice carrying hope that was tempered by experience but not defeated by realism. "Maybe the assumptions. Maybe the recognition that working-class dreams deserve support rather than charity, encouragement rather than sympathy. Nick proved something Friday night about what's possible. Whether the community chooses to build on that possibility or return to comfortable assumptions... that's still being decided."

As I drove back to Boston that evening, processing what I'd witnessed and trying to find language that could capture transformation in progress rather than transformation completed, three things became clear:

First, that Friday night's recognition had created permission rather than policy, possibility rather than mandate, the opportunity for change rather than the guarantee of change.

Second, that sustaining the frequency Patty had sung about required daily choice by hundreds of individuals rather than institutional modification by administrative decree, personal courage rather than systemic support.

Third, that the real test would come not in the immediate aftermath of shared recognition but in the months and years that followed, when the memory of authentic vulnerability faded and the machinery of social expectation reasserted its gravitational pull toward familiar patterns of division.

Tuesday, March seventeenth, 1979, had proven that transformation was possible but not inevitable, that recognition could cross class lines but required individual courage to sustain, that the frequency where all voices mattered was always available but never automatic.

The Battle of the Bands had ended, but the battle for whether its recognition could become lasting change was just beginning. And that battle would be fought not on stages or in parking lots but in cafeterias and classrooms, in daily interactions between kids who'd been reminded that their assigned identities were choices rather than destinies, in the space between what America could be and what it usually chose to remain.
