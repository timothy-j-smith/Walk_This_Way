## CHAPTER 33: THE FINAL SEPARATION

### **The Garage**

Three hours after the Battle of the Bands ended, Nick Johnson sat alone in Dan's garage, staring at the pawn shop guitar that had carried him through the most important performance of his life. The garage was dark except for the single bulb that hung from the ceiling, casting shadows that made everything feel larger, more significant, more worthy of the moment that was about to happen.

He'd been sitting there for an hour, trying to make sense of what had happened at the Battle of the Bands. Walking the Line had performed with raw authenticity, had channeled Dan's memory into every note of "Walk This Way," had even written an original song about the town's divisions that made half the audience cry and the other half uncomfortable.

But Patty had won. Not him, not Jackie. Patty, who'd reminded everyone that music wasn't about competition but about connection, about finding the resonance where all voices mattered.

And now Nick was sitting in an empty garage at midnight, wondering what the hell he'd been fighting for.

"Hey."

Nick looked up to see Jackie Marsh standing in the doorway, carrying his Martin D-28 with the casual care of someone who'd never had to worry about replacing what he broke. But something was different tonight. The easy confidence that had carried him through two years of high school performances was cracking under pressure that had nothing to do with music and everything to do with representing advantages he'd never asked for but couldn't escape.

"Hey," Nick replied, not sure what else to say.

Jackie walked into the garage and sat down across from Nick, guitar in hand, and for a moment, they were just two seventeen-year-old boys who'd forgotten they were supposed to be enemies.

### **The Recognition**

"I've been thinking about what Patty said," Jackie said finally, his voice carrying the particular vulnerability that comes from admitting you've been wrong about something important.

"About the frequency?" Nick asked, looking up from his guitar.

"About the frequency," Jackie confirmed. "About how we used to harmonize, now we harmonize. About how we used to create, now we compete."

Nick was silent, understanding that Jackie was trying to say something important, something that transcended the divisions that had separated them.

"I think we've been fighting for the wrong reasons," Jackie continued, his voice growing stronger. "I think we've been trying to prove that our approach to music was superior, when maybe the point was never about superiority. Maybe the point was always about connection."

Nick looked at Jackie, understanding that they'd both been wrong about what the other represented, that they'd both been wrong about what the divisions meant, that they'd both been wrong about what was possible.

"Different wavelengths of the same truth," Nick said, and the words felt like something Patty might have said.

### **The Reconciliation**

They started playing tentatively, like two people learning to dance again after years apart. Nick began with the opening riff to "Sweet Emotion," and Jackie joined in with the melody from "Bohemian Rhapsody," and somehow, impossibly, it worked.

The garage filled with the sound of two voices finding each other again, of raw power meeting technical precision, of two boys who'd forgotten they were supposed to be enemies remembering they'd once been friends.

They played for an hour, rediscovering the songs they'd once shared, the melodies they'd once created together, the resonance where all differences dissolved into something beautiful. They played "Sweet Emotion" and "Bohemian Rhapsody," "Walk This Way" and "Killer Queen," and somehow, impossibly, it all worked.

When they finished, when the last note had faded into the silence of the garage, they sat there looking at each other, two boys who'd just proven that the resonance still existed.

"Remember how we used to argue about Queen versus Aerosmith?" Nick asked, tuning his guitar.

Jackie smiled, tuning his own. "Raw power versus technical precision."

"Different wavelengths of the same truth," Nick said, and the words felt like something Patty might have said.

### **The Phone Call**

And then the phone rang.

Nick picked it up, expecting Grace or Stephen or someone else who'd been at the Battle of the Bands, someone who wanted to talk about what had happened, someone who wanted to understand how a high school talent show had transformed an entire community.

But it wasn't Grace or Stephen. It was Maxanne Sartori.

"Boys," she said, her voice crackling through the phone line like a lifeline thrown across years of silence, "I've been listening to your story all night. I think it's time we made it official."

"Official?" Nick asked, holding the phone between them so Jackie could hear.

"Studio C at WBCN. Right now. I've got the night shift engineer here, and he's willing to stay late for a story that matters. You record that Queen/Aerosmith mashup, and we'll have proof that the resonance still exists."

Nick looked at Jackie, and Jackie looked at Nick, and in that moment, they both understood that this was their last chance to be who they'd once been.

"Okay," Nick said.

### **The Recording Session**

Studio C at WBCN was smaller than Nick had imagined, but it felt like a cathedral. The walls were covered with acoustic foam that made everything sound larger, more significant, more worthy of the moment that was about to happen. The equipment was state-of-the-art, the kind of gear that made bedroom dreams feel like radio reality.

The engineer was a kid named Mike, maybe twenty-five, with the kind of enthusiasm that comes from working at a station that's about to die. He'd set up two microphones, positioned them so Nick and Jackie could face each other while they played, could see each other's eyes while they sang.

"This is it," Maxanne said, settling into the producer's chair. "One take. No overdubs. Just you two, making music the way it was meant to be made."

She paused, looking at the boys with the particular wisdom that comes from watching too many dreams die in translation. "Some music can't survive being made professional. Some wavelengths get lost when you try to clean them up. But tonight, we're going to try anyway."

Nick and Jackie sat down across from each other, guitars in hand, and for a moment, they were just two thirteen-year-old boys who'd discovered that music could be magic.

"Rolling," Mike said, and the red light came on.

They played the Queen/Aerosmith mashup—"Sweet Emotion" meeting "Bohemian Rhapsody," raw power harmonizing with technical precision, two voices finding the resonance where all differences dissolved into something beautiful.

And it was perfect.

Not technically perfect—there were moments when Nick's voice cracked with emotion, when Jackie's fingers stumbled over chords he'd played a thousand times. But it was emotionally perfect, authentically perfect, the kind of performance that happens when two people stop trying to be better than each other and start trying to be better together.

When they finished, when the last note had faded into the silence of the studio, they sat there looking at each other, two boys who'd just proven that the resonance still existed.

"Play it back," Maxanne said quietly.

Mike hit play, and the sound that came through the speakers was like hearing your own voice for the first time. Nick and Jackie listened to themselves, to the way their voices had blended, to the way they'd found each other again in the space between the notes.

### **The Final Fracture**

But then the door opened, and Dr. Richard Marsh walked into Studio C, his face a study in controlled anger, his presence carrying the particular authority of someone who'd been planning this intervention for months.

"Jackie," he said, and his voice carried the particular authority of someone who'd been planning this intervention for months. "It's time to go home."

Behind him, Grace Johnson appeared, looking tired and worried and proud all at once. "Nick, honey, it's late. We should go."

The spell was broken. The resonance was jammed by the static of adult reality.

Nick and Jackie looked at each other across the studio, and in that moment, they both understood that this had been their last chance to be who they'd once been.

"Dad," Jackie started, but Dr. Marsh was already moving, already claiming his son with the kind of possessive love that destroys everything it touches.

"Come on, son. Your mother's waiting in the car."

Grace took Nick's arm, gentle but insistent. "Let's go, honey. Your father would have been proud of what you did tonight."

They walked out of Studio C in separate directions, two boys who'd just proven that the resonance still existed, but who'd also proven that sometimes knowing something exists isn't enough to save it.

### **The Demo Tape**

Maxanne sat alone in Studio C, holding the master tape in her hands like it was evidence of a crime that had never been committed.

"What do we do with this?" Mike asked, looking at the tape with the particular reverence that comes from witnessing something that shouldn't have been possible.

Maxanne stared at the tape, at the proof that two boys had once found the resonance where all voices mattered, and she made a decision that would haunt her for thirty years.

"We preserve it," she said quietly. "We preserve it because someone needs to remember that this was possible."

She slipped the tape into her purse, and for the next three decades, it would sit in her personal collection, a secret she carried with her like a promise she'd made to two boys who'd never had the chance to keep it.
