# CHAPTER 22: NICK'S REHEARSAL - THE GARAGE SESSIONS

---

May 8th, 1979, and the garage on Dutton Road had become something else entirely. What had once been Dan Johnson's workshop—pegboard lined with tools, workbench cluttered with half-finished projects, boots waiting by the door—was now filled with the sound of four teenagers trying to figure out how to be a band.

Tuesday evening, forty-eight hours before the Battle of the Bands, Nick stood in that garage surrounded by his father's memory and his mother's hope and the weight of something he couldn't quite name, trying to remember why he'd started playing music in the first place.

The Lincoln boys were there—Stephen, David, and Mark, the inseparable trio who'd found in Nick's friendship the fourth corner of a musical square they hadn't known they were building. They'd been practicing for six hours, running through their set list until their hands ached and their voices grew hoarse, transforming Aerosmith covers into something that felt like theirs.

But something was different tonight. The music that had always felt like freedom was starting to feel like something else—something heavier, something that carried more than just notes and chords.

## **THE PRACTICE SESSION**

"What if we're not good enough?" Mark asked during a break, his usually steady hands shaking as he adjusted the settings on his borrowed keyboard. 

"We're not going to lose," Nick said, but his voice carried the hollow certainty of someone trying to convince himself more than his bandmates.

"But what if we do?" Stephen pressed, his bass guitar leaning against his shoulder. "What happens then?"

Nick didn't have an answer for that. Couldn't admit that he'd been asking himself the same question every night since Grace's speech at Friendly's, lying awake wondering what failure would mean when it wasn't just about a high school competition anymore.

Since that day, everything had changed. Kids he'd never met were stopping him in the hallway, telling him they were rooting for him. Parents at the grocery store were asking Grace how he was doing, like he was carrying something bigger than just his own dreams.

And with that change had come a responsibility he wasn't sure he was ready for.

## **THE GUITAR AND THE MEMORY**

Nick picked up his guitar—the same $25 acoustic his father had bought him three years ago, now worn smooth by countless hours of practice. The strings felt familiar under his callused fingers, the wood warm against his chest. This was where he'd learned to play, where he'd discovered that music could be more than entertainment.

Dan Johnson had been dead for three years, but his presence still filled the garage. His tools still hung on the pegboard, organized with the kind of precision that came from a lifetime of making do with what you had. His work boots still sat by the door, waiting for a man who would never come home from another construction site.

"Your dad would be proud," Grace had said that morning, watching Nick load his guitar into the car for another day of school. "He always said you had the gift."

But Nick wondered what Dan would think about all this. The Battle of the Bands had stopped being just about music. It had become something else entirely.

"Let's run through 'Dream On' again," he said, his voice carrying the determination of someone who'd decided to see this through.

## **THE SONGS**

The opening riff filled the garage, raw and powerful. Stephen's bass locked into the rhythm, David's drums providing the heartbeat, Mark's keyboard adding texture and depth. Together, they created something that was more than the sum of its parts—the sound of four kids who'd learned to make do with what they had and make it work.

"Dream On" had always been Nick's favorite Aerosmith song. There was something about Tyler's raw vocals, something about the way the song built from quiet desperation to full-throated defiance, that spoke to something deep inside him.

But now, with forty-eight hours left before the Battle of the Bands, the song felt different. It wasn't just about dreaming anymore. It was about proving something—though Nick wasn't entirely sure what.

"Perfect," Stephen said when they finished, his voice carrying the satisfaction of musicians who'd found their groove. "That's the best we've ever played it."

"Let's try 'Sweet Emotion,'" Nick said, pulling out the sheet music they'd been working on. "The bass line on this one is killer."

The bass line on "Sweet Emotion" was indeed killer—that iconic intro that Perry had made famous, the kind of riff that made you want to move even if you didn't know why. Stephen locked into it perfectly, his fingers finding the groove with the kind of precision that came from hours of practice on borrowed equipment.

They worked through their set list: "Dream On," "Sweet Emotion," "Walk This Way," "Toys in the Attic." Each song felt different now, heavier somehow, like they were carrying more than just notes and chords.

"Walk This Way" was their closer, the song that gave everything its name. Nick had always loved the way it built, the way it started with that simple guitar line and grew into something that felt unstoppable.

When they finished, the garage was silent except for the sound of their breathing, heavy with the weight of what they'd just played.

"That's it," Nick said, his voice rough with emotion. "That's what we've got."

## **GRACE LISTENING**

Outside, Grace Johnson stood in the driveway, listening to her son's music drift through the garage walls. She'd heard about the speech she'd given at Friendly's, about how it had changed things in ways she hadn't expected.

She'd never meant for it to happen. She'd just been tired of hearing her son dismissed by people who'd never had to choose between paying the electric bill and buying groceries. She'd just been tired of the condescending conversations about "those kids from Lincoln" as if geography determined character.

But now everything was different. Kids were stopping her at the grocery store, asking how Nick was doing. Parents were calling the house, wanting to know if they could help. It was like her son had become something bigger than just a teenager who liked to play guitar.

She leaned against the side of the house, listening to the sound of four boys trying to figure out how to be a band, and wondered if she'd made the right choice when she'd stood up to Dr. Patterson at Friendly's. She'd been tired of being condescended to, tired of hearing her son dismissed by people who thought they knew better.

But now Nick was carrying something she wasn't sure he was ready for.

## **THE FINAL PRACTICE**

Inside the garage, Nick practiced until his hands ached, until his voice grew hoarse, until the Lincoln boys finally went home to their own nervous preparations, leaving him alone with Dan's tools and Grace's hope and the weight of something he couldn't quite name.

The Battle of the Bands was twenty-four hours away, and Nick Johnson was discovering that the hardest part of competition wasn't playing better than your opponent. It was playing well enough to justify the faith that other people had invested in your success.

He sat alone in the garage, surrounded by his father's memory and his mother's expectations, and wondered what Dan would think about all this. The Battle of the Bands had stopped being just about music. It had become something else entirely.

The guitar felt familiar in his hands, the wood warm against his chest, the strings smooth under his callused fingers. This was where he'd learned to play, where he'd discovered that music could be more than entertainment.

But now it felt different. Heavier somehow. Like it was carrying more than just notes and chords.

The Battle of the Bands wasn't just about music anymore. It was about everything that had gone wrong between him and Jackie, everything that had gone wrong in Sudbury, everything that had gone wrong in America. And Nick was about to find out if he was strong enough to carry it all.
