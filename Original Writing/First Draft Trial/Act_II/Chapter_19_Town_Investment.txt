# CHAPTER 19: TOWN INVESTMENT - THE BATTLE OF THE BANDS GOES VIRAL (1979 STYLE)

---

April 15th, 1979, and the Battle of the Bands had stopped being a high school competition. It had become something else entirely—a referendum on class, a test of community values, a chance for Sudbury to prove to itself that it understood what mattered about music, about talent, about the American dream. And, let's be honest, it had also become the most entertaining thing to happen to this town since someone painted the water tower pink during the bicentennial celebration.

By the second week of April, the entire town was invested. And by "invested," I mean they were arguing about it in grocery stores, betting on it in bars, and treating it with the same intensity usually reserved for high school football championships and town council elections about whether to install a fourth stoplight on Main Street.

## **THE BARBER SHOP**

Frank's Barber Shop on Sudbury Road had been cutting hair and hosting debates for three decades. Every Tuesday morning, the same group of retired men gathered to discuss town politics, Red Sox prospects, and whether the new McDonald's on Route 20 was going to put the diner out of business. But for the past three weeks, they'd been arguing about something else entirely.

"Johnson's kid's got real talent," said Al Peterson, who'd worked construction with Dan Johnson before the accident and had the calloused hands to prove it. "That boy can make a guitar sing like it's got a soul. I heard him practicing through the garage door last week, and I swear to God, it sounded like angels were having a jam session."

"Talent don't pay the bills," countered Bob Mitchell, who owned the hardware store and had two kids at Harvard (which he mentioned approximately every three minutes). "Marsh's boy's been trained properly. Classical background, proper technique. That's what wins competitions. You can't just wing it and expect to beat someone who's been studying music theory since he was old enough to hold a violin bow."

The debate had been going on for days, each man taking sides based on their own experiences, their own understanding of what success looked like, their own biases about where talent came from and where it belonged. Frank, the barber, had started a betting pool. The odds were currently 3:2 in favor of Jackie Marsh, but that changed depending on who was in the shop at any given moment.

"Put me down for five bucks on Johnson," said Pete Williams, the retired postmaster. "I like an underdog story."

"Five bucks on Marsh," said Al Peterson, reaching for his wallet. "I like a sure thing."

Frank looked up from cutting old man Henderson's hair. "Gentlemen, we're talking about a high school talent show here, not the World Series."

"Frank," said Bob Mitchell, "you clearly don't understand what's at stake here."

## **THE DINER**

Sudbury Diner on Route 20 had become ground zero for the town's investment in the competition. Every booth held a different conversation about the Battle of the Bands, every table a different perspective on what was at stake. The waitresses had started wearing buttons—some supporting Nick, some supporting Jackie. The cook had declared himself neutral, but everyone knew he was secretly rooting for Nick because his nephew played bass in a garage band.

Grace Johnson sat in her usual booth, nursing a cup of coffee and listening to the conversations around her. She'd become something of a celebrity since her speech at Friendly's, but celebrity felt heavier than anonymity. People stopped by her table to offer encouragement, to share stories about their own musical children, to ask if Nick was nervous about the competition.

"Your boy's got the whole town behind him," said Mary Kowalski, whose son Steve was in Nick's band and had been practicing so much that his parents had started wearing earplugs around the house. "Every working family in Sudbury's rooting for him. I heard Mrs. O'Brien at the library saying she's going to wear her Aerosmith t-shirt to the competition."

But Grace could also hear the other conversations—the ones about how Nick was "just a garage band kid," about how Jackie had "real musical training," about how the competition would prove once and for all whether talent could overcome circumstance. She tried not to let it get to her, but it was hard when your son's future seemed to be hanging in the balance of a high school talent show.

"Grace," said the diner's owner, Tom Martinez, sliding into the booth across from her, "I've been thinking. What if we did a special menu for the Battle of the Bands? You know, 'Nick's Rockin' Burger' and 'Jackie's Classical Club Sandwich'?"

Grace laughed despite herself. "Tom, I think you might be taking this a little too seriously."

"Grace," Tom said seriously, "this town hasn't been this excited about anything since they built the new library. I'm not about to miss out on a marketing opportunity."

## **THE LOCAL PAPER**

The Sudbury Weekly had never covered a high school event with this much enthusiasm. The editor, Tom Harrison, had assigned three reporters to cover the Battle of the Bands, treating it like a major sporting event. The paper had run four front-page stories in the past two weeks, each one more dramatic than the last.

"Battle of the Bands: Class Warfare or Musical Celebration?" read the headline on April 10th. The article quoted parents, teachers, and students, each offering their perspective on what the competition represented. It also included a poll showing that 67% of Sudbury residents planned to attend the competition, which was roughly the same percentage that usually voted in local elections.

"Battle of the Bands: The Stakes Are Higher Than You Think," read the headline on April 12th. This article focused on the WBCN sponsorship and the professional recording session prize, positioning the competition as a potential launching pad for a musical career.

"The Battle of the Bands: A Town Divided?" read the headline on April 14th. This article examined the social divisions that had emerged around the competition, with working-class families supporting Nick and more affluent families supporting Jackie.

"The real question," wrote Harrison in his editorial, "is whether Sudbury can celebrate musical diversity or whether we'll use this competition to reinforce the divisions that already exist in our community. But let's be honest—this is also the most fun we've had as a town in years."

## **THE RADIO SPOTS**

WBCN had been promoting the Battle of the Bands for weeks. Maxanne Sartori's voice could be heard throughout the Boston area, building anticipation for what she called "the most important high school competition in Massachusetts history." The spots had been running every hour, building the competition into something larger than itself.

"This isn't just about two bands," she'd said in her most recent spot. "This is about two different ideas of what American music can be. This is about whether authenticity or excellence matters more. This is about whether we can find harmony in our differences or whether we'll let division destroy what could be beautiful. And it's also about a five-hundred-dollar prize and a professional recording session, which is pretty cool too."

The spots had been so effective that people from neighboring towns had started calling the school, asking if they could attend the competition. The principal's office had been fielding calls from as far away as Framingham and Natick.

## **THE SCHOOL HALLWAYS**

Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School had become a battleground of its own. Students wore their allegiances like team colors—Aerosmith t-shirts for Nick's supporters, Queen buttons for Jackie's fans. The hallways buzzed with anticipation, with arguments about which band was better, which approach to music was more authentic.

"Walking the Line's got real soul," said Sarah Murphy, who'd been Nick's lab partner in chemistry and had started wearing a leather jacket to school. "They understand what music's supposed to feel like. It's not just about hitting the right notes—it's about hitting the right emotions."

"Crown Jewels has technical excellence," countered Jennifer Walsh, who'd been in Jackie's music theory class and had started carrying a Queen album to school every day. "They understand what music's supposed to sound like. It's not just about feeling—it's about precision, about mastery, about taking music to its highest possible level."

The debate had divided the school along lines that had nothing to do with geography and everything to do with philosophy—about what mattered in music, about what mattered in life, about what it meant to be authentic in a world that seemed determined to force people into categories.

The teachers had started taking sides too. Mr. Peterson, the physics teacher, had begun incorporating sound wave examples from rock music into his lectures. Mrs. Williams, the home economics teacher, had started playing Queen albums during cooking demonstrations. Even the janitor had gotten involved, humming Aerosmith songs while mopping the floors.

## **THE PARENTS' MEETINGS**

The PTA had scheduled an emergency meeting to discuss the "tension" that the Battle of the Bands had created in the school. Parents from both sides had been calling the principal's office, concerned about the "hostile environment" that the competition had created.

"This has gotten out of hand," said Dr. Richard Marsh, speaking on behalf of the parents who supported Jackie. "A simple music competition has become a referendum on class and privilege. That's not what education should be about. We're supposed to be teaching our children to work together, not to compete against each other."

"This is exactly what education should be about," countered Grace Johnson, speaking for the parents who supported Nick. "Our kids are learning that talent comes from everywhere, that excellence isn't limited to those who can afford the best training. That's a lesson worth learning. And besides, it's just a talent show. It's supposed to be fun."

The meeting had lasted three hours, with parents arguing about everything from the educational value of rock music to whether the competition was promoting unhealthy competition. In the end, they'd agreed to disagree, but everyone had left with a better understanding of what was at stake.

## **THE BUSINESS COMMUNITY**

Local businesses had started taking sides as well. The music store on Main Street had put up a poster supporting Nick's band in their window. The bookstore had a display of Queen albums and Aerosmith posters. The pizza place was offering a "Battle of the Bands Special" with half-price slices for students wearing band t-shirts.

Even the town council had gotten involved, scheduling a meeting to discuss whether the competition should be moved to a larger venue to accommodate the expected crowd.

"This has become bigger than a high school event," said Mayor Patricia O'Brien, who'd been mayor for twelve years and had never seen the town this excited about anything. "This has become a community event, and we need to treat it as such. I've been getting calls from people in Framingham asking if they can come. Framingham! That's like asking if you can come to someone else's family reunion."

The council had voted unanimously to move the competition to the town hall auditorium, which could seat three hundred people instead of the school's one hundred and fifty. They'd also allocated two hundred dollars from the town budget to cover additional security and cleanup costs.

## **THE FINAL COUNTDOWN**

By April 29th, the day before the Battle of the Bands, the entire town of Sudbury was holding its breath. The competition had stopped being about two bands and had become about everything—about class and privilege, about talent and training, about authenticity and excellence, about whether America could find harmony in its differences or whether division would always win.

It had also become about the most fun anyone in town could remember having in years. People were planning viewing parties, organizing carpool groups, and making signs to wave during the competition. The local radio station had started playing both bands' songs every hour on the hour. The diner was offering a special "Battle of the Bands Breakfast" for anyone who wanted to fuel up before the competition.

The Battle of the Bands was no longer just a high school competition. It was a referendum on the American dream itself. And it was also the most entertaining thing to happen to Sudbury since someone had painted the water tower pink during the bicentennial celebration.

But here's the thing about small towns—when they get invested in something, they get really invested. And Sudbury was about to prove that when a community comes together around music, around talent, around the simple joy of watching two kids try to make something beautiful, magic can happen.

The Battle of the Bands was tomorrow. And the entire town was ready to find out what happened when dreams collided with reality, when talent met opportunity, when two boys who had once been best friends finally got their chance to prove what they were made of.

Spoiler alert: it was going to be complicated. But it was also going to be beautiful.
