# CHAPTER 25: MAXANNE - THE BATTLE OF THE BANDS

---

May 10th, 2024, and I'm sitting in this booth for the last time, watching the clock tick down toward midnight and thinking about what happened forty-five years ago tonight, about what could have been, about what should have been, about the possibility that died on a high school stage in Sudbury, Massachusetts.

The Battle of the Bands was tomorrow night, back in 1979. Twenty-four hours from that moment, Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School hosted what became the most talked-about high school event in Massachusetts history. And I was there, not just as a reporter, but as someone who'd been watching this story unfold for years, someone who understood what was really at stake when two former best friends faced off in front of an entire town that had chosen sides in a war they didn't fully understand.

But looking back now, with forty-five years of hindsight and the knowledge that WBCN would eventually go off the air and that Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh would never make the music they were meant to make together, I can see the tragedy that was unfolding in real time.

Let me tell you what I learned in the days leading up to that night. Let me tell you about the bands that competed, about the preparation that went into that moment, about the weight that everyone involved was carrying.

## **THE COMPETING BANDS**

There were six bands entered in the Battle of the Bands. Six bands, each one representing something different, each one carrying the hopes of people who had invested their dreams in the idea that music could change everything.

**Walking the Line** - Nick Johnson's band. Four kids from the northeast neighborhoods, practicing in a garage on Dutton Road, transforming Aerosmith covers into something that felt like theirs. They'd been working on "Dream On," "Sweet Emotion," "Walk This Way," and "Toys in the Attic." Each song felt different by then, heavier somehow, like they were carrying more than just notes and chords.

I'd heard them practice. I'd listened to the sound bleeding through the garage walls, the raw power of kids who'd learned to make do with what they had and make it work. There was something authentic about their sound, something that spoke to the particular kind of hope that comes from having nothing to lose and everything to prove.

**Crown Jewels** - Jackie Marsh's band. Four kids with professional-grade equipment, rehearsing in a basement studio that looked like something you'd find in a recording studio, not a suburban home. They'd been perfecting "We Will Rock You," "Somebody to Love," and "Bohemian Rhapsody." Every harmony was perfectly arranged, every chord progression mathematically precise, every song executed with the kind of technical excellence that made other bands sound amateur by comparison.

But there was something missing. Something that had nothing to do with technique or equipment or the kind of advantages that money could buy. Something that made their perfect performances feel hollow, technically impressive but emotionally empty.

**Rumours** - Patty Lally's band from Concord. Two kids who had decided to enter a competition they had no business entering, in a town they didn't live in, to remind everyone that music is supposed to bring people together, not tear them apart. They'd been working on Fleetwood Mac covers, "Don't Stop Thinking About Tomorrow" and "Go Your Own Way." There was something refreshing about their approach, something that felt like a reminder of what music is supposed to be about.

**The other three bands** - local kids who'd been swept up in the excitement, who'd formed bands and learned instruments and dreamed of rock stardom. They weren't really in the running, but they were part of the story, part of the energy that had turned this from a simple competition into something that felt like a movement.

## **THE PREPARATION**

I'd been following this story since the beginning, since that poster went up on the bulletin board and turned an entire high school into a battlefield. I'd watched the rehearsals, listened to the arguments, witnessed the way this competition had divided a community along lines that had nothing to do with geography and everything to do with class and privilege and the American dream.

Nick Johnson had been practicing in his father's garage, surrounded by Dan's tools and Grace's hope and the weight of something he couldn't quite name. He'd been working until his hands ached, until his voice grew hoarse, until the Lincoln boys went home to their own nervous preparations, leaving him alone with the responsibility of proving that working-class kids deserved more than sympathy.

Jackie Marsh had been rehearsing in his family's basement studio, surrounded by equipment that cost more than most families' monthly grocery budgets, trying to find the emotional core of songs that had once felt like rebellion but now felt like evidence of the very privilege that Grace Johnson had challenged in her speech at Friendly's.

Patty Lally had been practicing in St. Michael's Church in Concord, trying to figure out how to make the impossible possible, how to enter a competition she had no business entering, how to remind everyone that music is supposed to be about connection, not competition.

## **THE STAKES**

But here's what I learned, what I discovered through weeks of following this story, what I understand now that I didn't understand when that poster first went up on the bulletin board.

This wasn't just about music. This wasn't just about which band could play better or which approach to rock and roll was more authentic. This was about everything that had gone wrong in America, everything that had divided us, everything that had made us forget that we were supposed to be on the same side.

This was about class warfare disguised as musical competition. This was about privilege versus authenticity, technical excellence versus raw emotion, the kind of advantages that money could buy versus the kind of talent that comes from having nothing to lose.

This was about two former best friends who had been turned into symbols of everything that was wrong with our society, who had been asked to carry the weight of other people's dreams and other people's disappointments, who had been forced to compete in a war they never asked to fight.

## **THE WBCN CONNECTION**

WBCN was sponsoring this event. We were providing the prize money, the professional recording session, the legitimacy that came from being associated with "The Rock of Boston." We were turning a high school talent show into something that felt like it mattered, something that could change lives, something that could prove that music still had the power to transcend the divisions that tore us apart.

But I wondered if we were doing the right thing. I wondered if we were helping or hurting, if we were providing an opportunity or creating a spectacle, if we were giving these kids a chance to shine or forcing them to perform in a theater of other people's expectations.

I would be there tomorrow night. I would be reporting on the Battle of the Bands, documenting what happened when music stopped being about connection and started being about competition. I would be watching to see if Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh could find their way back to each other, if they could remember what they were when they were thirteen and fearless and capable of making magic together.

## **THE HOPE**

Because here's what I believed, what I've always believed, what I'll continue to believe even after WBCN goes off the air and this booth becomes just another empty room in an empty building.

Music has the power to heal. Music has the power to bridge divides. Music has the power to remind us that we're all human, that we all have dreams, that we all deserve a chance to make something beautiful together.

Maybe tomorrow night, Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh would remember that. Maybe they would look at each other across that stage and remember what they were when they were thirteen and fearless and capable of making magic together. Maybe they would find their way back to the harmony that once connected them, back to the frequency where Queen meets Aerosmith, back to the place where technical precision dances with raw emotion.

Or maybe they wouldn't. Maybe they would perform their assigned roles in other people's expectations, maybe they would prove that America's talent for division was stronger than their shared history of harmony, maybe they would walk away from that stage as enemies instead of friends.

But either way, I would be there. I would be watching. I would be listening. I would be hoping that music could still do what it was supposed to do—bring people together instead of tearing them apart.

The Battle of the Bands was tomorrow night. And the fate of two former best friends hung in the balance of songs that were never supposed to be weapons in a war they never asked to fight.

Looking back now, with forty-five years of hindsight and the knowledge of what would happen, I can see that we were all watching the death of possibility. We were watching the moment when music stopped being about connection and started being about competition, when two kids who could have changed everything instead became symbols of everything that was wrong with America.

But tomorrow night, in 1979, we still had hope. We still believed that music could heal what politics had broken, that harmony could bridge what class had divided, that two former best friends could find their way back to each other through the power of song.

Tomorrow night, we would find out if we were right. Or if we were just watching the final performance of something that should have been beautiful but had been turned into a weapon in a war nobody had asked for.
