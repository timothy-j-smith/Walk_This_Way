## CHAPTER 7: MAXANNE CHAPTER - THE DIVIDE

"Before I tell you about the battle," I say into the microphone, "I need to tell you about the battlefield. Not just the geography of Sudbury, but the geography of America itself."

I cue up Bruce Springsteen's "Born to Run"—because sometimes you need the Boss to explain what it means to be trapped in a place that's supposed to set you free.

"Boston, 1979," I continue, my voice carrying the weight of someone who's watched a city change around her. "This was still the city that had given birth to the American Revolution, that had fought for independence from tyranny, that had believed in the possibility that all men were created equal."

But something had shifted. The same city that had once been a beacon of democratic possibility had become a place where geography determined destiny, where the neighborhood you were born in determined the neighborhood you'd die in, where the frequency of your voice was determined by the frequency of your bank account.

"The thing about Boston," I tell my dwindling audience, "is that it's a city built on contradictions. We celebrate our revolutionary history while maintaining the same class divisions that the revolution was supposed to destroy. We pride ourselves on our intellectual achievements while ignoring the economic barriers that prevent most people from accessing those achievements."

I take a sip of bourbon, feeling the burn of truth in my throat.

"In 1979, Boston was becoming two cities. There was the Boston of the universities and the hospitals and the financial district—the Boston that attracted the best and brightest from around the world, the Boston that promised opportunity and delivered it to those who could afford the price of admission."

"And then there was the other Boston. The Boston of the working-class neighborhoods, the Boston of the families who'd been here for generations, the Boston of the kids who grew up hearing about the American dream but never seeing it delivered to their doorsteps."

"The Battle of the Bands," I continue, "wasn't just happening in Sudbury. It was happening everywhere. It was the sound of America trying to figure out whether it still believed in the possibility that talent could overcome circumstance, that music could bridge any gap, that two kids from different sides of the same town could find harmony in their differences."

I organize my next sequence of albums with methodical precision, understanding that even memory requires proper sequencing to be heard accurately.

"Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh weren't just two kids competing in a talent show. They were symbols of everything America was wrestling with in 1979. They were the sound of working-class authenticity versus privileged excellence, of garage band rebellion versus conservatory training, of the American dream as it was versus the American dream as it was supposed to be."

The irony was perfect. The same country that had once fought a revolution to establish that all men were created equal had become a place where equality was measured in dollars and cents, where opportunity was distributed according to zip codes, where the frequency of your voice was determined by the frequency of your bank account.

"But here's what gives me hope," I tell my audience, "even in the midst of all that division, even with all the static that America creates to keep people properly separated, there were still moments when the frequency came through. There were still moments when two kids from different worlds could find the same wavelength, could create something beautiful together, could prove that harmony was always possible if people chose connection over the comfort of division."

I check the clock: three hours and five minutes left.

"Coming up," I say, "the moment when those two worlds collided in the most beautiful way possible. The moment when Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh proved that music could bridge any gap, that friendship could transcend any boundary, that America could still find harmony in its differences if it was willing to listen."

But first, I need to tell you about the summer that changed everything. The summer when two thirteen-year-old boys discovered that they could make music together, that they could create something bigger than either of them could create alone, that the frequency that connected them was stronger than all the static that America could throw at them.
