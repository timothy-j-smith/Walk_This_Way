## CHAPTER 2: NICK'S WORLD - THE GARAGE

Maxanne Sartori drove up to the Johnson house in her powder-blue Karmann Ghia, the car looking ridiculous navigating the potholes that the town somehow never fixed on the working-class side of Hop Brook. The vehicle bottomed out twice, sending sparks dancing across the asphalt like tiny fireworks. But she'd heard rumors. A kid who made Steven Tyler sound like he was holding back. A garage where the sound punched you in the chest before you even opened the door.

The Johnson house squatted like it was tired: peeling paint the color of old mustard, sagging gutters that had given up pretending to channel water properly, front steps that listed to port like a ship taking on water slowly, gracefully, inevitably. The lawn had surrendered to New England winter months ago, brown grass poking through dirty snow like stubble on a dead man's face. The whole place looked like it was holding its breath, waiting for something to break.

But from the detached garage came something alive—distorted power chords that made Maxanne's radio-trained ears perk up like a dog hearing its master's key in the lock. The sound hit the cold March air like a physical force, vibrating through the concrete foundation and up through the bones of anyone close enough to feel it.

Grace Johnson answered the door still in her Friendly's uniform, the polyester fabric clinging to her frame like a second skin that never quite fit right. Her name tag read GRACE in that cheerful corporate font that lies about everything, the kind of font that suggests every customer interaction is a joy rather than a necessity that pays the mortgage that's almost always late.

She studied Maxanne—this Boston radio personality in bell-bottoms and oversized sunglasses, standing on her cracked front step like some visitor from a planet where people had money for clothes that didn't come from Jordan Marsh clearance racks. "You're that lady from WBCN. The one who broke Aerosmith."

"Guilty as charged," Maxanne said, studying Grace's face and understanding the weight of a mother's protective instinct. 

"Nick's in the garage." Grace paused, and Maxanne saw something flicker across her face—hope, maybe, or fear that hope was dangerous, expensive, something they couldn't afford to budget for. "Don't fill his head with dreams we can't afford."

The words hung in the air between them like smoke from a cigarette that neither woman was smoking. Maxanne understood the weight of that request, the way working-class mothers learn to protect their children from disappointment by managing their expectations. But she also understood that some dreams refuse to be managed, refuse to stay contained within the boundaries that poverty draws around possibility.

"Mind if I listen?" Maxanne asked.

Grace studied her for another moment, this woman who had discovered bands that filled stadiums, who had the power to make or break careers with a single song selection. "He's been at it since school let out. Won't stop until dinner."

Maxanne nodded and made her way toward the garage, the sound growing stronger with each step. The garage door was open just enough to reveal slivers of what was happening inside—the glow of a single bare bulb, the silhouette of a teenager bent over a guitar, the sacred geometry of four walls and concrete floor that had held more dreams than most concert halls.

Inside the garage, Nick Johnson was mid-solo, his fingers finding notes that shouldn't have been possible on a $25 pawn-shop-miracle guitar held together with electrical tape, determination and pure willpower. But in Nick's hands, it became something else entirely—an extension of his soul, a way to channel everything he couldn't say with words.

Three other kids backed him—all Lincoln boys, Maxanne would learn later, from the even-smaller town that shared a high school with Sudbury but not much else. They stopped when they saw her, that teenage terror of being caught in the act of caring about something, the music dying mid-note like a bird shot out of the sky.

"Don't stop on my account," Maxanne said, settling onto a paint can in the corner. "Play it like you mean it."

They exchanged glances—four working-class boys deciding whether to trust this intruder from the world of actual radio, their eyes darting between her and the door like cornered animals. But Nick made the decision for them, launching back into Aerosmith's "Dream On" with a fury that transformed the garage into something larger, something sacred, something that could hold all their dreams and fears at once.

Here's what Maxanne understood about authenticity that most people miss: it's not something you can learn at Berklee. It's not in the perfect pitch or the precision fingering. It's in the way Nick Johnson's voice cracked when he hit the high notes, not from poor technique but from feeling too much to contain. It's in the way the garage walls seemed to lean in, listening, as if Dan Johnson's ghost was nodding along to his son's pain made music.

"Every time that I look in the mirror," Nick sang, "all these lines on my face getting clearer..."

Seventeen years old, singing about aging. But he understood something about time that most teenagers don't—how fast it steals what you love, how young you can feel old when you're the man of the house before you're even a man.

The tools on the pegboard caught Maxanne's attention, but Nick's eyes were already there. Dan Johnson's tools, arranged by size like a shrine to honest work: hammers that would never swing again, wrenches that would never turn another bolt, a socket set missing the 9/16ths he'd had in his pocket that day. But to Nick, they weren't just tools—they were the weight of responsibility, the reminder that he was now the man of the house at fifteen, the evidence that dreams had to be earned with calloused hands and broken backs. For a moment, the music faltered as he thought about his father, about the way his mother came home from double shifts at Friendly's smelling like fryer oil and other people's happiness, and never once complained about her son spending grocery money on guitar strings.

When they finished, the silence in that garage was holy. The kind of quiet that happens after truth has been spoken, when the last note hangs in the air like incense, when even the dust motes seem to hold their breath in reverence.

"What's your band called?" Maxanne asked.

Nick shrugged, that teenage gesture that protects against caring too much. "Don't have a name yet. We're just... playing."

"You're doing more than playing," Maxanne said. "You got a demo?"

The other boys looked at Nick. He shook his head. "Can't afford studio time."

Maxanne pulled out her card—back when WBCN business cards meant something. "Come by the station. Give me a call, we'll find some time for you to lay down some tracks."

The hope on those boys' faces could have powered half of Boston, bright enough to light up the whole damn city from Southie to Somerville to Sudbury and back. But Nick hesitated, his eyes flickering between the card and the tools on the wall, between possibility and the weight of responsibility.

"Why?" he asked. "Why would you help us?"

Maxanne stood up, her knees protesting—even at thirty-five, concrete floors were unforgiving, the kind of cold that seeps into your bones and reminds you that you're not as young as you used to be. "Because rock and roll is supposed to be for kids like you. Kids who play in garages because concert halls don't know they exist yet. Because your father's tools on that wall know more about honest work than half the industry executives in this city."

She paused at the garage door, looking back at four boys holding instruments like life preservers. "And because Steven Tyler played his first gig in a garage not much bigger than this one. You're already channeling that same Aerosmith energy—raw, authentic, hungry. The only difference between him and you is someone opened a door."

But here's the thing about doors—Maxanne thought she was opening one for Nick Johnson. She didn't know she was also closing another.

As she walked back to her Karmann Ghia, she could hear the music starting up again behind her, the sound of four boys who had nothing to lose and everything to prove. The sound of working-class dreams refusing to stay quiet, refusing to accept that geography was destiny, refusing to believe that the frequency that connected them to something larger was just static in the wind. Aerosmith's "Sweet Emotion" screamed into the night—"Sweet emotion, sweet emotion..."—the opening lyrics carrying across the cold March air like a promise that raw feeling could still matter in a world that tried to package everything.

She didn't know it then, but she was about to meet the other half of the equation. The boy from the other side of Hop Brook who would prove that class divisions were just lines on a map, that music could bridge any gap, that sometimes the most powerful thing you could do was refuse to stay in your assigned lane.

Less than a mile away on the other side of Hop Brook, there was a piano recital to attend, and a different kind of cage to witness.
