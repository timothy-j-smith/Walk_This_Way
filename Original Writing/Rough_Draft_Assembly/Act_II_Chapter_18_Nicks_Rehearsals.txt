## CHAPTER 18: NICK'S REHEARSALS

The garage on Dutton Road had become a pilgrimage site. Not literally—Grace wouldn't allow that, had made it clear that reporters and curiosity seekers weren't welcome—but kids from the northeast neighborhoods kept finding reasons to walk past, to listen to the music bleeding through the walls, to witness the sound of their own hopes being rehearsed in three-chord progressions and four-four time.

Tuesday evening, forty-eight hours before the Battle of the Bands, Nick stood in that garage surrounded by his father's tools and his mother's expectations and his community's faith, trying to remember why he'd started playing music in the first place.

The Lincoln boys were there—Stephen, David, and Mark, the inseparable trio who'd found in Nick's friendship the fourth corner of a musical square they hadn't known they were building. They'd been practicing for six hours, running through their set list until their fingers bled and their voices cracked, transforming Aerosmith covers into something rawer, more urgent, more honest than the originals.

But something was wrong. The music that had felt like salvation was starting to sound like obligation. The garage that had been sanctuary was feeling like a pressure cooker. The songs that had once been about escape were becoming about proving something to people who'd already decided what they believed about kids from places like this.

"What if we're not good enough?" Mark asked during a break, his usually steady hands shaking as he adjusted the settings on his borrowed keyboard. "What if all this talk about representing working-class kids just makes it worse when we lose?"

"We're not going to lose," Nick said, but his voice carried the hollow certainty of someone trying to convince himself more than his bandmates.

"But what if we do?" Stephen pressed, his bass guitar leaning against his shoulder like a weapon he was too tired to carry. "What happens to your mom then? To all the people who believed in us?"

Nick didn't have an answer for that. Couldn't admit that he'd been asking himself the same question every night since Saturday, lying awake calculating the cost of failure when failure meant more than just losing a high school competition.

The weight of representation sat on his shoulders like a lead apron. Every note he played carried the hopes of every kid whose parents worked doubles, every family that counted pennies between paychecks, every teenage dream that had been dismissed as unrealistic by people who'd never had to choose between realistic and possible.

Nick picked up his guitar—the same $25 acoustic his father had bought him three years ago, now worn smooth by countless hours of practice. The strings felt familiar under his callused fingers, the wood warm against his chest. This was where he'd learned to play, where he'd discovered that music could be more than entertainment—it could be survival.

"Let's run through 'Walk This Way' again," he said, his voice carrying the determination of someone who'd decided that if he was going to carry this weight, he might as well carry it with dignity.

The opening riff filled the garage, raw and powerful, the sound of working-class kids refusing to be quiet. Stephen's bass locked into the rhythm, David's drums providing the heartbeat, Mark's keyboard adding texture and depth. Together, they created something that was more than the sum of its parts—the sound of kids who'd learned to make do with what they had and make it work.

But as they played, Nick could hear the difference. The song that had once been about rebellion now carried the weight of responsibility. Every note was a statement, every chord a declaration, every lyric a promise to everyone who'd invested their hopes in his success.

"Hop Brook Blues," he announced when they finished, pulling out the original song he'd been working on. "This one's mine."

The Lincoln boys looked at each other, recognizing the significance. Nick rarely played his own compositions, preferring to let other people's songs carry his emotions. But tonight, with forty-eight hours left before the battle, he needed to say something that only he could say.

The song started simply—just Nick and his guitar, a working-class kid telling his story in three chords and the truth. But as the other instruments joined in, building the arrangement layer by layer, something emerged that was bigger than any individual part.

It was the sound of kids from the wrong side of Hop Brook refusing to be defined by their circumstances. It was the sound of working-class dignity asserting itself against assumptions about what people like them were capable of. It was the sound of a seventeen-year-old boy trying to honor his father's memory while carrying his mother's courage.

When they finished, the garage was silent except for the sound of their breathing, heavy with the weight of what they'd just created.

"That's it," Nick said, his voice rough with emotion. "That's what we're fighting for."

Outside, Grace Johnson stood in the driveway, listening to her son's music drift through the garage walls. She'd heard about the speech she'd given at Friendly's, about how it had transformed her son from a teenager competing in a talent show into a symbol of working-class determination.

She'd never meant for it to happen. She'd just been tired of hearing her son dismissed by people who'd never had to choose between paying the electric bill and buying groceries. She'd just been tired of the condescending conversations about "those kids from Lincoln" as if geography determined character.

But now her son was carrying the weight of every working-class family's hopes, and she wondered if she'd done him a favor or condemned him to a burden he was too young to bear.

Inside the garage, Nick practiced until his fingers bled, until his voice cracked, until the Lincoln boys finally went home to their own nervous preparations, leaving him alone with his father's tools and his mother's courage and the impossible responsibility of proving that working-class kids deserved more than sympathy.

The Battle of the Bands was twenty-four hours away, and Nick Johnson was discovering that the hardest part of competition wasn't playing better than your opponent. It was playing well enough to justify the faith that other people had invested in your success.
