## CHAPTER 1: THE LAST VOICE OF BOSTON ROCK

The thing about endings is they make liars of us all. You promise to go out with dignity, say something profound, make those final moments count. But here I am, Maxanne Sartori, seventy-three years old and sitting in the same Studio C where I watched Steven Tyler's hair get caught in the mixing board back in '73, the air thick with cigarette smoke and desperation, and all I can think about is two kids who never made it onto my playlist.

WBCN dies at midnight. After fifty-six years of being the Rock of Boston—the station that launched Aerosmith when they were just kids from New Hampshire with more attitude than money—we're flipping to automated Christian contemporary. The equipment around me wheezes its last tonight: turntables I fought to keep relevant while the suits pushed for automation, cart machines that hold the DNA of every Boston band that ever mattered, mixing boards that have channeled more raw talent than any studio in America. Tomorrow they'll be sold for scrap or shipped to museums where teenagers will stare at them like relics from another era, wondering what all those knobs and buttons actually did.

"Maxanne?" The program director's voice crackles through the intercom like a bad phone connection. He's twenty-eight, wears sneakers that cost more than my first car—a '67 Mustang that I bought with my first WBCN paycheck—and thinks rock history started with Nirvana. Kid probably thinks Aerosmith was just some old band his dad listened to. "You've got four hours left. Make them count."

Make them count. As if four hours could contain what WBCN meant to a city that lived and breathed rock and roll. As if I could summarize fifty-six years of breaking bands, of late-night conversations with musicians who'd just poured their souls into three-minute songs, of watching Boston transform from a city of steel and sweat into whatever the hell it's become now. As if I could capture the moment when rock and roll was dangerous, when DJ voices mattered more than algorithms, when two kids with guitars could change the world if they could just stop hating each other long enough to plug in their amps.

I adjust the headphones—the same Sony MDR-7506s I've worn since 1971, held together now with electrical tape and pure stubbornness. The ON AIR sign blinks red like a dying heartbeat. One last time.

"This is Maxanne Sartori, and for the next four hours, you're listening to the final broadcast of WBCN, 104.1 on your FM dial. I've been your guide through the evolution of Boston rock since before some of you were born—since the days when The Standells were the house band at the Boston Tea Party, since J. Geils was just a kid with a harmonica and a dream, since Aerosmith was four guys from New Hampshire who thought they could take on the world. I've spun the first singles from bands who went on to fill stadiums, and I've played demos from kids who never made it past their garage. But tonight, I want to tell you about the night I watched the future of rock and roll die in a high school auditorium in Sudbury, Massachusetts. The night America chose division over harmony, when two kids who should have been allies became enemies over a question that seems quaint now: What is authentic rock and roll?"

I take a sip of bourbon—another violation of modern radio protocol, but who's going to fire me now?—and feel the warmth spread through my chest like liquid fire, the good kind that makes you remember why you started drinking in the first place. This is a bottle of Pappy Van Winkle that Steven Tyler himself sent when he heard about the station closing. 'For the first lady who ever gave a damn,' the note said. Outside the booth windows, Boston glitters in the October darkness like broken glass in a parking lot—all glass towers and gentrification where dive bars used to breed bands, where the Rat used to host shows that would make your grandmother blush, where kids with nothing but dreams could still believe they had a shot at changing the world.

"You want to know why WBCN is dying? Why this is the very last broadcast? Let me tell you about two kids in 1979 who showed me exactly when American radio—and America itself—chose money over music, when we stopped believing that a kid with a guitar and a dream could matter more than a marketing budget."

The console lights pulse expectantly like a heartbeat on life support. I've got carts loaded with the soundtrack of a generation: Aerosmith's "Dream On," Queen's "Bohemian Rhapsody," The Cars' "Just What I Needed," J. Geils Band's "Centerfold." But first, I need to take you back to when those legends were just beginning to cast shadows, when the real battles were fought not in recording studios but in the divided territories of suburban high schools where kids learned that geography was destiny.

"March, 1979. Disco was dying its overdue death. The Clash were about to teach America that punk had something to say. And in Sudbury, Massachusetts—that's about twenty miles west of here for you newcomers—two seventeen-year-old boys were about to go to war over a question that seems quaint now: What is authentic rock and roll?"

I spin my chair to face the wall of albums, each one a memory, a story, a moment when music mattered more than metrics. The vinyl spines catch the booth's amber light like tombstones in a graveyard of sound. My fingers find the right cart without looking—muscle memory from five decades of curating sound, my hands knowing these grooves better than my own face in the mirror.

"Nick Johnson lived in a house where the mortgage was always late and dreams were luxuries you couldn't afford. His father had died in a construction accident two years earlier, crushed beneath a load of steel beams when a crane cable snapped—the kind of industrial tragedy that makes page three of the Metro section and gets forgotten by everyone except the families who eat silence for dinner afterward. His mother worked doubles at Friendly's, came home smelling like fryer oil and other people's happiness, and never once complained about her son spending grocery money on guitar strings."

The bourbon burns like liquid fire, the good kind that makes you remember why you started drinking. In the engineering booth, the kid they've assigned to babysit my final show looks up from his phone. Maybe something in my voice warns him this won't be the nostalgia trip he expected.

"Jackie Marsh lived less than a mile away as the crow flies, but between them stretched Hop Brook—named a century ago for its hops, the old mill long gone to modernization. The brook divided Sudbury like a scar, separating the haves from the have-nots. In winter, kids from both sides might skate the same frozen pond, but when the ice melted, they returned to their proper territories. Jackie's father taught economics at Boston University, the kind of professor who wrote papers about the working class without ever living in their neighborhoods, who could analyze the economic geography of Sudbury's four quadrants without understanding that those invisible lines actually shaped real lives, real friendships, real possibilities. Jackie had a voice trained by the best teachers money could buy, fingers that had touched pianos worth more than Nick Johnson's entire house."

I pause, letting the silence breathe. Radio silence used to terrify program directors, but I learned long ago that the spaces between words can say more than the words themselves.

"These boys should never have met. America designs its suburbs specifically to prevent these collisions—invisible lines drawn by property values, school districts, the careful architecture of keeping the classes in their proper lanes. But music has always been democracy's wild card, the wavelength that cuts through all the bullshit boundaries we build."

Through the booth window, I can see the billboard across Boylston Street advertising luxury condos where The Rat used to host bands too raw for radio. Twenty-four hundred square feet starting at 2.2 million—you could buy the entire block of Lansdowne Street for that in '77. The Ramones played that club back when Joey was so tall he had to duck under the pipes backstage, and the sound system was held together with duct tape and prayers.

"I met them both the night of March fifteenth, 1979. The Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School Battle of the Bands. I was there because the station had sponsored it, back when we could afford to care about local music. Back when Clear Channel hadn't swallowed half the FM dial and algorithms hadn't replaced intuition."

Another sip. The bourbon is smooth, expensive—a gift from Steven Tyler himself when he heard about the station closing. 'For the first lady who ever gave a damn,' the note said. He still remembers, even after all these years, even after becoming the biggest rock star in the world, he still remembers the kid from WBCN who believed in four guys from New Hampshire before anyone else did.

"I need you to understand something about 1979. It wasn't just another year. It was the pivot point when rock and roll had to decide what it wanted to be. The corporations were circling, smelling money in what had been rebellion. MTV was three years away from making image matter more than sound. We were living in the last moments when two kids with nothing but talent and rage could matter as much as marketing budgets."

I load the first song—not Aerosmith, not The Cars or J. Geils Band, but a scratchy demo that never made it to vinyl. The tape hisses with age, the left channel crackles if you push it past six, but that imperfection is the point. This isn't about perfect sound. This is about perfect truth.

"This is the only recording that exists of Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh playing together. Not from that night in 1979—no, this is from the night after the Battle of the Bands, when they came to this very studio and proved that harmony was still possible, even after everything that had happened. March sixteenth, 1979. Listen to it. Listen to what we lost."

The demo starts—two young voices finding harmony over acoustic guitars, neither Aerosmith nor Queen but something uniquely theirs. It lasts ninety-three seconds before cutting off mid-verse. That's all that survived.

"Ninety-three seconds. That's how much evidence I have that America chose division over music. The rest of the story? That's what the next four hours are for. So pour yourself something strong, turn off your phone, and let me tell you how the Battle of the Bands became the war that killed rock and roll's democratic promise."

The ON AIR sign pulses like a dying heartbeat. Four hours left to tell the truth, four hours left to make sense of forty-five years of watching America choose noise over harmony.

I crack my knuckles—arthritis now, too many decades of spinning vinyl—and lean into the microphone one more time. The bourbon's warmth spreads through my chest like liquid courage, the good kind that makes you tell stories you've been carrying too long.

"But first, we need to talk about a garage on Dutton Road, and why the ghost of a dead construction worker might have been the only adult who understood what was really at stake…"
