## CHAPTER 1: THE LAST VOICE OF BOSTON ROCK

Four hours. That's what I've got left.

Four hours before WBCN becomes just another frequency in the graveyard of American radio. Four hours before the suits flip the switch and turn us into automated Christian contemporary—because apparently Jesus rocks harder than Freddie Mercury ever did. Four hours before fifty-six years of breaking bands, of late-night conversations with musicians who poured their souls into three-minute songs, of watching Boston transform from a city of steel and sweat into whatever the hell it's become now—four hours before all of that gets erased like a bad demo tape.

I'm Maxanne Sartori. Seventy-three years old, sitting in Studio B with a bourbon that costs more than most people's car payments, watching the ON AIR sign blink red like a dying heartbeat. Can you believe it?  The station called and brought me back.  This one last time and I am now in the same studio where I watched Steven Tyler hear his own voice on the radio for the first time back in '73—the look on his face when he realized that raw, untamed sound was actually going out to thousands of people, that it was real, that it mattered. The same turntables that spun the first singles from bands who went on to fill stadiums. The same microphone that's carried my voice through decades of chaos, rebellion, and the kind of music that made parents lock their daughters' bedroom doors.

"Maxanne?" The program director's voice crackles through the intercom like a bad phone connection. Kid's twenty-eight, wears sneakers that cost more than my first apartment—a studio in the Back Bay I rented with my first WBCN paycheck—and thinks rock history started with Nirvana. "You've got four hours left. Make them count."

Make them count. As if four hours could contain what WBCN meant to a city that lived and breathed rock and roll. As if I could summarize fifty-six years in some tidy narrative arc with a beginning, middle, and end. As if the story I'm about to tell could be wrapped up with a happy ending and a lesson learned.

But here's the thing about endings—they make liars of us all. You promise to go out with dignity, say something profound, make those final moments matter. And then you find yourself sitting in a radio booth with a bottle of Pappy Van Winkle that Steven Tyler himself sent when he heard about the station closing. 'For the first lady who ever gave a damn,' the note said. Even after all these years, even after becoming one of the biggest rock stars in the world, he still remembers.

I adjust the headphones—the same Sony MDR-7506s I've worn since 1971, held together now with electrical tape and pure stubbornness. The console lights pulse expectantly like a heartbeat on life support. Outside the booth windows, Boston glitters in the October darkness like broken glass in a parking lot—all glass towers and gentrification where dive bars used to breed bands, where the Rat used to host shows that would make your grandmother blush, where kids with nothing but dreams could still believe they had a shot at changing the world.

                                 MAXANNE
          This is Maxanne Sartori, and for the next four hours, you're listening 
          to the final broadcast of WBCN, 104.1 on your FM dial. The Rock of Boston. 
          The station that launched Aerosmith when they were just kids from New Hampshire 
          with more attitude than money. The station that gave J. Geils his first shot, 
          that broke The Cars when they were still just a garage band, that made Boston 
          believe it could rock as hard as anywhere else.

I take a sip of bourbon—another violation of modern radio protocol, but who's going to fire me?  I feel the warmth spread through my chest like liquid fire. The good kind that makes you remember why you started drinking in the first place. The kind that makes you tell stories you've been carrying too long.

                                 MAXANNE
          But tonight isn't about the bands that made it. Tonight is about the ones that 
          didn't, and there were many. And tonight is about one I could never get out of my head after all these years - it's about two kids from Sudbury,
          who should have changed everything but got caught in the crossfire of America's 
          class war. Tonight is about the night I watched two potential stars die in a 
          high school auditorium, when two seventeen-year-old boys who should have been 
          allies became rivals over a question that seems quaint now: What is authentic 
          rock and roll?

The silence stretches across the city like a held breath. In the engineering booth, the kid they've assigned to babysit my final show looks up from his phone. Maybe something in my voice warns him this won't be the nostalgia trip he expected.

                                 MAXANNE
          March, 1979. Disco was gasping its last breath—thank God. The Bee Gees had spent 
          three years convincing America that falsetto voices and dance floors were the 
          future of music. 'Saturday Night Fever' had turned every bar into a discotheque, 
          'Stayin' Alive' had replaced actual living, and 'Night Fever' had given us all 
          a fever we couldn't shake. But authentic rock and roll was fighting back. The 
          Clash were about to teach America that punk had something to say too. MTV was 
          three years away from making image matter more than sound. We were living in 
          the last moments when two kids with nothing but talent and passion could matter 
          as much as marketing budgets.

I spin my chair to face the wall of albums, each one a memory, a story, a moment when music mattered more than metrics. The vinyl spines catch the booth's amber light like tombstones in a graveyard of sound. My fingers find the right cart without looking—muscle memory, my hands knowing these grooves better than my own face in the mirror.

                                 MAXANNE
          Nick Johnson lived in a house where the mortgage was late and dreams were luxuries 
          you couldn't afford. His father had died in a construction accident two years 
          earlier, crushed beneath a load of steel beams when a crane cable snapped—the 
          kind of industrial tragedy that makes page three of the Metro section and gets 
          forgotten by everyone except the families who eat silence for dinner afterward. 
          His mother worked doubles at Friendly's, came home smelling like fryer oil and 
          other people's happiness, and never once complained about her son spending grocery 
          money on guitar strings.

The bourbon burns like liquid fire, the good kind that makes you remember why you started drinking. I can see the kid in the engineering booth scrolling through his phone, probably checking his social media, updating his status to "babysitting some old lady's final radio show." He has no idea what a garage means to rock and roll. He grew up with GarageBand, thinking music could be manufactured in a bedroom with the right plugins. He never felt the sacred geometry of four walls, concrete floor, and kids who had nothing to lose.

                                 MAXANNE
          Jackie Marsh lived less than a mile away as the crow flies, but between them 
          stretched Hop Brook—named a century ago for its hops, the old mill long gone 
          to modernization. The brook divided Sudbury like a scar, separating the haves 
          from the have-nots. In winter, kids from both sides skated the same nearby pond 
          trying to emulate Bobby Orr's graceful glides across the ice, but when the ice 
          melted, they returned to their proper territories. Jackie's father taught economics 
          at Boston University, the kind of professor who wrote papers about the working 
          class without ever living in their neighborhoods, who could analyze the economic 
          geography of Sudbury's four quadrants without understanding that those invisible 
          lines actually shaped real lives, real friendships, real possibilities.

I pause, letting the silence breathe. Radio silence used to terrify program directors, but I learned long ago that the spaces between words can say more than the words themselves. That's the thing about radio—it's not just about the music you play or the words you say. It's about the spaces you leave, the moments you let hang in the air, the way you make people lean forward in their cars, waiting for what comes next. It's about understanding that sometimes the most powerful thing you can do is shut up and let the music speak for itself.

                                 MAXANNE
          These boys lived in the same town, went to the same school, walked the same streets. 
          But America designs its suburbs specifically to prevent these collisions—invisible 
          lines drawn by property values, school districts, the careful architecture of 
          keeping the classes in their proper lanes. But music has always been democracy's 
          wild card, the wavelength that cuts through all the bullshit boundaries we build.

Through the booth window, I can see the billboard across Boylston Street advertising luxury condos where The Rat used to host bands too raw for radio. Twenty-four hundred square feet starting at 2.2 million—you could buy the entire block of Lansdowne Street for that in '77. The Ramones played that club back when Joey was so tall he had to duck under the pipes backstage, and the sound system was held together with duct tape and prayers.

                                 MAXANNE
          I met them both in the spring of 1979, about two months before the Lincoln-Sudbury 
          Regional High School Battle of the Bands. I was there because the station had 
          sponsored it, back when we could afford to care about local music. Back when 
          Clear Channel hadn't swallowed half the FM dial and algorithms hadn't replaced 
          intuition. This was WBCN's first regional competition, and it was a big deal—not 
          just for the kids, but for us. We were scouting talent, sure, but we were also 
          building our audience, steering more kids to our channel, which meant more paid 
          advertisements and a stronger foothold in the Boston music scene. The success 
          of this first Battle would determine whether we'd sponsor more in the coming years.

Another sip. The bourbon is smooth, expensive—a gift from Steven Tyler himself when he heard about the station closing. 'For the first lady who ever gave a damn,' the note said. He still remembers, even after all these years, even after becoming one of the biggest rock stars in the world.

I reach for the cassette on my desk, feeling the weight of it in my hands. The plastic case is warm from my grip, the label slightly faded, but still cataloged in it's perfect place 45 years later. "Demo - Nick/Jackie - Not Authorized - March 1979" written in block letters. The tape itself has that old-cassette smell—plastic and magnetic oxide and the faint trace of cigarette smoke from the studio where it was recorded. I can still remember that day, March 20, 1979, anout a week after the Battle of the Bands, when they came to this very studio and proved that harmony was still possible, even after everything that had happened.

                                 MAXANNE
          This is the only recording that exists of Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh playing 
          together. Not from that night in 1979—no, this is from 5 days after the Battle 
          of the Bands, when they came to this very studio and proved that harmony was 
          still possible, even after everything that had happened. 

The shred of evidence that we chose division over music. The rest of the story? That's what the next four hours are for. So pour yourself something strong, turn off your phone, and let me tell you how the Battle of the Bands became the war that killed rock and roll's democratic promise.

The ON AIR sign pulses like a dying heartbeat. Four hours left to tell the truth, four hours left to make sense of forty-five years of watching America choose noise over harmony.

I crack my knuckles and lean into the microphone one more time. The bourbon's warmth spreads through my chest like liquid courage, the good kind that makes you tell stories you've been carrying too long.

                                 MAXANNE
          But first, we need to talk about a garage on Dutton Road, and why the ghost of 
          a dead construction worker might have been the only adult who understood what 
          was really at stake…
