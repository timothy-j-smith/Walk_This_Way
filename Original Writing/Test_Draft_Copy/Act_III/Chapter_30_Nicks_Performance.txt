## CHAPTER 30: NICK'S PERFORMANCE

### **Walking the Line (9:00 PM)**

By the time Walking the Line stepped out beneath the scorched gymnasium lights, the audience had settled into an expectant hush—the kind that lives at the corners of every small-town gathering, a certain knowledge that for a handful of people, at least, tonight would matter. The air was thick with anticipation and the nearly invisible lines that split Sudbury into its untidy quadrants.

Nick Johnson led them onto the stage. Nothing showy about him. Jeans, black t-shirt, guitar dangling from his shoulder—every inch of him marked by effort, not affect. Out in the chairs, you could pick out faces from every direction of town: kids who rode the late bus, parents with arms folded, teachers pretending not to judge. In the first row, Grace Johnson sat upright, her pride restrained to a single slight tilt of her chin—rooting for her son without apologizing for what he’d had to overcome.

Stephen Murphy took his seat at the drums, careful and calm; Tommy Morrison shouldered his bass with a nod, and Danny Kowalski flicked the dust from the keyboard keys. They were, by Sudbury standards, an underdog act—less polish, more heart.

A quick glance over his shoulder from Nick—the kind that says, "Ready?"—and then a downstroke on his battered guitar, and the night truly began.

### **"Walk This Way"**

The opening lick of "Walk This Way" bounced off the cinderblock walls, all verve and just enough looseness to remind everyone it was being played live, right now. The rhythm section locked in tight: Tommy’s bass thumped like the faint, constant anxiety of every grownup in the crowd, while Stephen’s drumming was the sound of stubborn hope. Nick’s voice, slightly out of breath, was crooked but eager, almost daring the crowd not to enjoy it.

What "Walk This Way" lacked in clean edges it gained in electricity; in pockets of the gym, feet started to tap, shoulders loosened. You could read the faces—some still skeptical, some already swept up in the delight of seeing themselves reflected back from the stage, imperfect and urgent.

At the end of the last chorus, Nick didn’t let the moment settle. Instead, he teased a riff past the boundaries of the song—something wild, not in the record, stretching out into the rafters as if placing a bet against the limits of the night itself.

### **"Dream On"**

Without missing a beat, the band moved into "Dream On." Where Jackie's arrangement had been pristine, Nick’s was bruised and yearning. The others laid down just enough backing, but as Nick sang—his voice a shade more fragile, even as he reached for every note—it became clear that the careful separation between band and audience was dissolving.

Halfway through, Nick drew out the progression, bending the notes in a way that made the familiar feel new: hesitant, aching, carrying something older than seventeen years. It wasn’t the dream of a golden future; it was the kind of wish you make for one minute of clarity. The band watched him, uncertain at first, but soon found the wisdom to let Nick lead wherever he needed.

Then, slowly, they drew back—first Danny's keys, then Tommy’s bass, Stephen’s snare drifting to a stop—until only Nick’s guitar remained.

### **The Solo**

He played alone. Not for effect, but because the music demanded honesty and he had none left to hide. The solo Nick offered was no technical spectacle—it was quiet at first, searching, the melody unraveling in the air like the edges of a worn letter. There were places where his picking faltered, moments where the note barely caught, but each tone—each phrase—landed softly and deliberately, unburdened by pride.

For that span of time, the boundaries of the auditorium faded: Jackie's friends in the southwest watched every move; kids from the northeast, suddenly attentive, sat up straight; parents old enough to remember the original records felt a tug at something they thought long settled.

Nick played as if no one had ever told him music needed an audience. It was as close to confession as a teenage boy could manage—grief, hope, anger, gratitude—all traced out in six strings.

When it ended, you could have heard the echo of the last note ricochet off the walls, waiting for someone to claim it.

### **Final Notes**

The applause, when it came, was a slow, gathering thing—people not quite certain what they were applauding, only that something honest had passed between everyone in the room. Even the band seemed unsure whether the set was finished or only just beginning.

Nick nodded at his friends, then sketched a glance at the wings. Jackie watched him, neither triumphant nor disappointed—simply understanding, for the first time in years, who his friend really was.

As Walking the Line drifted offstage, there was a change in the air, subtle and unmistakable. The quadrants would not dissolve overnight, but for a minute at least, Sudbury had become one room—hopeful, uncertain, and a little bit larger than it had been before the first note played.

---

**Transition:** Performances over, all that remained was the waiting: the judges with their notebooks, the sound of folding chairs shifting, kids debating quietly in the aisles. But for this evening, what mattered most already had happened—the music had worked its slow magic, and nobody would quite forget what it felt like when one boy ripped open the separation between effort and feeling.
