# CHAPTER 26: THE BATTLE BEGINS

---

May 15th, 1979. Seven o’clock sharp and the Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School auditorium was so crowded even ghosts couldn’t squeeze in. The air vibrated with borrowed energy and cigarette smoke, the kind that clung to old coats and new haircuts alike. Eight hundred students jammed shoulder to shoulder, knees knocking against auditorium seats, the town’s sharp lines and blurry boundaries mapped out in polyester and denim. If Sudbury was carved into quarters, tonight, every tribe showed up with their banners loosely furled.

The Rampers—jocks and prom royalty, all teeth and good hair from families with the kind of lawns no kid ever mowed—basically owned the front. The Rats lurked by the doors and in the wings, greased hair beneath the school’s flickering lights, boots up on seats, forever practiced at making boredom look bold, a curl of smoke at their lips even with faculty on patrol. The Regs played it safe, huddled mid-house, eyes on the program, making plans to be invisible. And the Rollers—car kids, sleeves rolled, hands bearing proof of time spent with engines and Mr. Pacenka’s toolbox—stuck to the back rows, trading stories of speed and trouble nobody’s parents would hear about until July.

If you paid attention, you could swim through the auditorium’s current and see everything you needed to know about the town—plenty of lines, sure, but not a wall among them that couldn’t be kicked over by a good enough song. That’s what everyone was banking on tonight.

On stage, the promise of spectacle outshone even the instruments. Rented spotlights slashed through haze, stagehands whispered back and forth, a lifeline from the drama club barely masking the anxiety of the bands corralling their gear in the makeshift green room. The air vibrated with a backstage medley of nerves, cologne, and last-minute rehearsed banter. Every so often, from behind the curtain, laughter or a shout leaked through—a kind of pressure relief, but nobody could mistake it for confidence.

Front and center, the judges’ table looked like a jury for dreams: Maxanne Sartori—late-night voice from WBCN who’d championed Aerosmith years before they made it big; Carter Alan, baby-faced, quick-eared, with the kind of attention grownups struggle to feign; Bob Walker from Strawberries Records and Tapes, whose thick arms rested atop a clipboard like he was selecting the draft. And, of course, Chuck Morrison, WBCN’s morning show MC, tall and frayed at the edges, his enthusiasm loose in the elbows, exactly the sort of local star who could turn a town’s ordinary into something worth missing your curfew for.

“Good evening, Lincoln-Sudbury!” Chuck boomed into the battered mic, sending feedback twining around the rafters. “Welcome to the first ever WBCN Battle of the Bands! Sponsored by us, your favorite radio loudmouths, and Strawberries Records and Tapes, the place to get your wax, eight-tracks, and—if you’re lucky—immortality.” There was a laugh in his voice, and more than a few out near the fire doors. “Eight bands tonight. Five hundred bucks. Real recording time in the city. And one shot to say this town’s got more to offer than test scores and strip-mall pizza. Are we ready?!”

The response hit like Fourth of July at City Hall—rowdy and skeptical, the laughter edged with nerves. All eyes in the room, from the football captains to the kids who’d skipped dinner to buy guitar strings, were ready for either legend or trainwreck, and maybe both.

Chuck worked his list: Maxanne, Carter, Bob—all given their due by the house, but when Bob Walker’s name landed, the Regs and the Rollers rose to their feet, clapping harder than anyone else. Studio time was golden. Plugging a local kid on the city’s late-night radio? That was something to carry into adulthood.

“Here’s your lineup, Sudbury,” Chuck announced, the envelope held out like a soon-to-crest birthday cake. “Let’s see who’s brave, who’s foolish, and who—if anyone—can wake up this sleepy town.”

**1. The Jesters**—freshmen, too new to the world to be self-conscious, all bright hope and Cars covers  
**2. Detroit Rock City**—Acton-Boxborough’s KISS disciples, face paint and fire, more comedy than chaos  
**3. The Dead Heads**—Wayland’s jam faithful, playing Grateful Dead by way of summer camp  
**4. Talking Heads Talking**—Boston Latin’s new wave vanguard: sharp suits, sharper nerves  
**5. Walking the Line**—Nick Johnson’s crew, Aerosmith loud and full of challenge  
**6. Crown Jewels**—Jackie Marsh’s outfit, Queen covers built from both sweat and inheritance  
**7. Rumours**—Patty Lally’s Concord-Carlisle dreamers, conjuring Fleetwood harmonies  
**8. The Regulators**—Framingham’s Zep-worshippers, promising to break the windows with volume

The Jesters, kids barely out of middle school, opened the night with “Just What I Needed.” It was careful, earnest, almost sweet—a reminder of late summer swings and Friday night sleepovers, but nothing to rearrange your understanding of the universe. Detroit Rock City stomped in next, everything sprayed silver, platform heels wobbling, blood packets spent by the second verse. There were mishaps: fire-breathing that mostly steamed, cake makeup drooping into the eyes. Still the crowd cheered, partly for the spectacle, partly in hope their own embarrassing attempts at cool weren’t quite so obvious.

The Dead Heads rolled slow and warm through “Truckin’,” crowd hypnotized at first, then gradually fidgeting as minutes stretched into what felt like months. It was only after seven minutes of “Dark Star” and a couple restless sighs from the judges’ table that Chuck, mercy in his eyes, dimmed the lights: the old “wrap it up” signal. The applause that followed was brisk and forgiving.

After a break, Talking Heads Talking brought their own clinical weirdness—matching suits, nervous glances, “Psycho Killer” played within an inch of its life. A few older siblings in the crowd grinned knowingly while most kids waited for something they recognized, and the room’s temperature settled somewhere between cautious and kind.

But by the time the fourth band had left the stage, you could feel it—the mood turning electric. The opening acts were behind them; now it was the best in town, one after the next, ready to play not just for prizes or pride, but for their own tribes, for every side-glance in the hallway, every coach and teacher listening for a reason not to write the whole thing off as kids blowing off steam.

Backstage, three bands leaned into the moment—Nick Johnson’s crew pacing, Jackie Marsh’s Crown Jewels tuning in the last light, Patty Lally’s Rumours checking lipstick and setlists both. The whole story—divided loyalties, old friendships, sharp regrets—swirled below the surface, ready to break into song. Sudbury, for one night, was a town not divided but gathered, every tribe in the same room, betting on which anthem or fragile moment would linger when the house lights finally came up.

---

And somewhere out in that overheated room, between the stolen glances and the tribal drumbeat of feet on school-issue linoleum, kids whispered and wondered whether the next band—the one with the straight-backed kid whose father had moved every expectation onto his son’s shoulders—might actually turn the joke of the suburbs into a declaration. The curtain would rise on Jackie and the Crown Jewels first, then Nick and Patty, and whatever happened next would matter. Because before the spring was done, this place would need its own legends—earned, not borrowed.
