Chapter 3: The Gilded Cage

"Now," I continue, cueing up Queen's "Under Pressure" because some stories require their own soundtrack, while I methodically clean the vinyl with the anti-static cloth I've used for thirty years—understanding that proper care keeps the music pure, free from the scratches that accumulate when you stop paying attention to what matters.
The same week I discovered Nick Johnson's garage temple, I received a phone call that would complicate everything. Dr. Richard Marsh had called the station personally—not his secretary, not his assistant, but the man himself, which should have been my first warning that this wasn't just another proud parent pushing his prodigy.
"Ms. Sartori," his voice carried that particular Boston Brahmin accent that turns 'car' into 'cah' but somehow makes it sound aristocratic rather than provincial. "I understand you have an ear for... emerging talent."
"I have an ear for music," I'd corrected, because I'd learned to be wary of people who talked around what they meant.
"Precisely. My son Jackie is having a recital next week. He's been accepted to Juilliard's pre-college program—full scholarship, though naturally we don't need it. But before he leaves for New York, we thought... that is, he thought... perhaps someone from the local music community should witness what serious training can accomplish."
The pause after "serious training" lasted just long enough to make clear what he thought of garage bands and radio DJs and anyone who confused volume with virtue.
I take a sip of water from the glass I keep positioned exactly six inches from the microphone, maintaining the hydration ritual that's kept my voice clear through thousands of Boston broadcasts.
The Marsh house stood on Concord Road like it had been placed there by a decorator with unlimited budget and limited imagination. Colonial perfection in white clapboard and black shutters, each window box planted with flowers that bloomed on schedule, lawn so green it looked painted. The circular driveway could have hosted a small parade, and probably had during Jackie's elaborate birthday parties that the northeast quarter kids heard about but were never invited to attend.
Walking up to that front door—mahogany thick enough to stop bullets, brass knocker polished to mirror brightness—was like approaching a museum exhibit titled "How the Other Half Lives, Circa 1979." Even the doorbell played a classical melody, probably Bach, definitely pretentious.
Mrs. Eileen Marsh answered the door herself, which surprised me until I remembered that Wednesday was probably the help's day off. She was beautiful in that carefully maintained way that money could purchase—hair colored by professionals, skin preserved by expensive creams, clothes that whispered rather than shouted their cost. She moved like a dancer who'd never forgotten her training, every gesture calculated for maximum grace and minimum effort.
"Ms. Sartori, welcome. Richard is so pleased you could come." Her smile was perfect, practiced, the kind developed at boarding schools where charm was considered a life skill. "We've prepared the living room as a concert space. I do hope you'll find it adequate."
Adequate. The living room had been transformed into something that rivaled Symphony Hall. Persian rugs worth more than cars, oil paintings that belonged in museums, furniture that had probably been sitting in the same spots since the Adams administration. Folding chairs arranged in perfect rows faced a rented Steinway grand that cost more than Grace Johnson would make in five years. Champagne chilled in silver buckets, hors d'oeuvres arranged on china that had been in the family since someone important had dinner with someone else important sometime when America was still figuring out what it wanted to be.
The audience was exactly what you'd expect: prep school parents and university faculty, summer house owners and trust fund guardians, the kind of people who considered themselves cultured because they could pronounce "Tchaikovsky" correctly and owned first edition books they'd never actually read. They applauded with their fingertips and discussed their latest European vacations between movements, treating music like expensive wallpaper—pleasant background for more important conversations about stock portfolios and boarding school admissions.
Jackie entered wearing a tuxedo that fit like it had been grown on him rather than tailored. Seventeen years old but carrying himself like someone who'd been preparing for this moment since birth, every step measured, every gesture the product of deportment lessons and etiquette classes and the kind of training that taught you how to present yourself to people who mattered. He was beautiful in that way that good nutrition and orthodontics and personal trainers could manufacture—tall, aristocratic, blessed with the kind of bone structure that made photographers weep with joy.
But when he sat at the piano, something shifted. The performance mask slipped just enough to reveal something real underneath—a boy who'd spent so many years being told what excellence looked like that he'd forgotten what it felt like.
I adjust the microphone position with the minute precision I've maintained since my first Boston radio job, understanding that even the smallest details can affect whether truth gets transmitted clearly.
He opened with Chopin's Nocturne in E-flat major, because of course he did. The piece that every piano student tackles eventually, the musical equivalent of climbing Mount Washington—challenging enough to impress, familiar enough to judge. His technique was flawless, every note struck with precision that spoke of thousands of hours of practice, of lessons that started before memory, of parents who considered anything less than perfection a character flaw.
The audience nodded appreciatively, their champagne glasses reflecting the chandelier light like tiny crystal prisons. They were hearing what they expected to hear—technical mastery, cultural sophistication, proof that their investment in proper education was paying dividends. Dr. Marsh stood in the back of the room, arms crossed, face wearing the expression of a man whose plans were proceeding exactly as designed.
But I was watching Jackie's face, and I saw something his parents had trained themselves not to notice: he was dying in there. Behind the technical perfection was a boy playing someone else's definition of music, speaking someone else's language with flawless pronunciation but no soul, no personal investment, no sense that these notes meant anything beyond their market value in the cultural stock exchange.
He moved through the program like a beautiful robot: Bach's Goldberg Variations (selected excerpts, naturally), Debussy's "Clair de Lune" (because someone had told him it was romantic), Rachmaninoff's Prelude in C-sharp minor (because difficulty impressed people who confused complexity with depth). Each piece performed with technical excellence and emotional vacancy, like watching a surgeon operate on a mannequin.
Then something happened. Between the Rachmaninoff and what was supposed to be the evening's finale—Mozart's Sonata in A major, K. 331—Jackie stopped. Just sat there at the piano, hands in his lap, staring at the keys like they might tell him something important.
The audience stirred uncomfortably. This wasn't in the program. Spontaneity was not part of the evening's carefully choreographed demonstration of cultural superiority.
Dr. Marsh cleared his throat—a warning about maintaining proper recital pacing, about not disappointing the guests who had graciously donated their Wednesday evening to witness his son's excellence.
But Jackie wasn't listening to his father. He was listening to something else, something that had been trying to break through years of classical conditioning like water finding cracks in a dam.
His fingers found different keys. The opening piano chords of "Under Pressure"—that David Bowie and Queen collaboration that sounded like anxiety having a nervous breakdown—began to emerge from the Steinway like contraband smuggled into a church service.
The room froze. You could hear pearls clutching themselves, champagne glasses stopping halfway to carefully lipsticked mouths, the collective intake of breath from people who'd never been challenged by anything more dangerous than choosing between brie and camembert.
"Pressure, pushing down on me," Jackie sang, his classically trained voice doing things that Freddie Mercury would have envied, "Pressing down on you, no man ask for..."
And here's what that room full of cultivated taste and purchased refinement didn't understand: Jackie Marsh wasn't defiling their temple. He was consecrating it. He was taking their precious Steinway and making it confess that rock and roll was just classical music that had learned to feel something, that had permission to break its own rules, that understood emotion was more important than technique.
For four minutes and twenty-nine seconds, Jackie Marsh was free. His hands found chord progressions that shouldn't have been possible on a solo piano, harmonies that transformed Queen's anthem of anxiety into something that sounded like prayer, like confession, like a seventeen-year-old boy admitting he was suffocating in silk and cashmere and the weight of other people's expectations.
I reach for my bourbon, taking a careful sip while I organize the next sequence of albums with the obsessive precision that's kept my broadcasts flowing smoothly for four decades.
"Under pressure that burns a building down," he sang, and every word was autobiography, every note was rebellion, every chord change was a declaration that he was more than the sum of his expensive parts.
When he finished, the silence was different from any of the other silences that evening. This wasn't the appreciative quiet that followed technical mastery. This was the silence of scandal, of propriety interrupted, of carefully maintained facades cracking just enough to reveal the chaos underneath.
Dr. Marsh was the first to speak, his voice carrying the controlled fury of a man whose plans had been publicly derailed by his own son. "That was... unexpected."
"That was rock and roll," I said, standing up because someone needed to, because I'd just witnessed something that mattered more than all the Chopin nocturnes in the world. "That was your son showing you who he really is."
Jackie looked at me then, really looked, and I saw the same desperate hunger I'd seen in Nick Johnson's eyes—a need to be heard as himself, not as someone's projection of who he should be, not as a trophy to be displayed or an investment to be protected, but as a seventeen-year-old boy who'd discovered that music could be salvation if you were brave enough to let it.
"You're Maxanne Sartori," he said, his expression carrying wonder like he'd just recognized a celebrity, except the celebrity was someone who might actually understand what he was trying to say. "You play music that matters."
Music that matters. Not the program music his parents approved of, not the cultural vegetables he was supposed to consume for his own good, but music that felt like life instead of homework.
"I play music," I corrected gently, while carefully positioning the next cart with the precision that's become second nature. "Sometimes it comes from famous bands. Sometimes it comes from kids in garages. And sometimes," I looked directly at Dr. Marsh, letting him understand exactly what I thought of his careful plans, "it comes from piano prodigies who understand that Chopin would have loved electric guitars if he'd lived to see them."
The evening ended quickly after that. The champagne suddenly tasted flat, the hors d'oeuvres lost their appeal, and the audience began making those polite excuses that wealthy people had perfected—early morning meetings, babysitter schedules, sudden headaches that required immediate departure.
But before I left, I gave Jackie my card—the same one I'd given Nick Johnson three days earlier, though I didn't mention that connection because some ironies are too complicated to explain in a living room full of people who wouldn't understand them anyway.
"Come by the station," I told him quietly while his parents said goodbye to guests and pretended the evening had gone according to plan. "Bring whatever music lives in you, not what lives in your parents' expectations."
Later, driving back through Sudbury toward Boston, I thought about what I'd witnessed. Two boys on opposite sides of Hop Brook, both prisoners of expectations—one expected to fail, the other expected to succeed, both trapped by predictions that left no room for who they actually were or what they might become if given the chance to choose their own cages.
But here's the cruelest irony of all: I thought I was saving two boys from the boxes they'd been born into. I didn't realize I was setting them on a collision course that would prove boxes are easier to escape than each other, that sometimes the cruelest prison is the one that looks like freedom, that sounds like music, that promises to rescue you from everything except the thing that matters most—the distance between who you are and who you're supposed to be.
Back in 2024, I drain another glass of water and think about Jackie Marsh at seventeen, playing "Under Pressure" in his parents' living room like his life depended on it. Which, in a way, it did. Because that was the last time he would play music like it mattered more than approval, more than success, more than the careful preservation of advantages he'd never asked for but couldn't afford to lose.
Sometimes the most dangerous thing you can do is show people who you really are. And sometimes the most tragic thing is discovering that being yourself is a luxury you can't afford, especially when everyone around you has invested so heavily in the person they think you should be.
