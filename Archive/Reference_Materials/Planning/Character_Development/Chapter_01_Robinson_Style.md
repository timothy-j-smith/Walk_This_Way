# Chapter 1: The Last Voice of Boston Rock
## Marilynne Robinson Style

The thing about endings is that they make liars of us all, though we do not mean to lie, and perhaps that is the most terrible thing about them—that they arrive with such solemnity, such finality, that we feel obliged to speak words that will somehow encompass all that has been, all that we have meant to one another, when in truth we are only human, only finite, and our words, however carefully chosen, however sincerely offered, can never quite capture the fullness of what we have lived through together.

Here I sit, Maxanne Sartori, seventy-three years old now, in this same Studio C where once I watched Steven Tyler's hair become entangled in the mixing board, back in 1973, when the air was thick with cigarette smoke and with something else, too—a kind of desperation, yes, but also a wild hope, a belief that what we were doing in this room mattered more than anything else in the world. The equipment around me wheezes its last tonight, these turntables I fought to keep relevant while the men in suits pressed for automation, these cart machines that hold within their magnetic tape the very DNA of every Boston band that ever mattered, these mixing boards that have channeled more raw talent than any studio in America has ever seen. Tomorrow they will be sold for scrap, or perhaps shipped to museums where teenagers will stare at them with the blank incomprehension of those who have never known a world where sound was something you could hold in your hands, where music was something that happened in real time, where the voice of a disc jockey could shape the dreams of an entire city.

WBCN dies at midnight. After fifty-six years of being the Rock of Boston—the station that launched Aerosmith when they were just boys from New Hampshire, boys with more attitude than money, more dreams than sense—we are flipping to automated Christian contemporary. The program director's voice crackles through the intercom like a bad phone connection, though he is only twenty-eight years old and wears sneakers that cost more than my first car, that 1967 Mustang I bought with my first WBCN paycheck. He thinks rock history began with Nirvana, this child. He probably thinks Aerosmith was just some old band his father listened to, not understanding that they were once the voice of a generation, the sound of America when it stopped pretending to be civilized.

"Maxanne?" he says. "You've got four hours left. Make them count."

Make them count. As if four hours could contain what WBCN has meant to this city that has lived and breathed rock and roll for more than half a century. As if I could somehow summarize fifty-six years of breaking bands, of late-night conversations with musicians who had just poured their souls into three-minute songs, of watching Boston transform from a city of steel and sweat into whatever it has become now. As if I could capture in words the moment when rock and roll was dangerous, when the voices of disc jockeys mattered more than algorithms, when two kids with guitars could change the world if only they could stop hating each other long enough to plug in their amplifiers. When the Red Sox breaking your heart every October was the only thing that united this city across class lines, across all the invisible boundaries that we have built to separate ourselves from one another.

I adjust the headphones—the same Sony MDR-7506s I have worn since 1971, held together now with electrical tape and pure stubbornness—and the ON AIR sign blinks red like a dying heartbeat. One last time.

"This is Maxanne Sartori," I say into the microphone, and my voice carries across the airwaves to whoever is still listening, "and for the next four hours, you are listening to the final broadcast of WBCN, 104.1 on your FM dial. I have been your guide through the evolution of Boston rock since before some of you were born—since the days when The Standells were the house band at the Boston Tea Party, since J. Geils was just a kid with a harmonica and a dream, since Aerosmith was four guys from New Hampshire who thought they could take on the world. I have spun the first singles from bands who went on to fill stadiums, and I have played demos from kids who never made it past their garage. But tonight, I want to tell you about the night I watched the future of rock and roll die in a high school cafeteria in Sudbury, Massachusetts. The night America chose division over harmony, when two kids who should have been allies became enemies over a question that seems quaint now: What is authentic rock and roll?"

I take a sip of bourbon—another violation of modern radio protocol, but who is going to fire me now?—and feel the warmth spread through my chest like liquid fire, the good kind that makes you remember why you started drinking in the first place. This is a bottle of Pappy Van Winkle that Steven Tyler himself sent when he heard about the station closing. "For the first lady who ever gave a damn," the note said, and I keep it still, folded carefully in my wallet like a talisman. Outside the booth windows, Boston glitters in the October darkness like broken glass in a parking lot—all glass towers and gentrification where dive bars used to breed bands, where the Rat used to host shows that would make your grandmother blush, where kids with nothing but dreams could still believe they had a shot at changing the world.

"You want to know why WBCN is dying?" I ask my invisible audience. "Why this is the very last broadcast? Let me tell you about two kids in 1979 who showed me exactly when American radio—and America itself—chose money over music, when we stopped believing that a kid with a guitar and a dream could matter more than a marketing budget."

The console lights pulse expectantly like a heartbeat on life support, and I have loaded carts with the soundtrack of a generation: Aerosmith's "Dream On," Queen's "Bohemian Rhapsody," The Cars' "Just What I Needed," J. Geils Band's "Centerfold." But first, I need to take you back to when those legends were just beginning to cast shadows, when the real battles were fought not in recording studios but in the divided territories of suburban high schools where children learned that geography was destiny, that the accident of where you were born determined what you could become.

"March, 1979," I continue. "Disco was dying its overdue death. The Clash were about to teach America that punk had something to say. And in Sudbury, Massachusetts—that's about twenty miles west of here for you newcomers—two seventeen-year-old boys were about to go to war over a question that seems quaint now: What is authentic rock and roll?"

I spin my chair to face the wall of albums, each one a memory, a story, a moment when music mattered more than metrics. The vinyl spines catch the booth's amber light like tombstones in a graveyard of sound, and my fingers find the right cart without looking—muscle memory from five decades of curating sound, my hands knowing these grooves better than my own face in the mirror.

Nick Johnson lived in a house where the mortgage was always late and dreams were luxuries you could not afford. The place smelled of desperation and deferred maintenance, of coffee grounds and cigarette smoke and the particular funk that comes from too many people living in too little space. His father had died in a construction accident two years earlier, crushed beneath a load of steel beams when a crane cable snapped—the kind of industrial tragedy that makes page three of the Metro section and gets forgotten by everyone except the families who eat silence for dinner afterward, who learn to live with the particular weight of absence that comes when someone you love dies in a way that could have been prevented, in a way that makes you wonder if the world is not simply indifferent to human suffering but actively hostile to it. The kind of death that leaves behind tools hanging on pegboards like a shrine to honest work, and a widow who comes home from Friendly's smelling like fryer oil and other people's happiness, and never once complains about her son spending grocery money on guitar strings because she understands, in some deep way that she cannot articulate, that music might be the only thing that can save him from the same fate that claimed his father.

Jackie Marsh lived less than a mile away as the crow flies, but between them stretched Hop Brook—named a century ago for its hops, the old mill long gone to modernization. The brook divided Sudbury like a scar, separating the haves from the have-nots with the casual cruelty of geography, with the way that water, which should unite us all, has instead become a boundary, a marker of difference, a reminder of the vast distances that can exist between people who live within sight of one another. In winter, children from both sides might skate the same frozen pond, their laughter carrying across the ice like church bells, but when the ice melted, they returned to their proper territories like migratory birds following invisible flight paths, following instincts they did not understand but could not resist. Jackie's father taught economics at Boston University, the kind of professor who wrote papers about the working class without ever living in their neighborhoods, who could analyze the economic geography of Sudbury's four quadrants without understanding that those invisible lines actually shaped real lives, real friendships, real possibilities. Jackie had a voice trained by the best teachers money could buy, fingers that had touched pianos worth more than Nick Johnson's entire house, and yet there was something in him, some hunger, some need, that all the privilege in the world could not satisfy.

"These boys should never have met," I tell my audience, my voice carrying the weight of forty-five years of watching America divide itself. "America designs its suburbs specifically to prevent these collisions—invisible lines drawn by property values, school districts, the careful architecture of keeping the classes in their proper lanes. But music has always been democracy's wild card, the signal that cuts through all the boundaries we build, all the walls we erect to protect ourselves from what we do not understand."

Through the booth window, I can see the billboard across Boylston Street advertising luxury condos where The Rat used to host bands too raw for radio, too real for the sanitized world that was coming. Twenty-four hundred square feet starting at 2.2 million—you could buy the entire block of Lansdowne Street for that in 1977. The Ramones had played that club back when Joey was so tall he had to duck under the pipes backstage, and the sound system was held together with duct tape and prayers, and somehow it worked, somehow it was perfect, because perfection in rock and roll has never been about technical excellence but about something else entirely, something that cannot be measured or quantified but only felt.

"I met them both the night of March fifteenth, 1979," I continue. "The Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School Battle of the Bands. I was there because the station had sponsored it, back when we could afford to care about local music, back when Clear Channel had not swallowed half the FM dial and algorithms had not replaced intuition, back when we still believed that the best music came from places we had never heard of, from people we had never met, from dreams we could not even imagine."

I take another sip of the bourbon, and it is smooth, expensive—a gift from Steven Tyler himself when he heard about the station closing. "For the first lady who ever gave a damn," the note had said, and he still remembered, even after all these years, even after becoming the biggest rock star in the world, he still remembered the kid from WBCN who had believed in four guys from New Hampshire before anyone else did, who had heard something in their music that the rest of the world was not ready for yet.

"I need you to understand something about 1979," I say, my voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "It was not just another year. It was the pivot point when rock and roll had to decide what it wanted to be. The corporations were circling, smelling money in what had been rebellion. MTV was three years away from making image matter more than sound. We were living in the last moments when two kids with nothing but talent and rage could matter as much as marketing budgets, when authenticity could still triumph over artifice, when the voice of the people could still be heard above the noise of commerce."

I load the first song—not Aerosmith, not The Cars or J. Geils Band, but a scratchy demo that never made it to vinyl. The tape hisses with age, the left channel crackles if you push it past six, but that imperfection is the point. This is not about perfect sound. This is about perfect truth.

"This is the only recording that exists of Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh playing together," I tell my audience. "Not from that night in 1979—no, this is from four years earlier, when they were thirteen and still believed harmony was possible between kids from different sides of the tracks. Listen to it. Listen to what we lost."

The demo starts—two young voices finding harmony over acoustic guitars, neither Aerosmith nor Queen but something uniquely theirs, something that existed only in the space between them, in the moment when their different worlds briefly touched and created something new, something beautiful, something that could not survive in the harsh light of reality. It lasts ninety-three seconds before cutting off mid-verse. That is all that survived.

"Ninety-three seconds," I repeat. "That is how much evidence I have that America chose division over music. The rest of the story? That is what the next four hours are for. So pour yourself something strong, turn off your phone, and let me tell you how the Battle of the Bands became the war that killed rock and roll's democratic promise."

The ON AIR sign pulses like a dying heartbeat. Four hours left to tell the truth, four hours left to make sense of forty-five years of watching America choose noise over harmony, choose division over unity, choose the easy path over the right one.

I crack my knuckles—arthritis now, too many decades of spinning vinyl—and lean into the microphone one more time. The bourbon's warmth spreads through my chest like liquid courage, the good kind that makes you tell stories you have been carrying too long, stories that have grown heavy with time and meaning, stories that demand to be told before it is too late.

"But first," I say, "we need to talk about a garage on Dutton Road, and why the ghost of a dead construction worker might have been the only adult who understood what was really at stake."
