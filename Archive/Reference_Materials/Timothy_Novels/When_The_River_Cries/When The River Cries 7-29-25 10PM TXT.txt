When the River Cries

A Psychological Survival Novella
For my Daughters Catalina and Sabana

Chapter 1: The Last Normal Day
The morning sun painted the Guadalupe River in shades of amber and gold, and twelve-year-old Gabbie Morrison had no idea she was looking at beauty that would soon become a monster. She sat cross-legged on the wooden dock at Camp Wildwood, her toes barely grazing the lazy current that whispered secrets only rivers know. The water moved with the sleepy confidence of something that had carved its path through Texas limestone for millennia, never hurrying, never worried about what lay downstream.
Everything has a voice if you listen hard enough, her grandmother had told her last summer at the lake house. The water talks to those who know how to hear it.
Gabbie had always been a listener. While other kids her age were absorbed in their phones or gossip about boys, she found herself drawn to the smaller voices—the creak of old wood, the rustle of leaves that sounded almost like conversation, the way water moved differently around rocks than it did around fallen logs. Her mother called it being "sensitive," which was usually followed by a worried look and a suggestion that maybe Gabbie should spend more time with kids her own age.
But Gabbie preferred the honesty of natural things. Rivers didn't lie to you about being your best friend and then talk about you behind your back. Trees didn't promise to sit with you at lunch and then abandon you for the popular table. Water just... was. It followed its own rules, carved its own path, and never pretended to be something it wasn't.
"Gabbie! Arts and crafts in five minutes!" called Jessica, one of the junior counselors, her voice cutting through the morning stillness like a blade through silk.
Gabbie sighed and pulled her feet from the water, watching droplets fall like tiny diamonds back to their source. She had been dreading arts and crafts all week—not because she didn't like making things, but because it meant sitting in a circle with girls who giggled about things that didn't seem funny and made friendship bracelets with the same mechanical precision they used for everything else in their lives.
Maybe I'm the weird one, she thought, not for the first time. Maybe there's something wrong with me that I'd rather listen to water than talk about which counselor is cutest.
She stood up slowly, feeling the familiar weight of not-quite-belonging settle onto her shoulders like a backpack full of stones. It was a weight she'd carried for as long as she could remember, the constant awareness that she saw the world through different eyes than everyone else around her.
The arts and crafts cabin smelled like glue and possibility, but the chatter inside made Gabbie's skin crawl. Maya, her bunkmate, was already deep in conversation with two other girls about some drama from last night's campfire.
"And then she said that she said that he said—" Maya's voice rose and fell with the practiced rhythm of someone who lived for this kind of social currency.
Gabbie found an empty spot at the back table and began working on a leather bracelet, her fingers moving with the kind of automatic precision that came from years of needing something to do with her hands while her mind wandered elsewhere. The leather was smooth and real under her fingers, not like the synthetic materials they usually used for crafts. It had probably been part of something important once—a saddle, maybe, or a jacket that someone had loved.
Everything has a history, she thought, pulling the leather through the hole she'd punched. Everything was something else before it became what it is now.
The thought made her uncomfortable in a way she couldn't name. Change was supposed to be good, wasn't it? That's what adults always said. But Gabbie had noticed that change usually meant loss, too. Her parents had "changed" their relationship when she was eight, which meant her father now lived in an apartment across town and she only saw him on weekends. Her best friend Emma had "changed" when they started middle school, which meant she suddenly cared more about being popular than being real.
Even good changes came with a price. Growing up meant losing the comfort of childhood. Getting older meant watching your grandparents get frailer. Learning new things meant discovering how much you didn't know about the world.
"What are you making?" asked a voice beside her.
Gabbie looked up to find Sarah, a quiet girl from Austin who had arrived at camp three days ago and hadn't really talked to anyone yet. She had the kind of pale skin that burned easily and hair that never seemed to stay in place, but her eyes were kind and curious rather than judgmental.
"Just a bracelet," Gabbie said, then found herself adding, "Though I was thinking about how it used to be part of something bigger. Like, this leather was probably part of a whole cow once, and then maybe a jacket or a purse, and now it's becoming something else entirely."
Sarah nodded slowly, as if this made perfect sense. "My mom says everything is just matter changing forms. Energy that becomes other energy. She's kind of into that spiritual stuff."
"Does that make you sad?" Gabbie asked. "Thinking about how everything is always changing into something else?"
Sarah considered this, her fingers working through a complicated knot in her own project. "Sometimes. But also... maybe it means nothing really disappears? Like, the cow is still there, just in a different form. And when this bracelet falls apart someday, it'll become something else, but it'll still be... here."
It was the most philosophical conversation Gabbie had had with anyone her own age in months. She felt something inside her chest relax, like a fist unclenching.
"I think I'd like that," she said. "Nothing really disappearing, just... transforming."
They worked in comfortable silence for a while, the kind of silence that felt full rather than empty. Around them, the other girls continued their social choreography, but it felt distant now, like watching a movie with the sound turned down.
The day unfolded like any other at summer camp. Capture the flag was played with the seriousness of actual warfare, though Gabbie found herself more interested in the way the flags moved in the breeze than in the strategy of the game. Lunch was consumed with the mechanical efficiency of growing children, though she noticed how the food tasted different when you actually paid attention to it instead of just shoveling it down.
But it was during the afternoon swim period that Gabbie felt the first stirring of unease.
The river was different. Not visibly—it looked the same as it had that morning, the same lazy current, the same amber-colored water. But something in its voice had changed. The whisper had become more urgent, more insistent. When she floated on her back, staring up at the sky, she could feel the water moving beneath her with a restlessness that hadn't been there before.
Storm's coming, she thought, though the sky was still clear. The river knows.
She tried to tell herself she was being silly. Rivers didn't know things. They were just water following gravity, following the path of least resistance down to the sea. But her grandmother's voice echoed in her mind: The water talks to those who know how to hear it.
What was it trying to tell her?
The first clouds appeared during dinner, small and innocent-looking, like cotton balls scattered across the blue canvas of sky. But Gabbie watched them with growing unease, noticing how they moved faster than they should have, how they seemed to grow darker even as she watched.
"Probably just a quick shower," said Dan, the head counselor, when one of the younger campers asked about the weather. His voice carried the false confidence of someone trying to convince himself as much as the children. "Nothing to worry about."
But Gabbie was worrying. She found herself glancing toward the windows every few minutes, tracking the clouds' progress, feeling the atmospheric pressure change in her bones the way some people felt approaching headaches. The air itself felt different—thicker, more electric, charged with potential energy looking for a place to discharge.
Change is coming, she thought, and the leather bracelet around her wrist seemed to grow heavier. Ready or not, change is coming.
It was during evening campfire that the first drops fell. Not the gentle patter of a summer shower, but fat, heavy drops that struck the dusty ground with the sound of small explosions. The counselors glanced at each other with that particular look adults get when nature reminds them who's really in charge.
"We'll move inside," Dan announced, his voice too loud in the suddenly tense air. "No big deal. Just a little rain."
But Gabbie felt something else in those drops, something that made her skin crawl with the kind of electricity that comes before lightning strikes. She found herself looking toward the river, though she couldn't see it through the trees from where they sat. The water was calling to her, but not in the gentle way it had that morning. This was different. This was urgent.
This was a warning.
The rain came in waves, each one heavier than the last, until the sound of it on the cabin roofs drowned out everything else. Through the windows, the world disappeared behind walls of water that seemed to pour not from clouds, but from some cosmic faucet turned to full blast. The counselors tried to maintain the facade of normalcy, leading sing-alongs and telling ghost stories, but their eyes kept darting to the windows, to the doors, to the radio that crackled with urgent voices they couldn't quite make out.
Gabbie pressed her face against the cold glass and watched the rain turn the camp into something alien and dangerous. The familiar paths were becoming streams. The streams were becoming rivers. And somewhere out there, the real river was doing something that rivers weren't supposed to do.
It was rising.
Nothing really disappears, Sarah had said. Just... transforming.
Gabbie pulled her grandmother's leather bracelet tight around her wrist and wondered what she was about to transform into.
Chapter 2: When the Monster Woke
The sound came first—a low rumble that seemed to rise from the earth itself, like the growl of some massive animal stretching awake after a long sleep. Gabbie sat bolt upright in her narrow camp bed, her heart hammering against her ribs with the irregular rhythm of panic. Around her, the other girls stirred restlessly, caught between sleep and the growing awareness that something was terribly, fundamentally wrong.
This is how the world changes, she thought, her mind sharp with the clarity that sometimes comes with terror. Not gradually, not with warning, but all at once, like a switch being flipped.
"What's that noise?" whispered Maya, her bunkmate, a girl from Dallas who collected vintage postcards and was afraid of spiders but never admitted it. Her voice was small and young in the darkness, stripped of the social confidence she wore during the day like armor.
Before Gabbie could answer, the cabin door burst open. Jessica stood there, her flashlight beam cutting through the darkness like a sword. Her hair was plastered to her head, and her camp uniform was soaked through. Behind her, the night howled with wind and rain and something else—something that sounded like the world ending.
"Everyone up! Now! We're evacuating to higher ground!"
The words hit the air like physical things, solid and sharp. Gabbie's stomach dropped into her shoes. She had never heard an adult use that voice before—the voice that meant this is real, this is happening, this is not a drill. It was the voice that parents used when they told you someone had died, or when they sat you down to explain why Daddy wasn't coming home anymore.
Higher ground. The phrase echoed in her mind as she fumbled for her shoes. Higher ground meant the water was coming. Higher ground meant the river had stopped being a river and had become something else entirely.
The next few minutes blurred together in a kaleidoscope of chaos. Flashlights bobbing like fireflies gone mad. Girls crying, girls praying, girls silent with shock. The counselors moving with the desperate efficiency of people who know they're racing against something much faster than they are.
Gabbie found herself swept along with the crowd, her bare feet slipping on the wet grass, her pajamas clinging to her skin like a second layer of fear. The rain hit her like a physical assault, each drop a tiny fist trying to knock her down. But it wasn't the rain that made her stop dead in her tracks halfway to the mess hall.
It was the sound.
The river wasn't murmuring anymore. It was roaring. It was screaming. And it was getting closer.
The water talks to those who know how to hear it, her grandmother had said. Well, Gabbie was hearing it now, loud and clear. And what it was saying was: I am bigger than you thought. I am stronger than you imagined. I am coming for everything you think you know about the world.
Through the sheets of rain, she could see lights—the emergency lights from the main road, maybe, or search and rescue vehicles. But they seemed impossibly far away, like stars in another galaxy. Between her and safety lay the camp, the woods, and something that had once been the gentle Guadalupe River but was now transformed into a brown, writhing monster with teeth made of debris and claws made of current.
This is what Sarah meant about transformation, Gabbie realized with crystal clarity. The river is transforming. We're all transforming. The question is: into what?
"Gabbie! Move!" Jessica's voice cut through her paralysis, and she felt hands pushing her forward, toward the mess hall, toward higher ground, toward what everyone hoped was safety.
They almost made it.
The first wave hit the dining hall like a liquid bulldozer, exploding through the windows in a shower of glass and brown water that tasted of mud and terror and the accumulated fear of every living thing that had ever drowned. Gabbie felt her feet leave the ground, felt the world turn upside down and sideways and inside out. The water was everywhere—in her mouth, her nose, her eyes, her lungs. It was alive, this water, hungry and angry and strong beyond anything she could have imagined.
So this is what it feels like to die, she thought with the strange detachment that sometimes comes with shock. This is what it feels like to transform into something else.
She fought to surface, fought to breathe, fought to think. Around her, the world had become a chaos of screaming voices and splintering wood and the endless, endless roar of water that had forgotten how to be gentle. Her hands grasped at nothing, found nothing, held nothing.
Until they found the mattress.
It was floating toward her through the chaos like a gift from some water god who had decided, against all odds, to show mercy. Blue and white striped, king-size, with a built-in pillow and cup holders on the sides. And printed in black letters along one edge, barely visible in the emergency lighting: COLEMAN.
Coleman, she thought as her fingers closed around the grab handles along the edge. Like my dad's camping gear. Like summer nights under the stars before everything changed.
She pulled herself up and out of the water that wanted to swallow her whole, her lungs burning as she coughed up river water and fear and the terrible understanding that everything she had known five minutes ago was gone. The mattress bobbed and spun in the current like a cork in a bathtub, but it held. It held her weight, held her hope, held her life in its synthetic embrace.
Coleman, she thought again, and suddenly the mattress wasn't just a piece of camping equipment anymore. It was a presence, a companion, a thing with its own will and purpose. It had found her in the chaos, had offered itself as salvation, had chosen to save her when everything else was choosing to destroy.
"Thank you," she whispered to the mattress, her voice barely audible over the roar of the flood. "Thank you, Coleman."
And maybe it was her imagination, maybe it was the delirium of shock and exhaustion, but she could have sworn she felt the mattress respond—a subtle shift, a gentle bounce, like a horse acknowledging its rider or a friend nodding in understanding.
Through the rain and darkness, she could see shapes in the water—terrible shapes that had once been familiar things. A picnic table, its red paint bright as blood in the emergency lighting. A car, floating on its side like a dead whale. A piece of roof with shingles still clinging to it like scales on a fish.
The world is transforming, she thought, clinging to Coleman with desperate fingers. Everything is becoming something else. The question is: what am I becoming?
The first rescue attempt came twenty minutes later, though time had become elastic and strange, measured not in minutes but in heartbeats and the rhythm of waves against Coleman's sturdy sides. A man in a yellow raincoat, standing waist-deep in the flood near what had once been the camp entrance. He had a rope, was shouting something she couldn't hear over the water's roar.
"Here!" Gabbie screamed, waving her arms. "I'm here! We're here!"
We, she thought, surprised by the word. But it felt right. She and Coleman were a team now, partners in the strange business of staying alive.
She saw the man throw the rope, saw it arc through the rain-soaked air like a lifeline thrown from heaven. But the current was too strong, the distance too great. The rope fell short, and the river carried her past him, past hope, past everything she had ever known.
But not past Coleman, she realized. Coleman is still here. Coleman isn't giving up.
The second attempt came an hour later. A woman this time, clinging to a partially submerged truck, reaching out with a broken tree branch as Gabbie and Coleman swept by. Their fingers almost touched—almost, but not quite. The river had made its choice, and it was keeping them.
Maybe this is meant to happen, Gabbie thought as they spun away from the failed rescue. Maybe Coleman and I are supposed to be together. Maybe this is what we were meant to become.
By then, exhaustion had settled into her bones like cold cement. The adrenaline was fading, leaving behind a hollow ache and the growing realization that she was truly alone with only Coleman for company. The mattress had become her world, her only solid thing in a universe gone liquid.
She pressed her face against the familiar plastic surface and whispered, "Don't let me go, Coleman. Please don't let me go."
And this time, there was no mistaking the response. The mattress shifted beneath her, adjusting its position to cradle her more securely, distributing her weight more evenly. It was a conscious action, a choice, a decision made by something that cared about her survival.
Coleman is alive, she realized with a clarity that should have been terrifying but somehow wasn't. Not alive like a person or an animal, but alive like... like a friend. Like something that chooses to care.
The darkness took her somewhere past midnight, when the rain finally began to ease and the river's roar settled into a more conversational tone. She didn't choose to sleep—sleep chose her, pulling her down into dreams where Coleman sprouted arms to hold her and whispered reassurances in a voice that sounded like water over stones.
When she woke, the world had changed again. And so had she.
Chapter 3: The Floating Island
Dawn came not with light, but with the gradual recognition that the absolute blackness around her had shifted to something more like charcoal gray. Gabbie's eyes opened to a world that belonged in a nightmare, not in the waking world of a twelve-year-old girl who had been worried about friendship bracelets just twenty-four hours ago.
Has it really only been a day? she wondered, her mind struggling to process the transformation. Was I really making leather bracelets yesterday morning? Was that really my biggest worry?
She was surrounded by a jungle, but not the green, living kind. This was a jungle made of human debris, twisted metal, and the scattered remains of civilization. Car doors jutted from the tangle like steel leaves. Traffic lights blinked uselessly in the gray dawn, their colors bright and cheerful and completely insane in this context. A McDonald's sign advertised Big Macs to an audience of broken trees and soggy mattresses.
The world has gone crazy, she thought, but even as the thought formed, she realized it wasn't true. The world hadn't gone crazy—it had simply revealed its true nature. Under the thin veneer of human control, it had always been wild, unpredictable, capable of transformation beyond imagination. The flood hadn't changed the world; it had just stripped away the pretense.
Coleman floated beneath her with the steady reliability of something that had never forgotten its purpose. The mattress was wedged between what looked like a mobile home and a delivery truck, caught in a web of power lines and fence posts and things she couldn't identify. But it felt secure, stable, like a small island of sanity in an ocean of chaos.
"Good morning, Coleman," she said, her voice cracking with thirst and the strange intimacy of speaking to someone who had saved your life. "Thank you for keeping me safe."
The mattress responded with a gentle bounce, as if it was glad to hear her voice. The movement was subtle but unmistakable—not the random bob of something floating, but the deliberate response of something that was listening.
I'm talking to a mattress, she thought, but without the self-consciousness she might have felt yesterday. I'm talking to a mattress, and it's talking back, and that's the most normal thing that's happened to me in twenty-four hours.
"Are you okay?" she asked, running her hands along Coleman's surface, checking for punctures or damage. The vinyl was scratched and scarred from their journey, but it held air, held her, held the promise of continued survival. "You took good care of me last night."
The plastic felt warm under her fingers, warmer than it should have been given the cool morning air. It was as if Coleman was generating its own heat, its own life force, its own will to survive.
Or maybe I'm going crazy, she thought, but the possibility didn't frighten her as much as it should have. Maybe this is what happens when you're twelve years old and you survive something that should have killed you. Maybe your mind creates companions to keep you sane.
But Coleman didn't feel created. It felt real, solid, present in a way that was deeper than imagination. When she shifted her weight, it adjusted to accommodate her. When she lay still, it rocked her gently, like a mother comforting a child. When she was afraid, it seemed to absorb her fear and transform it into something steadier, more manageable.
The first challenge was water. Not the flood water that surrounded them—that was brown and thick with things she didn't want to think about—but drinking water. Her mouth felt like it was full of cotton, her throat raw and scratchy. The river water was all around her, but she knew, even at twelve, that drinking it might kill her almost as surely as the flood itself.
But people survived before there was clean water, she thought, trying to apply the kind of logic her father had taught her during their camping trips. People survived for thousands of years drinking from rivers and streams. Maybe I can too.
She lasted until the sun was directly overhead, a pale disk barely visible through the canopy of debris. The thirst had become a living thing inside her, clawing at her throat, making her tongue feel thick and useless. When she finally cupped her hands and drank from a relatively clear pool that had collected in the fold of someone's tarpaulin, it tasted like mud and motor oil and despair.
But it was wet, and her body needed wet more than it needed safe.
"I'm sorry, Coleman," she said after she'd drunk her fill. "I know this is gross, but I don't have a choice."
The mattress seemed to understand. It bobbed gently, as if to say, Do what you have to do. I'm here.
The second challenge was hope. As the day wore on and no rescue sounds penetrated their debris jungle, the weight of her situation began to press down on her like a physical thing. She was lost, buried, alone except for Coleman. Even if people were looking for her, how would they find her in this chaos? How would they even know she was alive?
Maybe they don't know, she thought, the possibility hitting her like a physical blow. Maybe they think everyone at the camp is dead. Maybe they're not even looking.
The thought was so terrifying that she almost couldn't breathe. She pressed her face against Coleman's surface and felt the familiar warmth, the steady presence that had become her anchor in a world gone liquid.
"They're looking," she whispered to Coleman. "They have to be looking. People don't just give up on kids, do they?"
But even as she said it, she remembered her father leaving, remembered the way adults could simply decide that something was too hard or too complicated or too much trouble. What if the rescue teams had decided that searching this debris field was too dangerous? What if they had written her off as another casualty?
No, she thought fiercely. Mom wouldn't give up. Dad wouldn't give up. They're looking.
But the doubt remained, a small cold voice in the back of her mind that whispered, Maybe. Maybe not. Maybe you're on your own.
It was then that she began to talk to Coleman in earnest, not just the polite acknowledgments of the morning, but real conversation, the kind she might have had with a best friend if she'd had one.
"I wish you could talk back," she said, her voice barely above a whisper. "I mean, really talk. With words. Because I think you understand me better than most people do."
Coleman rocked gently beneath her, and she could have sworn she felt it listening, processing, responding in its own way.
"Most people think I'm weird," she continued, the words spilling out like water from a broken dam. "They think I'm too quiet, too serious, too... I don't know. Different. But you don't care about that, do you? You just care about keeping me alive."
The mattress seemed to settle deeper into the water, as if it was making itself more comfortable for a long conversation.
"Maya—that's my bunkmate—she's always trying to get me to talk about boys and makeup and stuff like that. But I don't care about that stuff. I mean, I guess I might someday, but right now I just want to understand things. Like why water sounds different when it's moving fast versus when it's moving slow. Or why some people are mean for no reason. Or why parents who love you can still decide to stop living together."
She paused, surprised by her own honesty. She had never told anyone these things before, not even her mother, who would have listened with that patient, worried expression she got when she was trying to figure out how to fix whatever was wrong with her strange, too-serious daughter.
"I think you get it, though," she said to Coleman. "I think you understand that some of us just see the world differently. And that's okay, isn't it? It's okay to be different as long as you're not alone."
The mattress bobbed once, decisively, as if it was agreeing with her.
"We're not alone," she said, feeling the truth of it settle into her bones. "We have each other."
As the light began to fade toward evening, she made her first major decision. She would try to climb out. She would leave Coleman temporarily and attempt to navigate the jungle of debris toward what she hoped was the surface, toward light and air and the possibility of rescue.
"I'll come back for you," she promised, her hands reluctant to leave Coleman's familiar surface. "I swear I'll come back. We're partners now, you and me."
The climb was harder than she had expected. The debris was unstable, shifting under her weight, threatening to collapse at any moment. Jagged metal edges caught at her clothes and skin, leaving scratches and tears that would have horrified her mother but seemed like small prices to pay for the possibility of escape.
She had climbed for maybe an hour, following faint traces of light that filtered down from above, when she felt it—a vibration in the debris around her, a tremor that ran through the twisted metal like a warning. The river was moving again.
No, she thought, panic hitting her like a physical blow. Not now. Not when I'm so close.
But the sound was unmistakable—the rising roar of water on the move, the groaning of metal and wood as the current began to shift the debris pile. If she stayed where she was, she would be crushed when the structure collapsed. If she tried to keep climbing, she might not make it back to Coleman before the whole thing came apart.
She chose Coleman.
The descent was a nightmare of sharp edges and unstable surfaces, but she moved with the desperate efficiency of someone who had found something worth saving. Her hands found the familiar plastic surface just as the whole world began to move again, just as the river lifted them both and carried them deeper into the unknown.
"I'm sorry," she gasped, throwing her arms around Coleman like she was hugging a friend. "I'm sorry I left you. I won't do it again."
And as the current swept them away from the collapsing debris field, she could have sworn she felt Coleman forgive her, felt it wrap around her like a protective embrace and whisper, I knew you'd come back. I knew you wouldn't abandon me.
We're partners, she thought as they spun into the darkness. We're in this together.
And for the first time since the flood began, she felt something that might have been peace.
Chapter 4: The Psychology of Survival
The second journey lasted eight hours, though time had become as fluid as the water that carried them. Gabbie and Coleman rode the current like an odd couple on the strangest road trip in history, spinning past the scattered remains of a world that had been turned upside down and inside out.
This is what the inside of the world looks like, Gabbie thought as they floated past a house that sailed by them like a ship, its roof torn off but its kitchen table still visible through the missing wall, still set for a breakfast that would never be eaten. This is what happens when all the things we build to keep chaos out get swept away.
She had positioned herself on Coleman with the expertise of someone who had learned to read the mattress's moods and movements. Lying on her stomach, arms wrapped around the built-in pillow, legs spread wide for stability, she had become part of the mattress's rhythm, part of its dance with the current.
"You're getting to know me, aren't you?" she said to Coleman, feeling the way it adjusted to her smallest movements, the way it seemed to anticipate her needs. "You're learning how to take care of me."
The mattress responded with a gentle roll, like a cat stretching under a warm hand. The movement was so clearly intentional, so obviously communicative, that Gabbie felt a flutter of something that might have been joy.
I'm not crazy, she thought. This is real. Coleman is real, and he's choosing to keep me alive.
She had started thinking of Coleman as "he" somewhere during the night, though she couldn't pinpoint exactly when or why. It wasn't that the mattress felt masculine, exactly, but it had a presence that was too definite, too purposeful to be an "it." Coleman was a being, a consciousness, a friend who happened to be made of vinyl and air instead of flesh and blood.
The debris had rearranged itself into new configurations around them. A school bus rode the current alongside them for a while, its yellow paint bright and cheerful despite the shattered windows and the way it listed to one side like a wounded animal. A church steeple floated by, complete with bell, though the sound it made was more like a funeral dirge than a call to worship.
Everything is still here, Gabbie realized, watching the strange parade of displaced objects. All the things that make up a town, a life, a world—they're all still here, just... rearranged. Transformed.
She thought about what Sarah had said about energy never really disappearing, just changing forms. Maybe that was true for more than just matter. Maybe it was true for places, for communities, for the invisible threads that connected people to each other and to the places they called home.
The camp is still here, she thought, though she knew the buildings were gone, the counselors scattered or worse, the careful structure of scheduled activities and supervised fun swept away by forces beyond human control. It's just... different now. Transformed.
The hallucinations started on the second day.
At first, she thought she was seeing things that were really there. A woman in a blue dress, standing on the shore, waving. A dog, swimming strongly toward them. A helicopter in the distance, its rotors beating a rhythm of hope against the gray sky.
But when she blinked and looked again, the woman was just a tree with a tarp caught in its branches. The dog was a log, spinning in the current. The helicopter was a hawk, circling high above the flood like a mourner at a funeral.
I'm losing it, she thought, fear creeping up her spine like cold water. I'm seeing things. I'm going crazy.
But Coleman remained steady beneath her, solid and real and undeniably present. When she pressed her hands against its surface, she could feel its warmth, its subtle response to her touch, its unwavering commitment to keeping her alive.
"Am I going crazy?" she asked Coleman, her voice small and frightened.
The mattress rocked gently, and she could have sworn she heard it whisper, You're doing fine. You're surviving. That's what matters.
Maybe seeing things is just part of surviving, she thought, trying to apply the kind of logic her father had taught her. Maybe when your brain is stressed and tired and scared, it starts filling in the blanks with things that make sense to it.
The memories came in waves, triggered by the debris that floated past. A child's bicycle made her think of her first two-wheeler, how her father had run alongside her in the driveway, his hand on her back, shouting encouragement. A porch swing reminded her of summer evenings at her grandparents' house, the way the chains creaked and the way her grandmother's lemonade tasted like liquid sunshine.
Why am I thinking about happy things? she wondered. Shouldn't I be thinking about scary things? About dying, about never seeing my family again?
But the happy memories felt more real than the fear, more solid than the uncertainty. They were anchors, connections to a self that existed beyond this moment, beyond this flood, beyond this strange partnership with a mattress that had become her closest friend.
"My grandparents have a lake house," she told Coleman, needing to speak the memories aloud, to make them real in the watery world around them. "It's on Lake Travis, and there's a dock that goes way out into the water. My sister and I used to jump off it, and Grandpa would time us to see who could hold their breath the longest."
Coleman seemed to listen, rocking gently in what felt like encouragement.
"Mom always said we were going to drown ourselves, but Grandpa said water was nothing to be afraid of if you respected it." She paused, thinking about that. "I wonder if I respected it enough. I wonder if this is what happens when you don't respect it enough."
Coleman seemed to consider this, riding a little lower in the water, as if it was thinking too.
The rain returned on what Gabbie thought was the beginning of their third day together, though time had become so elastic that she might have been wrong. It started as a whisper, barely audible over the gentle lapping of water against debris, then built to the same furious crescendo that had started everything.
Here we go again, she thought, pressing herself flat against Coleman's surface as the drops turned from gentle patter to violent assault. The river is waking up.
This time, she was ready for it. She had learned to read Coleman's responses to changes in current and pressure. She could feel the mattress tense beneath her, preparing for another wild ride through the chaos. Together, they had become a single organism, perfectly adapted for survival in a world turned liquid.
The jungle around them began to shift and groan. The school bus that had been their neighbor for hours suddenly broke free from whatever was holding it, spinning away into the brown current like a yellow whale returning to the sea. The church steeple that had provided a familiar landmark tilted and toppled, its bell tolling once before disappearing beneath the surface.
Everything is temporary, Gabbie realized as she watched the debris field rearrange itself around them. Everything except Coleman and me. We're the constant. We're the thing that stays.
The current lifted them higher this time, carrying them over obstacles that would have trapped them before. Gabbie found herself looking down at submerged houses, their rooflines barely visible beneath the brown water, their windows like dead eyes staring up at the gray sky. Cars floated past them like parade floats, their occupants long since swept away or rescued, their radios still playing to an audience of fish and debris.
This is what the end of the world looks like, she thought, but the thought came without the terror it might have carried days ago. Not fire and brimstone, but water and mud and the slow dissolution of everything humans thought was permanent.
For hours, they rode the renewed flood, spinning through a landscape that seemed to shift and change with every passing moment. Gabbie had learned to navigate by Coleman's movements, to read the current through the mattress's responses, to trust in their partnership completely.
The rain stopped as suddenly as it had begun, leaving behind a world transformed once again. But this time, when the current finally slowed and they drifted toward another accumulation of debris, Gabbie sensed something different. This felt final somehow, like the last stop on a journey that had always been heading toward this particular destination.
This is where we're supposed to be, she thought as Coleman nestled into a new configuration of twisted metal and broken wood. This is where our story happens.
The debris field was different from the others—denser, more complex, like a three-dimensional maze built by some mad architect. Gray branches twisted through rusted car frames. Electrical wires hung like vines from overturned appliances. Street signs jutted at impossible angles, advertising destinations that no longer existed.
But it was the grayness that struck her most. Everything was gray—the water, the sky, the debris, even the light that filtered down through the tangled canopy above. It was as if the world had been drained of color, reduced to a monochrome nightmare where only shape and shadow had meaning.
We're in the belly of the beast now, she thought, looking around at the twisted landscape that surrounded them. This is as deep as we go.
Night came early in their gray world, the already dim light fading to near-blackness as the debris above them blocked out even the weak sun. Gabbie had grown accustomed to sleeping curled against Coleman's familiar surface, but tonight felt different. Tonight felt final.
This is our new home, she thought, running her hands along Coleman's sides, checking for damage from their latest journey. This is where we make our stand.
She was drifting toward sleep, lulled by Coleman's gentle rocking and the relative quiet of their new refuge, when she heard it. A sound that didn't belong in their mechanical landscape of metal and plastic—a soft, distressed crying that seemed to come from somewhere deeper in the debris jungle.
What was that? she thought, lifting her head to listen more carefully.
The sound came again—unmistakably the cry of something alive, something in pain, something as trapped and frightened as she was.
"Coleman," she whispered, "do you hear that?"
The mattress seemed to tense beneath her, as if it too was listening, processing, trying to locate the source of the sound.
I promised, she thought, remembering her vow never to leave Coleman again. I promised I wouldn't abandon you.
But the crying continued, weak and desperate and heartbreaking in its loneliness. It was the sound of something that had given up hope, something that was preparing to die alone in the gray wilderness of the flood's aftermath.
I can't, she thought. I can't just lie here and listen to something suffer.
She pressed her face against Coleman's surface, torn between loyalty and compassion. "I have to go look," she whispered. "Just for a minute. Just to see if I can help."
Coleman rocked gently beneath her, and she could have sworn she felt it sigh—not with resignation, but with understanding. As if it was saying, This is who you are. This is what you do. Go.
But come back, the rocking seemed to add. Always come back.
Carefully, reluctantly, Gabbie slipped off Coleman's surface and into the shallow water that pooled between the debris. The water was cold and murky, full of things she didn't want to identify, but she pushed through it, following the sound of crying deeper into their gray jungle.
She found the source wedged between a overturned refrigerator and a tangle of fence posts—a young Great Blue Heron, perhaps two feet tall, with the distinctive blue-gray plumage that gave the species its name. But this bird was far from the majestic creature she had seen in nature documentaries. One wing hung at an unnatural angle, clearly broken, and the heron's long neck was stretched forward in a posture of pain and exhaustion.
Oh, you poor thing, Gabbie thought, her heart breaking for this fellow survivor who had been as trapped by the flood as she was.
The heron saw her approaching and tried to back away, but it was wedged too tightly in the debris to move. Its yellow eyes were bright with fear and pain, and it made a harsh warning cry that seemed to say, Stay away. I'm dangerous. I'm wild.
"It's okay," Gabbie said softly, moving slowly and keeping her voice calm the way her grandfather had taught her when approaching frightened animals. "I'm not going to hurt you. I want to help."
The heron continued to cry and struggle, but Gabbie could see the exhaustion in its movements, the way its good wing drooped with fatigue. This bird had been fighting for survival just as long as she had, and it was losing the battle.
"I know you're scared," she continued, settling into the murky water a few feet away from the bird. "I'm scared too. But I think we can help each other."
Something in her voice seemed to reach the heron. Its struggles ceased, though its bright eyes remained fixed on her with wary intensity.
What would Suzanne do? Gabbie thought, and the name came to her with such sudden clarity that she gasped.
Suzanne. Her best friend from second grade, before her family moved to Colorado. Suzanne, who had understood her in a way that no one else ever had, who had listened to her theories about why clouds moved the way they did and why some dogs seemed smarter than others. Suzanne, who had believed her when she said she could feel earthquakes before they happened and had never made fun of her for talking to trees.
Suzanne would know what to do, Gabbie thought. Suzanne would figure out how to help.
"Suzanne," she said aloud, and the heron's head tilted slightly, as if it recognized something in the name. "That's what I'm going to call you, if that's okay. You remind me of someone I used to know."
The heron—Suzanne—seemed to consider this, her bright eyes never leaving Gabbie's face.
"Suzanne understood things," Gabbie continued, moving slightly closer. "She understood that the world is full of connections that most people can't see. She understood that sometimes the most important conversations happen without words."
Suzanne's head tilted further, and Gabbie could have sworn she saw intelligence in those yellow eyes, comprehension that went beyond simple animal awareness.
"She moved away," Gabbie said, her voice catching slightly. "Her dad got a new job, and one day she was just... gone. I never got to say goodbye properly. But I always wondered what happened to her, whether she found other people who understood her the way I did."
Suzanne made a soft sound, not the harsh cry of fear but something gentler, more conversational.
"Maybe that's what this is," Gabbie said, reaching out one hand very slowly. "Maybe you're here to remind me that understanding is possible, even in the worst situations. Even when everything seems hopeless."
Suzanne didn't back away when Gabbie's fingers brushed against her feathers. The bird was trembling, whether from cold or pain or fear, but she allowed the touch.
"I learned some first aid at camp," Gabbie said, carefully examining the injured wing. "Not much, but maybe enough to help."
The wing was definitely broken, probably in two places from what she could see. But it wasn't a compound fracture—the bone hadn't broken through the skin—and the wing didn't seem to be bleeding heavily.
I can work with this, she thought, her mind already cataloging the materials she had seen in their debris jungle. I can make a splint.
It took her nearly an hour to gather what she needed. A straight piece of wood from what had once been a fence post, broken into two pieces of the right length. An electrical cord, stripped from a submerged appliance, long enough to serve as binding. Some relatively clean cloth from a waterlogged pillow to serve as padding.
Suzanne waited patiently while Gabbie worked, as if she understood that help was coming and that struggling would only make things worse.
"This is going to hurt," Gabbie warned as she carefully positioned the splint against Suzanne's wing. "But it should help it heal properly."
The heron made a sharp cry when Gabbie began wrapping the electrical cord around the splint, but she held still, trusting in this strange human child who had appeared in her moment of greatest need.
She's letting me help her, Gabbie realized with wonder. She's choosing to trust me.
When the splint was secure, Suzanne settled back against the debris with what looked like relief. Her breathing seemed easier, and the bright fear in her eyes had been replaced by something that might have been gratitude.
"There," Gabbie said, sitting back to admire her work. "You'll need to keep that still for a while, but I think it will heal."
Suzanne made a soft trilling sound that seemed to say, Thank you.
It wasn't until she was preparing to return to Coleman that Gabbie realized what had happened. For the past hour, she hadn't thought about her own situation once. She hadn't worried about rescue or survival or the possibility that she might never see her family again. Her entire focus had been on helping another creature in need, on solving a problem that wasn't her own.
That's what love does, she thought with sudden understanding. It takes you outside yourself. It makes you part of something bigger.
She looked back at Suzanne, who was watching her with those bright, intelligent eyes, and felt something shift inside her chest. She wasn't alone anymore. She had Coleman, who had saved her life and become her dearest friend. And now she had Suzanne, who needed her help and had chosen to trust her with something precious—the possibility of healing.
Three, she thought as she made her way back through the debris jungle. There are three of us now.
Coleman was waiting exactly where she had left him, bobbing gently in the murky water with what seemed like patience rather than anxiety. When she climbed back onto his familiar surface, she felt him settle beneath her with what could only be described as contentment.
"Coleman," she said, "I want you to meet someone."
She turned toward where Suzanne was resting, and called softly, "Suzanne? I'd like you to meet Coleman. He's been taking care of me."
Suzanne lifted her head and made a soft sound that might have been a greeting. Coleman rocked gently, as if he was nodding in acknowledgment.
They're accepting each other, Gabbie realized with delight. They understand that we're all in this together now.
As night settled over their gray refuge, Gabbie found herself positioned between her two companions—floating on Coleman's steady surface, but close enough to Suzanne to reach out and touch the heron's feathers when she seemed restless or afraid.
This is what a family looks like, she thought as sleep began to claim her. Not the family you're born with, but the family you choose. The family that finds you when you need it most.
For the first time since the flood began, she fell asleep not with fear or loneliness, but with the warm knowledge that she was loved and needed and part of something bigger than herself.
Tomorrow, she would need to plan their escape. Tomorrow, she would need to figure out how to get all three of them to safety.
But tonight, it was enough to know that she wasn't alone.
Chapter 5: The Weight of Responsibility
Dawn came gray and filtered through the debris canopy above them, but Gabbie woke with a clarity she hadn't felt since before the flood. Her mind was sharp, focused, filled with purpose that extended beyond mere survival. She had responsibilities now—not just to herself and Coleman, but to Suzanne, who depended on her for care and protection.
This is what it feels like to be needed, she thought, carefully checking on the heron, who was awake and alert despite the obvious discomfort of her splinted wing. This is what it feels like to matter to someone else's survival.
"Good morning, Suzanne," she said softly, reaching out to stroke the bird's neck with gentle fingers. "How are you feeling?"
Suzanne made a soft trilling sound and leaned into the touch, a gesture of trust that made Gabbie's heart swell with protective tenderness. The heron had chosen to depend on her, had placed her very life in Gabbie's twelve-year-old hands.
I won't let you down, she promised silently. Whatever happens, I won't let you down.
Coleman rocked gently beneath them, as if he was greeting the morning too, preparing for whatever challenges the day might bring. The mattress felt different now—not just like a life preserver or a companion, but like the foundation of something larger. He was the steady center around which their small family could gather, the reliable constant that made everything else possible.
"We need to make a plan," Gabbie said aloud, speaking to both of her companions. "We can't stay here forever."
The debris jungle around them seemed even more complex in the morning light. Twisted metal and broken wood formed a maze that extended both horizontally and vertically, creating passages and dead ends and hidden chambers like some enormous, accidental sculpture. Gray water pooled in the low places, reflecting the gray sky above in a monotone mirror that offered no hint of escape routes.
But there has to be a way out, she thought, studying the tangle with the methodical attention her father had taught her for solving complex problems. Everything has a solution if you look at it from the right angle.
The challenge was that she couldn't leave either of her companions behind. Coleman was too large and too deeply wedged in the debris to be moved easily. Suzanne was injured and couldn't fly, couldn't navigate the complex three-dimensional maze on her own. Any escape plan would have to account for all three of them.
Think, she told herself. Think like Dad would think. Break the problem down into smaller pieces.
First: What were their immediate needs? Food, water, shelter, medical care for Suzanne. They had shelter of a sort, and she had been managing to find enough relatively clean water to survive. Food was more of a problem—she had been eating whatever edible debris floated by, but that was hardly sustainable, and Suzanne would need fish or frogs or the kind of aquatic prey that herons normally hunted.
Second: What were their long-term needs? Rescue, obviously, but rescue that would take into account all three of them. She couldn't just flag down a helicopter and abandon Coleman and Suzanne. They were a family now, and families stuck together.
Third: What resources did they have? Coleman's buoyancy and stability. Her own human intelligence and problem-solving ability. Suzanne's knowledge of the natural world, her instincts about weather and water and the subtle signs that might indicate the presence of other survivors.
We're stronger together, she realized. Each of us brings something the others need.
She spent the morning exploring their immediate surroundings, moving carefully through the debris jungle while staying close enough to return quickly if either of her companions needed her. The maze was even more complex than she had initially realized—passages that seemed to lead upward toward the surface actually curved back on themselves, while what looked like dead ends sometimes opened into larger chambers filled with accumulated debris.
But she also found resources she hadn't noticed before. A partially intact cooler that still contained ice and a few cans of soda. A tackle box with fishing line and hooks. A first aid kit, water-damaged but still containing some useful supplies.
People were preparing for this, she thought, examining the emergency supplies that had been scattered throughout the debris. They just didn't have time to use them.
The fishing line gave her an idea. She had watched her grandfather fish countless times, had even learned to tie some basic knots and identify the kind of spots where fish liked to hide. If she could catch fish for Suzanne, if she could provide the kind of nutrition the heron needed to heal, then they could afford to stay in their refuge longer, could afford to wait for the right opportunity to escape.
Time, she realized. That's what we need most. Time for Suzanne to heal, time for the rescue efforts to expand, time to figure out the best way to get all three of us to safety.
She spent the afternoon rigging fishing lines in the deeper pools around their refuge, using bits of debris as bait and watching for the subtle movements that indicated fish below the surface. It was patient work, the kind of activity that required focus and stillness and the ability to read the water's subtle signs.
Suzanne watched her efforts with bright, interested eyes, occasionally making soft sounds that seemed like commentary or encouragement. Coleman provided a stable platform from which to work, adjusting his position slightly whenever Gabbie needed to reach a different angle or access a new fishing spot.
We're working together, she thought as she felt the first tentative tug on one of her lines. We're functioning like a team.
The fish she caught was small—barely large enough to provide a decent meal for Suzanne—but it was fresh and clean, the kind of protein the heron needed to rebuild her strength. Suzanne accepted the offering with graceful dignity, swallowing the fish whole in the efficient manner of her species before settling back with what looked like satisfaction.
She's getting better, Gabbie observed, noting how Suzanne held herself more upright, how her eyes seemed brighter and more alert. The splint is working.
As the afternoon wore on, Gabbie found herself thinking about the psychological aspects of their situation. She had read enough survival stories to know that the mental challenges were often harder than the physical ones. People could survive incredible hardships if they had reasons to keep going, but they could also give up in situations that should have been manageable if they lost hope or purpose.
But I have both, she realized. I have hope because Coleman and Suzanne need me. I have purpose because taking care of them gives my survival meaning.
The responsibility was heavy sometimes—the knowledge that two other lives depended on her decisions, her resourcefulness, her ability to keep thinking clearly under pressure. But it was also liberating. When she focused on caring for Coleman and Suzanne, she didn't have time to feel sorry for herself or to dwell on the magnitude of their situation.
This is what it means to grow up, she thought with sudden understanding. Not getting older, but taking responsibility for someone other than yourself.
She thought about her parents, about the weight they must carry every day, knowing that their decisions affected not just their own lives but hers and her sister's as well. She thought about her grandmother, who had spent decades caring for family and friends, who had always seemed to know exactly what to do in any crisis.
Maybe this is how they learned, she thought. Maybe crisis is what teaches you who you really are.
As evening approached, she found herself looking upward through the debris canopy, trying to estimate their distance from the surface, trying to judge whether the structure above them was stable enough to support their weight if they attempted to climb out.
Not yet, she decided. Suzanne needs more time to heal, and I need to be sure about our route before we commit to it.
But she also knew they couldn't wait indefinitely. Their food supplies were limited, and while she was getting better at catching fish, she couldn't guarantee a steady source of nutrition for all three of them. More importantly, she could feel the debris field settling around them, the gradual compression that suggested the structure was becoming more stable but also more compact, more difficult to navigate.
We have maybe three or four days, she estimated. After that, we'll need to make our move whether we're ready or not.
The thought should have frightened her, but instead it filled her with a kind of grim determination. She had kept herself and Coleman alive for three days. She had rescued and treated Suzanne. She had turned a desperate survival situation into something that resembled a functioning household.
I can do this, she thought, settling into her familiar position on Coleman's surface while Suzanne tucked her head under her good wing for the night. Whatever comes next, I can handle it.
Coleman rocked gently beneath her, and she could feel his confidence in her, his unshakeable belief that she would find a way to save them all. Suzanne made a soft, contented sound, the first truly peaceful noise Gabbie had heard from the bird since they'd met.
They trust me, she realized, and the knowledge was both humbling and empowering. They believe I can get us out of here.
As she drifted toward sleep, Gabbie found herself thinking not about the challenges ahead, but about the strange gifts the flood had given her. It had taken away everything familiar, everything safe, everything she had thought she needed to be happy. But it had also given her Coleman and Suzanne, had shown her strengths she hadn't known she possessed, had taught her that love could exist in the most unlikely forms and places.
Maybe this is what transformation really means, she thought as sleep claimed her. Not just changing from one thing to another, but discovering what was inside you all along.
Tomorrow would bring new challenges, new decisions, new tests of her ability to protect the small family she had found in the gray wilderness of the flood. But tonight, surrounded by the breathing presences of two creatures who trusted her completely, she felt something she hadn't experienced since before the flood began.
She felt ready.
Chapter 6: The Architecture of Chaos
Morning came with the same gray filtering of light through their debris cathedral, but today Gabbie studied their prison with the methodical attention of an architect trying to understand a building's structure. Standing carefully on Coleman's surface, she could just barely touch the ceiling of their enclosure with her fingertips—maybe seven or eight feet from the waterline to the tangled canopy above.
Almost two Gabbies tall, she thought, using herself as a measuring stick the way her father had taught her when they were camping. Two of me stacked on top of each other would just about reach the top.
But what a ceiling it was. The flood had created something that belonged in a Stephen King novel—a grotesque sculpture of civilization's detritus woven together by forces beyond human comprehension. A child's red tricycle hung suspended in a web of telephone wires, its wheels spinning lazily in the slight breeze that somehow found its way into their enclosed world. The front grille of a pickup truck had somehow become wedged between two street signs—one advertising "MAIN STREET" and the other declaring "DEAD END"—creating an ironic commentary on their situation that would have been funny if it weren't so terrifying.
The tsunami effect, she realized, remembering documentaries she'd seen about natural disasters. Everything gets picked up and carried along together, and when the water stops, it all gets deposited in the same impossible pile.
A mannequin from some department store floated just beneath the surface near their refuge, its plastic smile eerily cheerful as it bobbed in the murky water. A shopping cart had somehow become tangled with the limbs of a real tree, creating a hybrid of natural and artificial that hurt to look at. Household items that should never have been in the same place—a toilet, a church pew, a neon "OPEN" sign from some long-destroyed business—were now permanently fused together in configurations that defied logic.
This is what happens when the world comes apart, Gabbie thought, studying the chaos with a mixture of fascination and revulsion. All our careful categories, all our neat separations between things—none of it matters when the water rises, when the river cries.
She turned to Coleman, running her hands along his familiar surface, checking once again for any signs of damage or wear. The mattress had proven remarkably durable, but she could see the toll their journey had taken—scratches and scuffs that told the story of their survival, battle scars that somehow made him seem more real, more present.
"Coleman," she said softly, "I want to ask you something important."
The mattress rocked gently beneath her, and she could feel his attention, his readiness to listen.
"Suzanne is part of our family now," she continued, glancing toward the heron, who was preening her good wing with the careful precision of someone tending to something precious. "I know I brought her here without asking you first, but... would you welcome her? Officially, I mean. As part of us?"
Coleman's response was immediate and unmistakable—a gentle settling in the water, a subtle shift that brought him closer to where Suzanne rested, as if he was extending an invitation, opening his protective circle to include their new companion.
He understands, Gabbie thought with wonder. He really understands about family, about belonging, about making room for someone who needs us.
The gesture reminded her powerfully of her sister Mollie, who had always been the one to include Gabbie in things, to make space for her younger sister in social situations that might otherwise have been awkward or lonely. Mollie, who was sixteen now and had just gotten her driver's license, who was athletic and popular and resourceful in ways that Gabbie admired and sometimes envied.
What would Mollie do in this situation? she wondered, studying the debris maze around them with new eyes. What superhuman athletic feat would Mollie pull off to get us all out of here?
Mollie had always been Gabbie's best coach, her most enthusiastic supporter, the person who believed she could accomplish anything she set her mind to. When Gabbie had been afraid to try out for the swimming team, Mollie had spent hours in their backyard pool, teaching her different strokes and building her confidence. When Gabbie had struggled with math, Mollie had patiently worked through homework problems with her until the concepts clicked.
Mollie would find a way, Gabbie thought with certainty. She'd look at this mess and see possibilities instead of obstacles. She'd figure out how to turn all this chaos into an advantage.
But even as she drew strength from thinking about her sister, Gabbie felt a familiar pang of loss. Since Mollie had gotten her license and started dating Ryan Hutchinson, things had been different between them. Not bad, exactly, but... changed. Mollie was still kind, still supportive, but there was a distance now that hadn't been there before, as if part of her attention was always somewhere else.
She's growing up, Gabbie had told herself. She's becoming who she's supposed to be.
But privately, Gabbie had sworn that when her turn came, she wouldn't change like that. She wouldn't let new freedoms or new relationships pull her away from the people who mattered most. She would remember what it felt like to be the younger sister who needed inclusion, who needed someone to believe in her capabilities.
I won't change, she had promised herself. I'll stay who I am.
Now, floating in their gray refuge with Coleman and Suzanne, she wondered if that promise had been naive. Maybe change wasn't something you chose—maybe it was something that happened to you, something that grew out of new responsibilities and new relationships and new understandings about what the world demanded from you.
Maybe I'm changing right now, she thought, looking at her two companions who depended on her for their survival. Maybe this is what change really looks like.
Her philosophical musings were interrupted by a sound that made her heart leap into her throat—a distant mechanical rhythm that seemed to cut through the gray afternoon air like music.
Thwap, thwap, thwap.
At first, she couldn't process what she was hearing. The sound was so foreign to their water-logged world, so impossible in the context of their debris prison, that her brain simply couldn't categorize it.
Thwap, thwap, thwap.
And then recognition hit her like a physical blow, and she had to grab Coleman's sides to keep from falling into the water.
Helicopter.
Thwap, thwap, thwap.
It was the most beautiful sound she had ever heard, more beautiful than any music they had forced her to listen to in the camp's mandatory music class, more beautiful than her mother's lullabies or her father's bedtime stories or Mollie's encouraging cheers from the sidelines of various athletic events.
They're looking for us, she realized, her heart hammering with sudden hope. They haven't given up. They're still looking.
But even as joy flooded through her, a practical voice in the back of her mind whispered the obvious problem: How are they going to see us down here? How are they going to know we're here?
The sound grew louder, then began to fade as the helicopter moved on, and Gabbie felt panic rise in her throat like bile. They were so close—rescue was literally overhead—but they might as well have been on another planet for all the good it did them.
Signal, she thought desperately. I need to create some kind of signal.
But how? She was trapped under eight feet of debris in a gray world where everything blended together in a monotone nightmare. Even if she could climb to the surface—and that was a big if, given Coleman's size and Suzanne's injury—would there be anything distinctive enough to catch a pilot's attention?
Her desperate strategizing was interrupted by a more immediate problem: the gnawing emptiness in her stomach that had been growing stronger all morning. She hadn't eaten anything substantial in nearly two days, and the work of caring for Suzanne and exploring their refuge had burned through what little energy reserves she had left.
Food, she thought, and the word seemed to echo in her mind with increasing urgency. I need food.
The irony wasn't lost on her that hunger had become her most pressing concern in a situation where she was already in mortal danger. But the body was pragmatic—it demanded fuel regardless of circumstances, regardless of whether fuel was available.
She had never been a particularly picky eater, unlike Mollie, who had gone through phases of refusing entire food groups and driving their mother to distraction with her dietary restrictions. But Gabbie had always had one absolute, non-negotiable aversion: cheese.
Any form of cheese—mild, sharp, processed, artisanal—was guaranteed to make her gag. The smell alone was enough to send her fleeing from pizza parties and school cafeterias. She couldn't understand how other people could voluntarily put something so fundamentally wrong-smelling and wrong-tasting into their mouths.
Cheese is my nemesis, she had often told her family, and they had learned to accommodate her peculiar revulsion.
But now, as hunger gnawed at her insides like a living thing, all she could think about was cheese. Specifically, all the cheese she had refused over the years, all the pizza slices she had scraped clean before eating, all the sandwiches she had deconstructed to remove the offending dairy products.
What I wouldn't give for a piece of cheese right now, she thought, and the irony was so perfect, so cruel, that she almost laughed. Any cheese. Even the nasty processed stuff from school lunches.
But there was no cheese in their debris jungle, no food of any kind except for the occasional fish she could catch for Suzanne. Her stomach cramped with emptiness, and she pressed her hands against her abdomen, trying to quiet the demanding rumble.
I'm not thinking straight, she realized with a clarity that surprised her. Hunger is making me irrational. I need to be careful about the decisions I make.
It was a moment of self-awareness that felt important, like a small victory over the circumstances that were trying to reduce her to pure animal survival instinct. She was still capable of recognizing when her judgment might be compromised, still capable of stepping back and evaluating her own mental state.
Logic versus intuition, she thought. Which one do I trust when my brain isn't working at full capacity?
She looked at her two companions, who had become not just her family but her council of advisors in this strange new world they inhabited together.
"What do you think?" she asked them aloud. "Should I try to climb out when the helicopter comes back? Should I risk leaving you both here to try to signal for help?"
Coleman rocked gently beneath her, and she tried to read meaning into the movement. Was he encouraging her to go, or warning her to stay?
Suzanne lifted her head and fixed Gabbie with those bright, intelligent eyes. The heron made a soft sound that seemed thoughtful, contemplative, as if she was actually considering the question.
They can't answer me, Gabbie thought. Not in words. But maybe that's not what I need right now. Maybe what I need is just to voice the question, to put the dilemma into words so I can think about it more clearly.
The third night was approaching—she was almost certain it was the third night, though time had become so fluid that she might have been wrong. Three nights since the flood, three nights since she had last eaten real food, three nights since she had slept in a bed that didn't rock with the rhythm of water.
Decisions, she thought, settling back against Coleman's familiar surface. Everything comes down to decisions.
The helicopter sound had faded completely now, but the knowledge that rescue was actively searching for survivors had changed something fundamental about their situation. They weren't just waiting anymore—they were preparing. The next time that beautiful mechanical rhythm cut through their gray world, they would be ready.
The question was: ready for what?
Chapter 7: The Tools of Survival
Gabbie spent the remaining daylight hours cataloging every piece of debris within reach, her mind working with the methodical precision her father had taught her for organizing camping gear. Everything has a potential use, he had always said. The trick is seeing beyond what something was designed for to what it could become.
Her inventory was both depressing and oddly hopeful. A broken umbrella with most of its fabric torn away, but the metal ribs intact and potentially useful as hooks or levers. Several lengths of electrical cord in various states of decay, some still flexible enough to serve as rope. A child's plastic sand bucket, cracked but still capable of holding water or small objects. Half of a wooden baseball bat, the handle end, worn smooth by countless hands but still solid.
Tools, she thought, arranging her collection on Coleman's surface like a medieval craftsman laying out implements. These aren't garbage anymore. They're tools.
She tested each piece, bending the umbrella ribs to check their flexibility, tugging on the electrical cords to gauge their strength. Her hands moved with growing confidence as she began to see connections, possibilities, ways that these random objects could be combined into something useful.
The broken umbrella could become a grappling hook if she tied it to one of the cords. The baseball bat handle could serve as a lever for moving heavy debris. The plastic bucket could carry water up to Coleman and Suzanne if she made it to the surface and found a clean source.
I'm becoming someone different, she realized as she worked. Someone who sees solutions instead of problems.
Through Coleman's subtle movements, she could feel that the current below them was beginning to settle. The mattress no longer rocked with the violent unpredictability of fast-moving water, but with the gentler rhythm of something finding its equilibrium. The river was tired too, she thought. Tired of carrying away everything it touched, tired of being a destroyer instead of the life-giver it was meant to be.
We have a window, she told her companions. The water is calming down. If we're going to move, it has to be soon.
Coleman responded with what felt like agreement, a slight settling that seemed to say, We trust your judgment.
Suzanne lifted her head and made a soft sound that Gabbie had learned to interpret as encouragement. The heron was stronger today, more alert, though still obviously in need of careful protection.
"I'm going to scout our escape route," Gabbie announced, gathering her makeshift tools and checking that she could still reach Coleman quickly if the debris shifted. "I'll be back soon. I promise."
She had decided to head left, toward what her instincts told her should be the direction of the riverbank. It was an educated guess based on the way the current moved and the pattern of debris accumulation, but in their gray world, instinct was often all she had to go on.
The journey through the debris maze was like navigating a three-dimensional puzzle designed by someone with a twisted sense of humor. She had to duck under a suspended stop sign, squeeze between two shopping carts that had somehow become welded together by the force of collision, and step carefully over a jumble of kitchen appliances that looked like they'd been dropped from a great height.
Her clothes, which had started this ordeal as her favorite summer camp outfit—purple tank top with "CAMP WILDWOOD" printed in cheerful yellow letters, khaki shorts that hit just above her knees, white canvas sneakers that her mother had bought specifically for camp activities—were now barely recognizable. The tank top had torn at the left shoulder, leaving a ragged hole that exposed more of her back than she was comfortable with. The shorts were stained beyond recognition and had several small tears from encounters with sharp metal edges. One of her sneakers had lost its laces entirely, and both were now a mottled brown color that had nothing to do with their original white canvas.
I look like a refugee, she thought, catching a glimpse of her reflection in a partially submerged car window. I look like someone who's been through a war.
But there was something else in her reflection too—something in her eyes that hadn't been there before the flood. A hardness, maybe, or a kind of focused intensity that spoke of someone who had seen too much and learned to adapt. She looked older somehow, though only three days had passed since she'd been worried about friendship bracelets and which boy at camp might like her.
I am older, she realized. Three days ago I was a kid. Now I'm... something else.
She was working her way around a particularly complex tangle of bicycle frames and street signs when she saw it.
IT.
Everything that she had dreaded finding, everything that her mind had carefully avoided contemplating during their three days in the debris jungle.
A body.
He was wedged between what looked like pieces of a wooden fence and the twisted remains of a porch swing, positioned in a way that made him look almost comfortable, as if he had simply decided to rest for a moment in this impossible place. Salt-and-pepper hair that might once have been neatly combed now hung in wet strands around a face that was... wrong. Horribly wrong.
Most of his clothes were gone, torn away by the violence of the flood, leaving only shreds of what might have been a business shirt and the waistband of dark pants. His skin was the color of old newspaper, mottled and bloated in ways that made Gabbie's stomach clench with recognition of what she was seeing.
He was someone, she thought, forcing herself to look at him, to acknowledge his humanity even in death. He had a family. People who loved him. People who are looking for him.
The thought made her chest tight with grief for this stranger, for the family that was waiting for news, for the children who might be wondering why Daddy hadn't come home.
"I'm Gabbie," she said aloud, her voice barely above a whisper. "Sir, are you... are you still there?"
The words felt stupid even as she spoke them, but some deep instinct told her that approaching the dead required announcement, required respect, required acknowledgment of the boundary she was crossing.
The slight current that still moved through their debris field shifted something in the water, and the man's head moved slightly, turning toward her with the mechanical movement of something no longer controlled by will or consciousness.
Gabbie found herself staring into the sockets where his eyes should have been.
They were gone. Completely gone. In their place were dark, empty cavities that seemed to go deeper than they should, deeper than anatomy would suggest. She could see into his skull, could see gray matter that glistened wetly in the filtered light of their underwater world.
Fish, her mind supplied with clinical detachment. Fish eat the soft parts first. Eyes, lips, fingertips.
The knowledge came from some nature documentary she had watched with her father, but knowing the biological explanation didn't make the reality any less horrifying.
Her stomach clenched, rebelled, and she doubled over as her body tried to expel contents it didn't have. The first heave brought up nothing but bitter air. The second produced a thin stream of the contaminated water she had been drinking. But on the third heave, something thick and viscous emerged—a small amount of brownish-green bile that tasted like poison and death and the absolute bottom of human endurance.
That's bad, she thought with the part of her mind that was still capable of rational analysis. That's really bad. Bodies don't produce bile like that unless something is seriously wrong.
She was dying. Maybe not today, maybe not tomorrow, but her body was beginning to shut down from the combination of starvation, contaminated water, and the sheer physical stress of survival. The bile was a warning, a red flag that she was running out of time.
Ignore it, she told herself, wiping her mouth with the back of her hand and forcing herself to stand upright. Deal with the immediate problem. Find the escape route. Save Coleman and Suzanne. Everything else is secondary.
But even as she told herself to move on, she found herself looking back at the dead man, wondering about his story. Had he been trying to save someone when the flood caught him? Had he been alone, or had there been family members swept away with him? Was he a hero who had died trying to help others, or just another victim caught in the wrong place at the wrong time?
I could be him tomorrow, she thought. If I don't find food, if I don't get us out of here, I could end up wedged in some debris field for another twelve-year-old to find.
The thought should have terrified her, but instead it filled her with a kind of grim determination. She would not become another body for someone else to mourn. She would not give up on Coleman and Suzanne. She would not let this flood claim three more lives simply because the situation seemed impossible.
Mollie wouldn't give up, she thought, drawing strength from the image of her sister's athletic determination. Mollie would find a way to turn this horror into motivation.
She forced herself to move past the dead man, continuing her exploration of the debris maze with renewed urgency. Time was running out—for all of them. But she still had strength, still had tools, still had two companions who believed in her ability to save them.
I won't let you down, she promised silently, whether to the dead man or to Coleman and Suzanne or to herself, she wasn't sure.
The escape route revealed itself gradually as she worked her way through the tangle. A path that led upward through a series of natural ledges formed by the way debris had settled against debris. It would be dangerous—several sections looked unstable, ready to collapse if disturbed—but it was possible.
I can do this, she thought, already planning how she would guide Coleman through the narrower passages, how she would carry Suzanne when the path became too treacherous for an injured bird to navigate alone.
She was making her way back toward her companions when she heard it again—fainter this time, but unmistakable.
Thwap, thwap, thwap.
The helicopter was coming back.
This time, she would be ready.
Chapter 8: The Museum of Lost Things
The return journey to Coleman and Suzanne took Gabbie through sections of their debris cathedral that she hadn't fully explored before, and what she found there belonged in some mad curator's exhibition of civilization's strangest artifacts. A wedding dress hung from a stop sign like a ghost bride waiting for a ceremony that would never come, its white silk now the color of old teeth, moving in the slight current with an obscene parody of dancing. Beside it, a collection of garden gnomes had somehow arranged themselves in a perfect circle around a partially submerged barbecue grill, their painted smiles grotesquely cheerful in the underwater gloom.
The Museum of Lost Things, she thought, navigating around a grandfather clock that chimed the quarter hour despite being half-full of river water. This is where everything ends up when the world decides to rearrange itself.
A child's dollhouse sat perched on top of a file cabinet, its tiny rooms still perfectly furnished despite the chaos around it. The miniature family inside—mother, father, two children, and a dog—remained frozen in their domestic tableau, having tea in a kitchen while their real-world equivalent had been swept away by forces beyond imagination. The juxtaposition was so perfectly wrong that Gabbie found herself stopping to stare, wondering if some cosmic joke was being played on her sanity.
Even the fake family survived better than most of the real ones, she thought, and the dark humor of it made her laugh—a sound that came out more like a sob in the gray half-light of their prison.
The laughter felt dangerous, hysterical, like the first crack in a dam that had been holding back too much pressure for too long. She pressed her hand to her mouth, forcing herself to focus on the immediate task of returning to her companions. Breakdown was a luxury she couldn't afford, not when Coleman and Suzanne were depending on her to be the one who kept it together.
But the bizarre tableaux continued to assault her consciousness as she made her way through the maze. A mannequin head wearing a graduation cap sat on a throne made of stacked car tires, presiding over its domain with the serene authority of some post-apocalyptic monarch. A row of parking meters stood at attention like metal soldiers, their expired tags a testament to a world where time had been measured in quarters and hours instead of heartbeats and survival.
This is what happens when God plays with action figures, she thought, remembering the way she and Mollie used to set up elaborate scenarios with their toys, creating disasters and rescues and dramatic reunions that seemed so important in the moment but were forgotten by dinnertime.
Except this isn't pretend, she reminded herself as she squeezed past a peculiar sculpture formed by a shopping cart that had somehow become perfectly nested inside a church pew. This is real, and somewhere out there, real people are looking at real lists of the missing and the dead.
The thought of those lists made her stomach clench with something beyond hunger. Her name was probably on one of them. Her parents were probably sitting in some Red Cross shelter or hotel room, staring at their phones and waiting for news that might never come. Mollie was probably driving around in her newly-licensed freedom, checking every hospital and evacuation center, refusing to believe that her little sister could simply be gone.
They don't know about Coleman, she realized. They don't know that I found something better than a life preserver. They don't know that I'm not just surviving—I'm living.
The distinction felt important, though she couldn't quite articulate why. There was a difference between merely not dying and actually building something meaningful in the midst of chaos. She had created a family, had taken responsibility for other lives, had discovered strengths and capabilities she'd never known she possessed.
I'm not the same person who fell into this water three days ago, she thought, running her hand along a bicycle wheel that spun lazily in the current, its spokes creating hypnotic patterns in the filtered light. That girl was worried about friendship bracelets and boys and whether she was weird for preferring the sound of water to the sound of her bunkmates giggling. This girl knows how to set a broken wing and catch fish and read the mood of a mattress.
She found Coleman and Suzanne exactly where she had left them, but something in their positioning had changed. Coleman seemed more alert, riding higher in the water as if he was preparing for action. Suzanne had tucked her injured wing more securely against her body and was holding her head in the ready posture of a bird prepared for flight—or at least the closest approximation to flight that her condition would allow.
They know, Gabbie realized. They can sense that something is about to happen.
"I found a way out," she told them, settling onto Coleman's familiar surface and feeling the immediate comfort of his steady presence. "It's going to be dangerous, and we'll have to be very careful, but I think we can do it."
Coleman rocked gently in what she had learned to interpret as confidence, trust, willingness to follow her lead wherever it might take them.
Suzanne made a soft sound that seemed to say, We're ready.
No, Gabbie thought, studying the debris maze with new eyes. We're not ready yet. But we will be.
The helicopter sound was getting closer now, the mechanical rhythm cutting through their gray world with increasing clarity. But this time, Gabbie had a plan that went beyond simply hoping to be seen. This time, she had tools and knowledge and, most importantly, the absolute certainty that she would not leave anyone behind.
She spent precious minutes arranging her makeshift equipment, turning the broken umbrella and electrical cord into a grappling system that could help her navigate the more treacherous sections of their escape route. The plastic bucket would carry water for Suzanne, whose healing body needed constant hydration. The baseball bat handle would serve as both walking stick and lever for moving obstacles that might block their path.
Like Dad always said, she thought, checking and double-checking each knot and connection. Proper preparation prevents poor performance.
The memory of her father's voice steadied her, reminding her that she came from people who solved problems, who faced challenges with methodical planning and stubborn determination. Her father might not be here to help her navigate this crisis, but he had given her the tools to navigate it herself.
And Mom gave me the empathy to care about saving others, she added, looking at Suzanne with protective tenderness. And Mollie gave me the determination to never give up on the people I love.
The helicopter was almost directly overhead now, its sound so loud that it seemed to vibrate through the water itself. This was it—her chance to signal for rescue, to end this ordeal and return to the world of the living.
But as she prepared to begin their escape, a new thought struck her with the force of revelation: What if she didn't want to return to the world exactly as she had left it? What if this experience had changed her so fundamentally that going back to worrying about friendship bracelets and social hierarchies felt impossible?
What if I don't want to go back to being the weird quiet girl who talks to water? she wondered. What if I want to stay being the person who saves birds and communes with mattresses and sees possibilities in chaos?
The question was academic, really. She had to get them out of here—all three of them—before her body's deterioration reached the point of no return. But the underlying issue would remain: How do you return to normal life after discovering that you're capable of so much more than normal life ever demanded?
That's a problem for later, she decided, gathering Suzanne carefully in her arms and feeling Coleman adjust his position to accommodate their movement. Right now, we escape. Everything else can wait.
The helicopter's downdraft was beginning to affect the water around them, creating ripples and currents that made Coleman bob with unusual energy. It was time to move.
Together, she thought, taking one last look around their gray sanctuary. We leave together, or we don't leave at all.
Chapter 9: The Ascent
The escape route that had seemed manageable during her earlier reconnaissance now revealed itself as a three-dimensional nightmare that would have challenged an experienced rock climber, let alone a twelve-year-old girl carrying an injured bird and guiding an air mattress through spaces barely wide enough for human passage.
But Gabbie had learned something about herself over the past three days: she was capable of things that should have been impossible.
Start with the easy part, she told herself, guiding Coleman toward the first section of their route—a relatively open channel between two overturned cars that would allow the mattress to float freely while she carried Suzanne ahead to scout for obstacles.
The helicopter was circling now, the pilot clearly aware that this debris field was worth investigating but not yet able to pinpoint any signs of life. The mechanical rhythm of the rotors had become the soundtrack to their escape, a urgent percussion that seemed to say, Hurry, hurry, hurry.
But not too hurry, Gabbie reminded herself. Panic kills. Dad always said panic kills.
She had positioned herself so that she could guide Coleman with one hand while cradling Suzanne against her chest with the other. The heron seemed to understand the gravity of their situation—she held herself very still, her good wing tucked tight against her body, her injured wing carefully immobilized by the splint that had become as much a part of her as her own feathers.
We're a team, Gabbie thought as they navigated the first section successfully. All three of us working together.
The second section was more challenging—a narrow passage where Coleman had to be partially deflated to squeeze between a refrigerator and a concrete barrier. Gabbie used the broken umbrella as a guide, pushing it ahead of them to test for snags or sharp edges that might puncture the mattress.
Please hold together, she whispered to Coleman as she watched him compress and stretch through the tight space. Just a little bit longer.
Coleman responded by somehow making himself more streamlined, more flexible, as if he was consciously adapting his shape to meet the demands of their escape. The sight was so clearly impossible, so obviously outside the laws of physics that govern inanimate objects, that Gabbie felt a flutter of something that might have been fear.
Am I losing my mind? she wondered. Am I seeing things that aren't there because I need them to be there?
But the evidence of her senses was unmistakable. Coleman was helping. Not just floating passively, but actively participating in their escape, reading the route ahead and adjusting his behavior accordingly.
Maybe I am crazy, she thought. But if this is crazy, I'll take it over sane any day.
The third section required them to climb—actually climb—up through a tangle of tree branches and electrical wires that formed a natural ladder toward the surface. This was where Gabbie's plan became truly desperate, truly dependent on the kind of luck that survival stories were made of.
She secured Coleman to the base of their improvised ladder using the electrical cord, creating a belay system that would prevent him from drifting away if something went wrong. Then, with Suzanne still cradled against her chest, she began to climb.
The ascent was a nightmare of sharp edges and unstable handholds. Her already-tattered clothes caught on every protrusion, adding new tears to the collection she had accumulated over their three days in the debris field. By the time she was halfway to the surface, her tank top was little more than strategic strips of fabric, and her shorts had acquired a tear that ran from her left hip almost to her knee.
I look like a feral child, she thought, catching another glimpse of her reflection in a shard of mirror glass that had somehow become embedded in the tangle. I look like something that belongs in the woods, not in civilization.
But there was strength in her reflection too—a lean, wiry toughness that hadn't been there before the flood. Her arms had developed the kind of muscle definition that came from constant physical challenge, and her eyes held a focus and intensity that spoke of someone who had learned to see clearly in situations where clarity meant the difference between life and death.
I look like Mollie after a particularly brutal basketball practice, she realized. I look like someone who's been tested and passed.
The thought gave her the energy to complete the climb, to push through the final tangle of debris and emerge, blinking, into actual daylight for the first time in three days.
The world above was nothing like the world she remembered.
What had once been the familiar landscape of East Texas—rolling hills covered with pine and oak, pastures dotted with cattle, small towns connected by ribbons of highway—was now an alien moonscape of brown water and scattered debris. The flood had receded somewhat, but it had left behind a layer of mud and destruction that transformed everything into a monochrome nightmare.
This is what the end of the world looks like, she thought, turning in a slow circle to take in the scope of the devastation. Not fire and brimstone, but mud and silence and the absence of everything that used to be alive.
The helicopter spotted her almost immediately. She watched it bank sharply and head directly toward her position, the pilot's skill evident in the way he navigated around the taller pieces of debris that still jutted from the receding flood waters.
Here we go, she thought, waving her arms frantically to maintain their attention. This is where it gets complicated.
The helicopter hovered about thirty feet above her, the downdraft from its rotors creating a windstorm that threatened to knock her off her precarious perch on top of the debris pile. A rope ladder unrolled from the aircraft's open door, and she could see a figure in a rescue harness preparing to descend.
Just one person, she realized with growing anxiety. They're only planning to rescue one person.
The rescuer—a middle-aged man in Coast Guard gear—reached the debris pile with the practiced efficiency of someone who had done this many times before. His face was kind but focused, his movements economical and purposeful.
"Are you hurt?" he shouted over the noise of the helicopter.
"I'm okay!" she shouted back. "But I'm not alone!"
The confusion on his face was immediate and obvious. He looked around the debris pile, clearly expecting to see other human survivors.
"My family!" Gabbie shouted, pointing down toward the tangle where Coleman and Suzanne were waiting. "I can't leave my family!"
The rescuer's expression shifted from confusion to the kind of patient concern that adults used when dealing with children who were clearly in shock. He probably thought she was hallucinating, that the trauma of survival had created imaginary companions or caused her to mistake debris for people.
He thinks I'm crazy, she realized. He thinks I'm a traumatized kid who's lost touch with reality.
"We need to get you out of here right now," the rescuer said, reaching for her with gentle but firm hands. "The debris field is unstable. We can search for other survivors from the air."
"No!" Gabbie pulled away from him, moving toward the edge of the debris pile. "You don't understand! They're real! They saved my life!"
She was already starting her descent back into the maze, ignoring the rescuer's shouts of protest. She had to get Coleman and Suzanne. She had to show him that her family was real, that they deserved to be saved just as much as she did.
He'll understand once he sees them, she told herself as she climbed down through the tangle. Once he meets Coleman and Suzanne, he'll understand that we're all in this together.
But even as she descended toward her companions, a small voice in the back of her mind whispered the question she didn't want to face: What if they didn't understand? What if the adult world she was trying to return to had no place for the kind of family she had created in the chaos?
What if saving Coleman and Suzanne meant giving up her only chance at rescue?
Then that's a choice I'll have to make, she thought grimly. But I'm not making it until I've tried every other option first.
Coleman and Suzanne were waiting exactly where she had left them, and the relief on both their faces—yes, faces, because by now she was certain they both had faces, expressions, personalities as real as any human she had ever known—was evident and heartbreaking.
"We're getting out of here," she told them firmly. "All of us. I promise."
The rescuer's voice echoed down through the debris maze, calling her name (though she hadn't told him her name, which meant someone else had identified her, which meant her family knew she was alive), begging her to return to the surface immediately.
He sounds desperate, she thought. He sounds like someone who's lost kids before.
The realization gave her an idea—not a good idea, maybe, but the only idea she had.
She began to climb back toward the surface, this time with Coleman's rope in one hand and Suzanne cradled against her shoulder. If the rescuer wouldn't come down to meet her family, she would bring her family up to meet the rescuer.
This is either going to work, she thought as she struggled upward with her precious cargo, or it's going to be the stupidest thing anyone has ever done in the history of helicopter rescues.
But either way, she added, feeling Coleman's steady presence and Suzanne's trust and the rescuer's growing panic from above, we're doing it together.
Chapter 10: The Impossible Family
The ascent with Coleman and Suzanne was like trying to solve a three-dimensional puzzle while balancing on a tightrope in a windstorm. The air mattress, even partially deflated, was awkward and unwieldy in the narrow spaces between debris. Suzanne, despite her trust in Gabbie, couldn't help flinching every time they had to squeeze through a particularly tight passage, and each flinch sent spikes of pain through her injured wing that she communicated with soft, distressed sounds.
This is insane, Gabbie thought as she maneuvered Coleman around a particularly stubborn tangle of fence posts and shopping carts. I'm risking all our lives for the chance to prove that my family is real.
But even as the rational part of her mind catalogued the risks and found them unacceptable, another part—the part that had been growing stronger over the past three days—remained absolutely certain that this was the right choice. She would not abandon Coleman and Suzanne. She would not return to the world of the living without the beings who had taught her what it meant to be truly alive.
The rescuer's voice grew more urgent as she climbed, his professional calm beginning to crack under the strain of watching a twelve-year-old girl disappear into a debris field that could collapse at any moment.
"Gabbie!" he called—and yes, he did know her name, which meant her parents were somewhere nearby, which meant this nightmare was almost over. "Gabbie, you need to come up right now! The structure isn't stable!"
He's right, she acknowledged, feeling the way the debris around them shifted and groaned with each movement. This whole thing could come down like a house of cards.
But they were almost to the surface now, almost to the point where she could show the rescuer that Coleman and Suzanne were real, were deserving of salvation, were part of a family that had been forged in the chaos of survival.
Just a little bit further, she told her companions silently. Just hold on a little bit longer.
Coleman seemed to understand the urgency of their situation. The mattress had somehow made himself even more streamlined, compressing his air chambers in ways that should have been impossible but allowed him to navigate spaces that had seemed too narrow just moments before. It was as if he was willing himself to be smaller, more manageable, more suitable for rescue.
Suzanne, meanwhile, had positioned herself against Gabbie's shoulder in a way that distributed her weight perfectly, making the climb easier rather than harder. The heron's bright eyes were fixed on the growing patch of gray sky above them, and Gabbie could feel the bird's anticipation, her hope for freedom and healing and the chance to fly again.
We're going to make it, she thought as her head cleared the final layer of debris and she found herself once again on the surface of their gray world. All three of us are going to make it.
The rescuer was waiting for her, his face a mixture of relief and exasperation and something that might have been admiration for her sheer stubborn determination.
"Gabbie," he said, his voice gentler now, "we need to talk."
"This is Coleman," she said without preamble, gesturing to the air mattress that had somehow positioned himself in the most stable part of the debris pile. "He saved my life. He's been taking care of me for three days."
The rescuer looked at Coleman with the kind of polite attention adults used when humoring children's imaginary friends. It was clear that he saw an air mattress—probably a useful piece of survival equipment, but hardly a family member deserving of rescue.
"And this is Suzanne," Gabbie continued, carefully adjusting the heron so that the rescuer could see her clearly. "Her wing is broken, but I made a splint. She's going to be okay, but she needs proper medical care."
The rescuer's expression shifted when he saw Suzanne. This, at least, was something he could understand—a child who had rescued an injured animal during a crisis. It was touching, even admirable, but hardly a reason to delay a helicopter rescue in an unstable debris field.
"That's very compassionate of you," he said carefully, "but we need to get you to safety first. Wildlife rehabilitation services can handle the bird."
"No," Gabbie said firmly. "You don't understand. They're not just a mattress and a bird. They're my family. They saved me, and I'm not leaving them behind."
The helicopter pilot's voice crackled through the rescuer's radio, urgent and professional: "We need to move now. Weather's deteriorating, and this debris field is shifting."
The rescuer looked at Gabbie with the kind of desperate calculation that came from years of making impossible choices in crisis situations. He could probably force her into the rescue harness, could physically drag her to safety despite her protests. But doing so would mean leaving behind the air mattress and the injured bird, and something in Gabbie's eyes told him that she would never forgive him—or herself—for that abandonment.
"The mattress won't fit in the helicopter," he said finally, trying to find a compromise that would satisfy everyone. "But we could arrange for the bird to be transported separately."
"Coleman goes where I go," Gabbie said, her voice carrying a certainty that surprised even her. "He's not just survival equipment. He's... he's my partner. My friend. He chose to save me when everything else was trying to kill me."
The rescuer glanced at Coleman, clearly trying to see what Gabbie saw—some sign of personality, of consciousness, of the mysterious quality that had transformed an inflatable mattress into a beloved family member.
And for just a moment, as if responding to the scrutiny, Coleman did something that air mattresses weren't supposed to do.
He rocked.
Not the passive bobbing of an object floating in disturbed water, but a deliberate, rhythmic movement that seemed almost like a greeting, an acknowledgment of the rescuer's presence.
The rescuer stared at Coleman for several long seconds, his professional skepticism warring with the evidence of his own eyes. Then he looked back at Gabbie, seeing something in her face that made him reach a decision that would have seemed impossible five minutes earlier.
"Okay," he said, reaching for his radio. "We're going to need a bigger helicopter."
Twenty minutes later, a second aircraft arrived—a heavy-lift helicopter with enough space and power to accommodate unconventional rescue scenarios. The crew worked with the kind of efficient professionalism that spoke of people who had seen everything and were no longer surprised by the creative ways that survival could manifest itself.
Coleman was loaded onto the helicopter with the same care and attention that would have been given to any human survivor. The crew secured him in place with specialized straps and rigging, ensuring that he remained stable and undamaged during the flight.
Suzanne was placed in a specially designed carrier that protected her injured wing while allowing her to see her companions. The heron seemed to understand that they were finally safe, finally together, finally on their way to a world where healing was possible.
And Gabbie sat between them, one hand on Coleman's familiar surface and the other resting gently on Suzanne's carrier, feeling the weight of responsibility and love and the strange joy that came from knowing that sometimes—just sometimes—families could survive anything if they refused to let go of each other.
We made it, she thought as the helicopter lifted off and the gray world of the debris field fell away beneath them. All three of us made it.
But even as relief flooded through her, she found herself wondering about the world they were returning to. Would there be a place in that world for the kind of family she had created? Would people understand that love could exist between a girl and a mattress and a bird? Would they accept that survival could create bonds that transcended the normal boundaries of species and consciousness?
We'll figure it out, she decided, feeling Coleman's steady presence and Suzanne's trust and the helicopter's powerful engines carrying them all toward whatever came next. Whatever happens, we'll figure it out together.
After all, they had survived the impossible. Everything else was just details.
Epilogue: Six Months Later
The lake house belonged to Gabbie's grandparents, but it had become something more in the months since the flood—a place of healing, of recovery, of learning how to be a family in ways that no one could have anticipated.
Coleman floated in the clear blue water of Lake Travis, his familiar blue and white stripes bright in the afternoon sunlight. He looked no different than he had in the gray world of the debris field, but his presence felt somehow more settled, more at peace. The lake suited him, Gabbie thought. Clean water, gentle currents, a place where floating was about pleasure rather than survival.
Suzanne stood in the shallows near the dock, her wing fully healed now but still careful, still protective of the part of herself that had been broken and mended. She had never quite regained her full ability to fly—the wing was strong enough for short glides but not for the epic migrations that were her birthright as a Great Blue Heron. But she seemed content with her new life, dividing her time between fishing in the lake and resting in the carefully constructed aviary that Gabbie's grandfather had built.
She chose to stay, Gabbie reflected, watching the heron move through the water with graceful precision. Even when she could have left, she chose to stay with us.
The choice hadn't been without complications. Wildlife rehabilitation experts had argued that keeping a wild bird was inappropriate, potentially harmful to both the bird and the ecosystem. But Suzanne had made her own preferences clear, becoming agitated and distressed whenever separation was suggested, calming only when she was reunited with Gabbie and Coleman.
Some bonds, the lead veterinarian had finally admitted, transcend our normal understanding of animal behavior.
The same could be said for Coleman's role in their unconventional family. Psychologists and trauma specialists had initially been concerned about Gabbie's attachment to an inanimate object, worried that her relationship with the mattress was a symptom of dissociation or an inability to process the trauma of her experience.
But further observation had revealed something more complex and harder to categorize. Coleman's responses to Gabbie's presence were subtle but consistent—a slight adjustment in position when she approached, a particular way of moving in the water that seemed almost conversational. Whether this represented some form of consciousness or simply Gabbie's highly developed ability to read and respond to environmental cues was ultimately less important than the fact that the relationship was clearly beneficial to her recovery.
She's not retreating from human connection, her therapist had noted in her report. She's expanding her definition of what connection can look like.
And it was true. Gabbie had not become the isolated, traumatized child that everyone had feared. Instead, she had become something unprecedented—a twelve-year-old girl who moved through the world with a confidence and clarity that seemed to come from having survived the impossible and learned that love could exist in the most unlikely forms.
Her relationship with Mollie had deepened rather than diminished in the aftermath of the flood. Her sister, shaken by how close she had come to losing Gabbie forever, had made a conscious choice to prioritize family over the social pressures that had been pulling her in different directions.
I don't want to be the sister who was too busy to pay attention, Mollie had said during one of their late-night conversations on the lake house porch. I don't want to be the person who realizes too late that the most important things were right in front of me all along.
Their parents, too, had been transformed by the experience. The crisis had forced them to confront their own priorities, their own assumptions about what mattered in life. They had moved back in together—not because the flood had magically solved their marital problems, but because they had remembered why they had fallen in love in the first place and decided to work toward becoming the people they wanted to be.
Trauma breaks some families apart, their family counselor had observed. But sometimes it can also break down the barriers that were keeping families from being truly close.
The lake house had become their headquarters for this ongoing experiment in healing and connection. Gabbie spent her mornings on the water with Coleman, developing an intuitive understanding of currents and weather patterns that impressed even her grandfather. Her afternoons were devoted to Suzanne, learning about avian behavior and rehabilitation techniques that might help other injured birds.
And her evenings were spent with her human family, sharing stories and meals and the comfortable silence that came from people who had learned not to take each other's presence for granted.
This is what happiness looks like, she thought, floating on Coleman's familiar surface while Suzanne waded nearby and her family's voices drifted across the water from the dock. Not the absence of problems, but the presence of love in forms I never expected.
The flood had taken away the world she had known, but it had given her something infinitely more valuable—the knowledge that she was capable of creating family and meaning and joy in circumstances that should have destroyed all three.
As the sun began to set over Lake Travis, painting the water in shades of gold and amber that reminded her of another river in another time, Gabbie Morrison smiled and settled more comfortably against Coleman's surface.
Tomorrow would bring new challenges, new opportunities to discover what was possible when love refused to be limited by conventional boundaries. But tonight, surrounded by the breathing presences of her chosen family, she was exactly where she belonged.
We survived, she thought, feeling Coleman's gentle rocking and Suzanne's soft presence and the distant sound of her sister's laughter carrying across the water. We survived, and we're still together, and that's everything.
The lake lapped gently against the shore, whispering secrets that only those who knew how to listen could hear. And Gabbie Morrison, who had learned to hear the voices of water and mattresses and birds, listened with the contentment of someone who had found her place in the world.
Even if that place was unlike anywhere anyone had ever imagined home could be.

THE END
Author's Note: "When the River Cries" explores themes of survival, resilience, and the unexpected forms that love and family can take in our darkest moments. Through Gabbie's extraordinary journey, we see how crisis can strip away everything we thought we knew about ourselves and reveal strengths and capacities we never knew we possessed. Her relationship with Coleman and Suzanne challenges our assumptions about consciousness, connection, and the boundaries between species and states of being. Ultimately, this is a story about the courage to love unconditionally and the willingness to redefine family based on devotion rather than convention.
