# Prologue: The Last Voice of Boston Rock

Four hours left. That’s all Boston gets, all I get, before WBCN—The Rock of Boston—flips the switch and fades into the static hum where lost stations go. Four hours and the old frequency, the nervous red ON AIR glow in Studio B, the city’s ragged heart beating through my headphones, all of it—gone, boxed up, and tossed wherever corporate stations go to die. There are farewells and then there are endings. This, I guess, is both.

Here’s the ugly truth: I’ve been rehearsing this night in my head for years. It was always going to end. Everything does. But I never figured I’d have so much left to say. And I didn’t realize—until tonight—how much those unsaid things ache, how silence weighs more than song when the lights start to go out in the places you loved.

You want to know the parallel? It’s not just WBCN dying. It’s not even rock radio itself. It’s the loss of innocence you never notice until one morning—maybe looking in the rearview mirror at a stoplight—you catch your own eyes and wonder when the brightness left. For me, it happened most sharply in 1979, under cheap lights at Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High. Two kids—Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh—both with enough talent to change their worlds, both with guitars in hand and hearts wired to music the way other kids were wired to hope. Best friends as children, by senior year they passed each other in the hallway like strangers. And I carry that, too—how does that happen? How can we let it?

We like to believe places like Sudbury keep kids safe from bitterness, from all the divisions and rot that grow up outside city limits. But that year—that final year—proved what I’d known from behind these radio walls all along: music can bridge divides, but it can also map them, echo them, leave scars where there should have been understanding.

So tonight, as the city’s glitter settles cold on the Charles and all the old clubs are converted to condos, I’m breaking radio silence to tell their story. Our story. Not some tidy nostalgia with neat verse-chorus-verse shape. The real thing—the mess, the loss, the chance that maybe, just maybe, it’s not too late to listen for what we missed.

Pour yourself something strong, if you’re listening. This isn’t just a history lesson. This is my confession. There’s so much I wish I’d done—so much I wish I had the guts to say earlier, to Nick, to Jackie, to Boston itself. I tried to change things. Some things can’t be changed. But they damn well can be remembered.

“This is Maxanne Sartori,” I say, voice rough with old heartbreaks and more years than I care to count. “And for the next four hours, WBCN is alive. As long as I’ve got the mic, no one, not even the suits, can shut us up. I’m going to tell you about Nick and Jackie and a town that divided two best friends. I’m going to tell you about how music—the kind we shared and the kind we lost—made us, saved us, and sometimes broke us on the way out.”

I’m Maxanne Sartori. Seventy-three years old. Back in Studio B for one last broadcast, the studio’s track lighting so harsh it makes the dust spin like lost spirits. I can smell it all: stale coffee, sour leftover cigarettes, the faint memory of a thousand sweaty hands clapping in unison at the Orpheum. Right now, there’s a heavy glass of bourbon sweating in my palm—a little Pappy Van Winkle to keep my courage honest. I never used to drink on the job, but tonight is the end of the rules, and the old code of conduct left when they started letting the morning crews voice-track their playlists from home.

On the other side of the glass, the new program director hovers—a kid who probably can’t name a single band that ever played The Rat. I can almost feel his sneakers judging my whole generation; he’s twenty-eight, all anxious smiles and an iPad glued to his hand. His voice cracks through the intercom: “You’ve got four hours left, Maxanne. Make them count.” Of course he says it like a challenge, as if it's a marketing pitch. As if I didn’t spend a decade surviving deadlines and drunks, keeping this station’s signal alive when Boston needed rock and roll more than another Dunkin’ Donuts.

They called me back for this. Me—the so-called legend who found Aerosmith, who introduced thousands of kids to the real gospel coming out of blown PA speakers all over New England. Maybe they thought I’d give them some easy nostalgia, spin Tom Petty and tell a few safe stories about backstage excess. What nobody knows—not the suits, not the listeners still tuned in, maybe not even myself till now—is that I have something left to say. Four hours to finally tell the story that’s haunted me every day since 1979.

Funny thing, guilt. They say it fades with time, but I don’t buy it. It just changes shape. There’s a particular hollow you get after a long career and too many late nights, a space where regret and hope settle in like bad insulation. I’ve had forty-five years to picture different endings: for WBCN, for those two boys from Sudbury, for myself if I’d just made one decision differently. Sometimes, in the small hours, I wonder if anyone hears the difference between honest music and a good impersonation, if anyone can still feel what Boston used to sound like before—before everything started selling out in stages.

Here’s the truth, for what it’s worth. This station was never just a job to me—it was rebellion and family and forgiveness and every kind of heartbreak squeezed into the space between a Gibson guitar chord and a pair of headphones duct-taped just enough to keep faith that the next song was worth everything. Boston’s suburbs grew up behind the wistful curtain of disco, and inside them lived kids like Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh—two souls who should have kept each other whole, but wound up collateral damage in a war nobody declared. That’s the story I need to tell, even if no corporate sponsor wants to hear it.

Tonight, I’m not just spinning records. I’m opening the door to the past, and you’re coming with me. If you’re tuning in, maybe pour yourself something strong—cheap or expensive, doesn’t matter. Maybe you never came to Lansdowne Street, or maybe you just miss the way Kenmore used to smell of rain and vinyl after midnight. Doesn’t matter. This is for you too.

The glass is cool in my hand as I turn up the fader, ready to let memory pour out alongside the signal. “This is Maxanne Sartori,” I say, voice a little rough, “and tonight, you and I are bearing witness. For the next four hours, WBCN is alive—and so is everything we almost lost and maybe, if we listen hard enough, might still be able to remember.”
