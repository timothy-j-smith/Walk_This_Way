## CHAPTER 9: BEFORE THE TALENT SHOW

Three hours and forty-seven minutes left.

I'm sitting here in Studio B, watching the ON AIR sign blink off and on—a warning light in the dark, like some stubborn old traffic signal holding back midnight—and it hits me: I've been feeding you the end of the story without ever serving up the start. The Battle of the Bands in 1979—that’s the big finale, sure, but tonight we need to rewind the tape to where the story really finds its groove. Not the first garage jam, not even the slow-fused friendship between Nick and Jackie, but the instant two best friends looked at a middle school talent show and thought, hell, why not give it a shot?

Because that's the thing about music—it's not just about talent or technique or even passion. It's about the moments when you decide to step out of the shadows and into the light, when you choose to share something that matters to you with people who might not understand it, might not appreciate it, might not even care.
I take another sip of bourbon—Pappy Van Winkle, because Steven Tyler doesn't send cheap gifts—and I think about May 15, 1975. The day Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh stopped being just two kids who played music together and became something that scared the hell out of everyone who thought they understood how the world was supposed to work.
I set the glass down—good bourbon, not the kind you buy on sale at the packie, but something heavy and sweet that tastes a little like trouble and old promises. The kind of drink you save for nights when memory refuses to fade quietly. For a second, it warms my chest, the way music used to when I believed it could change the shape of a life. My mind drifts to May 15, 1975. That was the day Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh quit being just a couple of kids messing around with chords in the garage and became something bigger, something disruptive, the sort of thing that rattles the bones of anyone who believes the world should always color inside the lines.

                                 MAXANNE
          May 15, 1975. The annual Ephraim Curtis Middle School Talent Show. 
          Open to all middleschoolers who dare, one winner, and a gymnasium full
          of parents who'd spent 
          the afternoon convincing themselves that their children were special 
          without being too different, talented without being too unusual, 
          competitive without being too ambitious.

Outside the booth windows, Boston glitters in the October darkness, but I'm seeing Sudbury in May, the kind of spring afternoon that makes thirteen-year-olds believe anything is possible.

                                 MAXANNE
          Now, I need you to understand something about middle school talent shows 
          in suburban Massachusetts in 1975. They weren't about discovering new 
          talent or celebrating artistic expression. They were about maintaining 
          the social order, about proving that the kids who were supposed to win 
          would win, that the system worked exactly the way it was designed to work.

I reach for the cassette on my desk, the one labeled "Demo - Nick/Jackie - Not Authorized - March 1979." But tonight I'm not playing that tape. Tonight I'm telling you about the day they decided to make it, about the moment when two best friends looked at each other and said, "Why not us?"

                                 MAXANNE
          Nick and Jackie had been playing music together for almost two years 
          by then. The garage sessions had become legendary. Working-class families would 
          gather to hear what two thirteen-year-old boys had discovered about 
          the power of collaboration.

The engineer kid out there is hunched over his phone, probably texting his girlfriend that he's stuck "supervising the old legend on her nostalgia shift." He doesn't realize his job title tonight is part-time radio tech, part-time Maxanne-sitter. The irony makes me smile into the glass. He grew up thinking GarageBand was all you needed, that music magic was in a menu, not a garage. He never saw what four cinderblock walls and a concrete floor can do when you have nothing to lose and everything left to prove.

                                 MAXANNE
          But here's the thing about garage sessions—they're safe. They're 
          contained. They're for people who already understand what you're 
          trying to do. The talent show was different. The talent show was 
          public. The talent show was for everyone—parents who'd never heard 
          rock and roll, teachers who thought music was something you learned 
          from sheet music, kids who'd never been to a garage session and 
          wouldn't understand why two boys playing acoustic guitars mattered 
          more than the pretty girl with voice lessons.

I pause, letting the silence breathe. Radio silence again.  Just for a moment.

                                 MAXANNE
          The decision to enter the talent show happened on a Tuesday afternoon 
          in April. Nick was in the garage, working on a new riff he'd heard 
          on WBCN—something by Led Zeppelin that he was trying to figure out 
          on his acoustic guitar. Jackie was sitting on Dan's workbench, 
          watching Nick's fingers move across the frets, recognizing the 
          classical structure beneath the rock and roll surface.

          "We should enter the talent show," Jackie said.

          Nick looked up from his guitar, surprised. "What talent show?"

          "The middle school one. May 15th. Mrs. Reed mentioned it in 
          music class today."

          Nick went back to his riff, but I could see the idea taking hold. 
          
          "Why would we do that?"

          "Because we're good," Jackie said, and there was something in his 
          voice that surprised both of them. "Because we're really good, 
          and nobody knows it except us."

I glance down at my glass, letting the moment settle. There’s comfort in ritual, the way liquid amber catches the studio lights and conjures up memories of late-night conversations, old vinyl spinning on a turntable, laughter echoing down narrow Boston hallways. So much has changed since then, but the urge to tell their story—to remind the world of what really mattered—never goes away.

                                 MAXANNE
          That was the moment, right there. Not when they started playing music 
          together, not when they became best friends, but when Jackie said 
          "nobody knows it except us" and Nick realized he was right. They 
          were good. They were really good. And the garage sessions had been 
          their secret, their safe space, their proof that different approaches 
          to the same dreams could enhance rather than compete with each other.

          But secrets don't change the world. Secrets don't challenge the 
          social order. Secrets don't make parents question whether their 
          children are being prepared for the right kind of success.


                                 MAXANNE
          They spent the next three weeks preparing. Not practicing—preparing. 
          Because this wasn't about getting better at what they already did. 
          This was about figuring out how to translate garage session magic 
          into something that would work in a middle school gymnasium, with 
          fluorescent lights and folding chairs and parents who'd never heard 
          of Led Zeppelin.

          Nick wanted to do covers. "Walk This Way" by Aerosmith, "Bohemian 
          Rhapsody" by Queen—songs that would prove they understood rock and 
          roll, that they weren't just kids playing acoustic guitars in a 
          garage.

     
I pause, tracing my finger around the rim of my coffee mug, grounding myself in the present as memory threatens to pull me back again. There’s a comfort in repetition—in the hum of studio lights, the low crackle of an old record spinning nearby, the city beyond the windows settling into its midnight hush. Some stories refuse to stay untold; they push their way through, demanding to be spoken aloud.

                                 MAXANNE
          Their final choice was brilliant. A mashup of "Bohemian Rhapsody" 
          and "Walk This Way"—Queen meets Aerosmith, classical meets rock, 
          privilege meets working-class authenticity. It was everything 
          they were, everything they'd become, everything they were trying 
          to prove about the power of collaboration.

          But here's the thing about mashups—they're risky. They require 
          the audience to understand both songs, to appreciate the skill 
          required to blend them, to recognize that what they're hearing 
          isn't just performance but creation. And middle school talent 
          shows aren't designed for risk. They're designed for safety, 
          for predictability, for the kind of excellence that doesn't 
          challenge anyone's assumptions about how the world is supposed 
          to work.

                                 MAXANNE
          The day of the talent show, Nick and Jackie arrived at school 
          with their guitars and their arrangement and absolutely no 
          expectations. They weren't trying to win—they were trying to 
          share. They weren't trying to prove anything to anyone else— 
          they were trying to prove something to themselves. That their 
          friendship mattered. That their music mattered. That different 
          approaches to the same dreams could create something bigger 
          than individual talent.

          Karen Bowers was there too, of course. Karen Bowers was always 
          there. Blonde, perfectly prepared, with a voice trained by 
          private instructors who charged more per lesson than Grace 
          Johnson made in a day at Friendly's. Her stage presence 
          suggested she'd been born for spotlights, her technique 
          reflected years of coaching designed to eliminate any trace 
          of regional accent or personal quirk that might interfere 
          with commercial appeal.

          She was performing "Tomorrow" from "Annie." Of course she was. 
          Because Karen Bowers represented everything suburban communities 
          thought they wanted: discipline, investment in proper training, 
          predictable excellence. She sang like she was auditioning for 
          roles she was destined to win, performed like she was doing 
          everyone a favor by sharing her obvious talent.

An ambulance wails down Tremont, Doppler-effect fading, as I drum my fingers on the battered studio desk. Behind the glass, my engineer—a college kid in a Bruins hoodie—peers out from a fortress of mixing boards and empty Red Bull cans, eyeing me the way you watch a pot that might boil over.

                                 MAXANNE
          But here's what Karen Bowers didn't understand—and what Nick and 
          Jackie did. Music isn't about technical perfection. It's about 
          emotional truth. It's about the willingness to risk everything 
          you think you know about yourself in service of something bigger 
          than individual achievement.

          Nick and Jackie weren't trying to be perfect. They were trying 
          to be authentic. They weren't trying to win. They were trying 
          to connect. They weren't trying to prove that they were better 
          than anyone else. They were trying to prove that different 
          approaches to the same human longing could enhance rather than 
          compete with each other.

          And that's what scared the hell out of everyone who thought 
          they understood how the world was supposed to work.

I crack my knuckles and lean into the microphone one more time. The bourbon's warmth spreads through my chest like liquid courage, the good kind that makes you tell stories you've been carrying too long.

                                 MAXANNE
          The middle school gymnasium was packed—parents with cameras, kids 
          with attitudes, teachers hoping for the best and expecting chaos. 
          The fluorescent lights buzzed overhead like angry wasps, casting 
          everything in that institutional pallor that made even the most 
          hopeful performances look like medical procedures.

          Nick and Jackie were scheduled to perform sixth in the eight-act 
          lineup. Karen Bowers was scheduled to close the show, the traditional 
          spot for the inevitable winner, performing "Tomorrow" from "Annie" 
          with the kind of polish that made everyone else feel like amateurs.

          But sometimes, very occasionally, something happens that changes 
          how everyone understands what's possible. Sometimes two kids with 
          acoustic guitars and absolutely no expectations prove that authenticity 
          can trump technique, that collaboration can create something bigger 
          than individual talent, that the best music happens when different 
          approaches to the same human longing decide to collaborate rather 
          than compete.

          And that's exactly what happened on May 15, 1975, in the Ephraim 
          Curtis Middle School gymnasium, when Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh 
          stopped being just two kids who played music together and became 
          something that scared the hell out of everyone who thought they 
          understood how the world was supposed to work.

Clock on the wall says I’m running out of night and out of bourbon, too, as the ON AIR light flickers, taunting me like a promise you can’t quite cash. Out there, Boston sprawls under neon and streetlamp, all lost hopes and half-remembered melodies, while my voice drifts from this booth to whoever’s listening—a ghost on the FM dial, clinging to stories some folks would rather forget.

You want the truth? Here it is, barbed-wire and all: the real revolution never happened on a record label’s timeline, and it damn sure didn’t have a sponsor. Sometimes harmony loses to noise, and the noise comes wearing a tie and a fixed smile. That’s the story I’ve carried, and the one you need to hear.

But first, we need to talk about what happened when Nick and Jackie stepped onto that stage and proved that sometimes the most dangerous thing you can do is be exactly who you are.
