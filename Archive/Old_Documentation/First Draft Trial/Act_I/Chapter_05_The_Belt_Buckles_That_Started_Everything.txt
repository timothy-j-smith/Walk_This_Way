## CHAPTER 5: THE BELT BUCKLES THAT STARTED EVERYTHING

Five years before Maxanne Sartori would discover them, before the Battle of the Bands would tear them apart, before America's class war would claim them as casualties, two twelve-year-old boys met in a middle school cafeteria and discovered that music could be a bridge instead of a barrier.

The Ephraim Curtis Middle School cafeteria in September 1974 was the kind of place that smelled like institutional food and adolescent desperation. Brown bag lunches from mothers who still cared enough to pack them, cafeteria pizza that tasted like cardboard with cheese, individual milk cartons that always seemed to be warm, and Jell-O in colors that didn't exist in nature. Kids mixed milk and Jell-O together like mad scientists conducting experiments in the chemistry of gross, creating concoctions that would make their mothers weep if they ever saw them.

But the real soundtrack of that cafeteria wasn't the clatter of trays or the sound of kids discovering new ways to ruin perfectly good food. It was disco music blasting from someone's transistor radio—"Dancing Queen" by ABBA filling the air with synthetic strings and falsetto voices that sounded like they'd been processed through a cheese grater. Most kids seemed to accept it as background noise, the way you accept the sound of traffic or your parents arguing in the next room. It was just there, like the fluorescent lights that made everyone look slightly green and the linoleum floors that had been mopped so many times they'd lost their original color.

At a corner table, Jackie Marsh sat alone, clearly the new kid. He was smaller than he'd become, but already carried himself with the careful precision of someone who'd been taught that posture mattered. His brown hair was cut in that awkward middle-school way that made him look like he was trying too hard, and his clothes were clean but not quite right—the kind of outfit that screamed "my mother picked this out" in a language every other kid could understand.

But what caught your eye wasn't his clothes or his haircut or the way he sat like he was waiting for someone to tell him where to go. It was the belt buckle. A brown leather belt with a gold script "QUEEN" buckle that he wore like it was an Olympic gold medal. The buckle caught the fluorescent light and threw it back at the world, demanding attention the way only twelve-year-old boys can demand attention—with the absolute certainty that their taste in music defines their entire personality.

Across the cafeteria, Nick Johnson entered like a small tornado looking for something to destroy. He was wiry in that way that suggested he'd been running since birth and hadn't quite figured out how to stop. His black hair was longer than most kids' parents would allow, and he moved with the restless energy of someone who'd been told to sit still one too many times. His clothes were clean but worn, the kind of clothes that suggested his mother bought them on sale and expected them to last until he outgrew them.

And then there was his belt buckle. A black leather belt with a chrome "AEROSMITH" buckle in gothic letters that looked like it had been designed by someone who'd never seen a font they didn't want to make more dramatic. The buckle was polished to a mirror finish, and when the sun streaming through the cafeteria windows hit it just right, it threw a reflection that could blind you from across the room.

Which is exactly what happened to Jackie Marsh.

The sun caught Nick's buckle like a spotlight, and for an instant, the reflection hit Jackie square in the eyes. Jackie threw up his forearm to block the glare, squinting until the reflection faded. When he lowered his arm, Nick was walking toward him, and their eyes met. Not because they recognized each other—they'd never spoken, never been introduced, never even acknowledged each other's existence. But because they both noticed the other one's belt buckle at the same time.

Nick walked over with the kind of confidence that masks nervousness, the way kids do when they're trying to look cool but aren't quite sure how to pull it off. He stopped at Jackie's table and pointed at the Queen buckle.

"Queen? Seriously?"

Jackie looked up, defensive but curious. "What's wrong with Queen?"

Nick grinned and showed his own buckle. "Nothing. Just... Aerosmith's better."

Jackie stood up, fire in his eyes. "Better? Have you heard 'Bohemian Rhapsody'?"

"Have you heard 'Dream On'?"

They stared at each other for a moment, two twelve-year-old boys who'd just discovered that music could be a battlefield. Around them, the disco music continued—"Le Freak" by Chic now blasting from the radio, that insistent bass line that made you want to dance even when you didn't want to dance.

Jackie gestured toward the music, disgusted. "Can we agree that disco sucks?"

Nick's face lit up like someone had just handed him the keys to the kingdom. "Oh my God, finally someone who gets it! It's like... music without soul."

"Exactly! Where's the artistry? Where's the emotion? What is a 'Bee Gee' anyway?"

Nick sat down, extending his hand. "I'm Nick. I think I saw you in Mr. Apfel's homeroom. I just didn't see that buckle of yours."

"Jackie. My dad just moved us here from Ithaca."

Nick leaned forward, his eyes bright with the kind of excitement that only comes from finding someone who speaks your language. "So... Queen versus Aerosmith. This is happening..."

Jackie grinned, and for the first time since he'd arrived in Sudbury, he looked like he belonged somewhere. "Oh, it's happening."

Around them, the Bee Gees' "Staying Alive" started playing, and several middle schoolers began singing along, rocking their heads back and forth to the disco beat like they were trying to shake loose their brains. Jackie and Nick just shook their heads in disgust, united in their hatred of something that felt like a musical invasion.

But here's the thing about that moment—it wasn't just about music. It wasn't just about two kids who'd found each other in the chaos of middle school. It was about something deeper, something that would matter more than either of them could understand at twelve years old.

Jackie Marsh lived on the good side of Hop Brook, in a house where his father taught economics at Boston University and his mother played piano at dinner parties. He'd grown up with classical music and private lessons and the assumption that excellence was not just expected but required. His Queen buckle wasn't just a fashion statement—it was a declaration that he understood music could be both sophisticated and accessible, that Freddie Mercury had taken opera and made it rock, that there was room in the world for both Chopin and "Bohemian Rhapsody."

Nick Johnson lived on the other side of Hop Brook, in a house where his father worked construction and his mother counted coupons and prayed the car would start in the morning. He'd grown up with the sound of power tools and the smell of sawdust and the understanding that if you wanted something, you had to work for it. His Aerosmith buckle wasn't just a fashion statement—it was a declaration that he understood music could be both raw and powerful, that Steven Tyler had taken the blues and made it dangerous, that there was room in the world for both "Dream On" and "Walk This Way."

But in that moment, in that cafeteria, with disco music blasting around them like a musical assault, none of that mattered. What mattered was that they'd found each other. What mattered was that they'd discovered they weren't alone in their hatred of music that felt like it was trying to sell them something instead of telling them something. What mattered was that they'd formed their first alliance—not against each other, but against the world that was trying to tell them what music should sound like.

The Bee Gees kept singing about staying alive, and the other kids kept dancing, and Jackie and Nick sat at their table and talked about music that mattered. They talked about Queen's harmonies and Aerosmith's power. They talked about Freddie Mercury's voice and Steven Tyler's stage presence. They talked about songs that made you feel something instead of just making you want to move.

And somewhere in that conversation, between the arguments about which band was better and the shared disgust at disco's synthetic soul, they became friends. Not because they agreed about everything, but because they agreed about the one thing that mattered most: music should be real, it should be honest, it should come from someplace deep inside that couldn't be manufactured or processed or turned into a product.

They didn't know it then, but they'd just discovered something that would define their entire relationship. They'd found each other through music, and music would be the thing that brought them together and the thing that would eventually tear them apart. They'd formed a bond that would survive middle school and and then start to fracture as the moved into high school when music stopped being a bridge and became a battlefield.

But that was years away. In that moment, in that cafeteria, with disco music blasting around them and their belt buckles catching the light, they were just two twelve-year-old boys who'd found each other in the chaos and discovered that sometimes the most important thing you can do is find someone who speaks your language.

The bell rang, signaling the end of lunch period, and they stood up together, still arguing about Queen versus Aerosmith, still united in their hatred of disco, still completely unaware that they'd just taken the first step toward a friendship that would change everything.

As they walked out of the cafeteria together, Jackie's Queen buckle and Nick's Aerosmith buckle caught the light one more time, throwing reflections that seemed to dance together in the air like musical notes finding their harmony.

It was the beginning of everything. It was the end of nothing. It was two boys discovering that music could be a bridge instead of a barrier, that friendship could cross any divide, that sometimes the most important battles are fought not with weapons but with songs.

And it all started with a belt buckle catching the light.
