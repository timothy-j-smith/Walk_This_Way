Chapter 4: Walking the Invisible Lines

March 1979, and Sudbury is still thawing from another long New England winter, but the lines that divide it never freeze, never blur. You could ride your bike from one end to the other, cut through a crosshatch of dirt roads and cracked pavement, smelling woodsmoke and pig manure, but run right into fences you can’t see—class, pride, history, and all the ways people keep score.

The northeast quarter wakes up before dawn, their houses pressed shoulder to shoulder, families packed in like eggs waiting to crack. Early noise from the triple-deckers: coughing kids, radios playing Led Zeppelin, a dad yanking on work boots while his wife curses the busted heater and the wolfish cold. By seven, the kids are out—lunches in paper bags, pawnshop guitars slung over thin shoulders, already practicing their first escape.

A mile south, beyond where the brook hums and the land starts to stink of rot and pig shit, the Cavicchio farm sprawls. The barn roof is dented with old weather and the mud is thick as blood this time of year. Giuseppe stands at the fence, coat open, eyes on the frost retreating from the fields, the ghosts of Ford and all the other men who came to buy him out swirling in the morning haze. He can smell garbage in the wind, knows nothing in this town moves without someone else’s waste greasing the wheels.

On the other side of the landfill, the houses stand apart—colonials with white columns, driveways long as the queue at the dump on a Saturday morning. Inside: mothers rehearsing for the next PTA ambush, fathers hunched behind newspapers or novels, sons and daughters flexing their fingers over piano keys. The afternoon sun spills onto sheet music and the floors are worn pale by generations of soft, clean feet; music as certain as tap water and a life mapped in disciplined notes.

Up northwest, behind mossy walls and iron gates half-swallowed by yew and forsythia, Sudbury’s money sits, proud and nearly invisible. Children are raised in the soft hush of expectation: string lessons, French tutors, dress rehearsals for futures inherited, not earned. The family names here are badges, whispered in the school halls and stitched into the town’s history like a mischievous secret.

All quarters pay tribute at the landfill each Saturday, a muddy democracy smelling of rot. Station wagons and battered pickups line up wheel-to-wheel, fathers measuring distance with boot heels while their children gag in the back seats. Everything ends up here—broken toys, wine bottles, yellowing magazines—and for a half hour, amid the reek, everyone is level. No one brags, no one explains. It’s just garbage and gravity and the sound of bottles shattering in the sun.

Between the dump and the pig farm run the dirt paths—mini-bike routes tattooed deep in the earth by ten-year-olds who know how to wrench freedom from the smallest machines. The lines scrawled across empty lots and under live wires; the only places in town where a boy might forget to notice which side he starts from and which he ends up on.

Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh live within a mile of each other. Jackie’s mother keeps his piano polished to a shine, insists on practice before dinner and prayer before sleep. Nick’s dad is up before sunrise most days, chasing work in Framingham and giving advice through a screen of Lucky Strike smoke: “Don’t trust a kid who doesn’t bleed when he falls.” They meet sometimes at the edge of the pig farm, or behind the dump where nobody pretends. They trade chords, swap insults, and for a few minutes, forget who they're supposed to become.

Main Street runs between them—a colonial movie set for tourists and school field trips: white steeples, a common, and the promise of stocks long gone. The center holds, but only just. There’s an old order here, sworn in amber, and everyone falls in line whether they mean to or not.

This is the year the music will break the surface. March melting into April, two boys preparing for a Battle of the Bands that is really just another skirmish in Sudbury’s quiet little war. The stage is only a few steps wide, but the space between the boys—between what’s expected and what’s possible, between the music you play and the music you feel—is harder to cross than all the dirt roads in Middlesex County.

Everyone thinks they know where they belong. But when the amps crackle and the lights find them, Nick and Jackie will have to decide for themselves if crossing those lines is possible, or if the town they love will turn their harmony into just another kind of fight.

**Professional Review:** This rewrite dives deeper into the marrow of character and moment, less map than memory, but still sometimes drifts to exposition—the living pulse of Sudbury occasionally undercut by turns toward summary. The palpable ache of longing and the friction between place and people ring true, yet the chapter still hesitates at the threshold of real dramatic conflict.

**Rating:** 7.5/10

As Richard Russo: The chapter now pulses with individual voices and scenes that hurt and hover in memory; places and people are in dialogue rather than on parade. What’s here is finally the beginning of a story, not just a setting; make sure you never let the geography do more talking than the boys themselves.
