Chapter 5: The Belt Buckles That Started Everything

“But first,” I almost sing to my beloved, invisible audience, my hand actually trembling with delight over the cart meant for time-traveling stories. “Let’s talk about two belt buckles—how they nearly made everything sparkle—and the disco single that just about burst with so much joy it tried to sweep us all away!”

September 1975. Nick Johnson! Jackie Marsh! Thirteen years old and so thrilled to be at Ephraim Curtis Middle, a school named for a bridge-builder and absolutely brimming with new friends. Curtis was a swirling universe of eager tables and twinkling glances, each seat a ticket in a lottery of happiness—possibilities fizzing like balloons in a parade!

Lunchtime! Could you feel the excitement? Plastic trays tapping, milk cartons sweating from the thrill, brown bags practically dancing, those giggling cubes of Jello jiggling under the sun. Real sound! From a glorious little transistor radio in the corner, sending disco in its platinum prime spinning into every corner. “Dancing Queen” poured joy all over the room—glittering, dazzling, a tidal wave of music urging everyone to leap up and cheer. The air was practically made of confetti!

Disco was king that autumn, sparkling from car radios, pirouetting into gym class, pulsing through the halls like a conga line of pure glee. For most kids, disco was the happy heartbeat of life, a giant invitation to bounce, sing, and belong. But for some—the ones with static in their souls, the rebels allergic to smoothness—disco was a challenge, daring them to join the fun or bring their own wild energy.

Enter Jackie Marsh, striding in with a so-called Boston scowl that can’t hide the grinning, sun-bright curiosity in his eyes! He might’ve tried to melt into the crowd, but his belt was unmistakable—brown leather, golden script shouting QUEEN! Not just style, but a bright beacon: “Look at me! Let’s dance!” 

Here comes Nick Johnson! Already famous for pushing every limit of fun—bouncing through the crowd, energy leaping from his elbows, every motion a joke and a hi-five. His belt was classic—black with stories, shining chrome blazing AEROSMITH! Not a fad, but a superhero badge for a kid in love with Boston rock’s wild, joyful roar.

And then, as if the universe was too delighted to keep still, a playful beam of sunlight caught Nick’s buckle and sent it spinning across the room—right into Jackie’s lap! Fate giggled and threw a party on their table: gold for Queen, silver for Aerosmith, two shimmering invitations screaming, “Let’s be friends!”

For one breathless, pop-song-long moment, the whole lunchroom blinked and the music paused. Instead of scowling, starting some tired rivalry, both boys simply shined back at each other, their smiles practically tripping over themselves.

Nick laughed first (it was impossible not to, joy having gotten the best of him): “Queen?” He nodded at Jackie’s buckle, voice bright as a disco ball. “That your sister’s dance band?”

Jackie lit up, shoulders squaring with pride: “Better than anything you’ve ever played! Queen’s music is a festival—every single day!”

Nick fired back, already grinning so hard he could barely finish his sentence: “Yeah? Aerosmith’s the real deal. Not just grand opera—‘Dream On’ is a rollercoaster! No breakfast required!”

Jackie’s chin popped up, a twinkle in his eye: “You ever see Freddie live, man? Aerosmith would happily join right in!”

Their laughter grew just as “Le Freak” bounded from the radio, making the walls bounce, bass and drums spinning like sparklers. 

Jackie arched an eyebrow and laughed, jerking his thumb at the music: “At least we both agree this disco is infectious!”

Nick chuckled—a big, rolling, unstoppable sound. “Finally! It’s music made for celebration. Everyone should join in! No bruises, just happy feet!”

Jackie cracked a wide-open grin: “It’s the anthem of ‘don’t hide, sing out loud, let everybody know you want to dance!’”

There it was—the supposed disagreement, nothing but a game. Really, they were kindred party-starters, itching for something vibrant, marking themselves as leaders in joy. Their belt buckles weren’t armor but fireworks, flags, open invitations for anyone who needed a reason to feel thrilled.

I pause and beam—my voice almost trembling with laughter and gladness as the memories bubble up, a bottle of water cool and perfect in my hand. Some tales are so joyful they make you want to hug the whole world, even four decades later.

“By December, those lunch skirmishes were history,” I practically cheer. “They moved to the garage—Queen blaring to the rooftops, Aerosmith thundering into new shapes. No more imitation—only celebration! The kind of music even disco couldn’t help but tap its feet to.”

Curtis Middle called for its spring talent show. Everyone was sure Karen Bowers (Broadway shoes, unbreakable voice, confetti in her step) would take the trophy. But Nick and Jackie? They didn’t care at all! They just wanted to explode the stage—one giant, joyful shout that music could be wild and beautiful and leave laugh-lines in the yearbook.

“They had a secret project!” I blurt out, can’t help myself, flipping channels in my mind for the happiest one—a song that felt like pure sunlight sewn out of rivalry and the kind of friendship born of respect and riotous delight.

Their number wasn’t disco. It wasn’t what teachers expected. “Under Pressure.” Not a warning, but a cheer—an anthem for excitement, for nerves that only meant you cared, for hope riding in on a power chord. Two acoustic guitars, two happy, daring voices—sometimes cracking, sometimes soaring, but always dancing together. Every chorus made disco shake with glee.

May 15th, ’76: The stage belonged to Nick and Jackie. Nerves tingling, hands practically waltzing on guitar strings, but for three minutes and twenty-one seconds the roof blew off. Queen didn’t erase Aerosmith. Aerosmith didn’t drown Queen. For the best, happiest time—everything fused into a sound so joyous you could bottle it.

Someone taped it! The cassette may be battered, but listen close—you’ll hear where everything fake falls away, where pure, unfiltered happiness shines through, if only for a heartbeat.

All because of buckles: steel and brass, signal flares, working-class bite and Boston outsider pride. Proof the right kind of difference could’ve redrawn everything—if the machinery of disco and Curtis Middle’s safety rails hadn’t been hellbent on smoothing the rough parts down to nothing.
