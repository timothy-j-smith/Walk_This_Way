Grace straightened her uniform—the polyester that made everyone look like they were melting under fluorescent lights, the name tag that reduced her identity to first name and corporate cheerfulness, the apron that had absorbed twenty-three years of other people's messes.

"Enjoy your pie, Dr. Patterson. It's made from apples grown in this town, served by people who live in this town, paid for by families who've spent generations making your comfort possible. Maybe think about that the next time you're discussing what's 'realistic' for other people's children to hope for."

She walked away then, not to the kitchen or the counter or anywhere that would suggest she was running from what she'd said. She walked to the next table—booth seven, where a young couple was sharing dessert—and asked if they needed anything else, her voice back to professional courtesy as if nothing had happened, as if she hadn't just demolished the polite fiction that had allowed Friendly's to function as neutral territory in a town divided by every kind of boundary except honesty.

"The manager came out of the office eventually," I tell my audience, lighting another cigarette because Grace's story deserves proper ceremony. "Tom Andrews, fifteen years managing Friendly's, never seen anything like it. He took Grace into the back room, had the conversation that managers have with employees who've violated the cardinal rule of customer service: never make the customers uncomfortable with your humanity."

But Tom Andrews had grown up in Sudbury too. Had worked construction with Dan Johnson, had been to the funeral, had watched Grace carry herself with dignity through circumstances that would have broken most people. He gave her a lecture about professionalism and public relations and the importance of maintaining appropriate boundaries between staff and customers.

Then he gave her a raise.

"Fifteen cents an hour," Grace told me that June afternoon, laughing the way people laugh when the irony is too perfect to be tragic. "Fifteen cents for finally saying what everyone in this town knows but pretends they don't. For proving that the help has opinions, feelings, children whose futures matter as much as anyone else's."

The story spread through Sudbury like spilled coffee, staining everything it touched. By Monday, everyone had heard some version of what had happened at Friendly's, though the details varied depending on who was telling it and what they needed it to mean.
