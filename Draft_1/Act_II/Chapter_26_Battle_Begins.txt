# CHAPTER 26: THE BATTLE BEGINS
---

The fear in the green room was thick as the Aqua Net hairspray—Nick could smell both, neither doing much to calm his nerves. He planted himself in the farthest corner from Jackie Marsh, though in a room barely twelve feet wide, it hardly created any distance. Between them lay four years of words not spoken.

The relics left behind said someone once cared—cracked mirror ringed with dead bulbs; a couch older than any of the kids; those stenciled "L-S DRAMA DEPT" folding chairs that squeaked with every anxious shift. Even the mini-fridge, unplugged, cradled a six-pack of ancient Coke. Every inch of the room felt crowded: three bands, seventeen kids, two teachers, all vibrating with anticipation.

For at least the seventh time, Nick ran through his checklist: duct-taped input jack—still holding, spare pick jammed behind the headstock—good, chrome Aerosmith buckle flashing beneath the fluorescents. The scent of shop grease clung to him, stubborn as ever, the evidence of hours at the garage. He caught sight of the persistent stain on his knee, a badge neither he nor his mother could get out.

Stephen Murphy tapped out silent bass lines against the plywood wall, eyes closed. David Cassel fidgeted, drumstick spinning a nervous orbit until it fell—then started again. Mark Pianka obsessively swiped his palms on his jeans. Their auto shop teacher, Joe Pacenka, stood behind, arms folded, his expression worn and proud, as if staking everything on the kids he'd shaped from raw material.

Jackie sat across the cramped space, as motionless as a painting hung for inspection.

Jackie’s posture could have passed for an audition: back straight, hands perfectly folded, shirt freshly pressed—baby blue, as if daring anyone to rumple it. Not even his hair rebelled. Elizabeth perched nearby, her violin case protective across her knees. Ace Callahan flexed his knuckles with crisp little cracks, Max Wesslen lost in his own soundtrack, headphones pressing a groove into his hair.

In the center, where the static was thickest and the air most uncertain, Patty Lally lounged barefoot—sandals kicked aside as if she always walked on this kind of wire. Her long wheat hair its own kind of signal. She offered everyone a steady look, as if she could see through all the jitters. Robin Bosshard’s guitar rested horizontally over both knees; Kristy Wesslen, intent on her hair braids, repeated the same ritual for comfort. Steve Bankuti beat a wordless rhythm on his thighs.

The door snapped open. Chuck Morrison swept in—WBCN’s own morning DJ, all six-foot-plus and TV-perfect hair, his leather jacket throwing back light like a prizefighter’s robe. His presence seemed to chill the room. One hand gripped a clipboard, the other smoothed his already flawless smile, his voice projecting as if the walls were studio glass.

“Here we go! We’re at capacity 290 strong, standing room, fire marshal getting ulcers.” He tried a big laugh; it bounced off stoic faces. He riffed through the lineup: The Jesters (nice, but too nice), Detroit Rock City (full Kiss makeup, tuning up already), The Dead Heads (“if they run ‘Dark Star’ more than ten, we cut their mics”), Talking Heads Talking (“weird but they make you think”), and finally, he twirled for drama: “THE Main Event—our local heroes!” He called the roll—Walking the Line, first up; Crown Jewels, next; Rumours to close. “Questions?” Only Jackie, composed as a cello bow, asked how much time. “Fifteen minutes, top. Four songs. Don’t overrun. Judges are watching.” He beckoned toward the doorway: “Want to see your fate?”

He flung the door wide—a flash of light, the stage, judges like distant mountains at the table: Maxanne Sartori (impossible not to recognize, battered headphones, denim unaged), Carter Alan (leaning away, already unimpressed), Bob Walker (Strawberries Records kingpin). “Prize is $500 plus a hundred hours in the studio—real record time,” Chuck intoned, but Nick’s curt “We know” hung, dry as winter air. Chuck backed out, all bluster fading. Quiet fell, replacing his bravado with the scent of nerves.

The first distorted chords from Detroit Rock City thundered through the wall. Sound washed over everyone in the green room—scrappy, a bit out of tune, but raucous. David muttered about blown speakers; Stephen rolled his eyes. Joe Pacenka rumbled, “Let them play. You focus on yours.” Jackie, silent, placed his palm on the wall, measuring whether he could feel more than hear what was coming. Elizabeth reached for him: “You okay?” Jackie’s reply: “I am fine”—all perfect diction, zero content.

Patty’s gaze flicked between Nick and Jackie. Nick didn’t return it. Too much old summer in that look—too much risk. The middle school talent show, Aerosmith and Queen, two best friends; all the things that had made oil and water mix for a season. He shut that part out, before it smothered him.

Eventually the crowd’s applause meant Detroit Rock City had finished. Chuck’s voice, always too bright, echoed through the monitors with forced cheer. Then came The Dead Heads, and their spacey “Dark Star”—unfolding, endless. Five minutes became eight. Robin deadpanned, “Still the same song?” Patty: “Still the same.” In the monitors, a cough became an instrument. At ten minutes Chuck’s voice executed a mercy killing: congratulations, then abrupt silence. Ace called out the obvious: “That hurt.” Jackie just watched patterns only he could see. Next up, Talking Heads Talking—a kind of angular, jumpy riddle. Confused sounds, then applause that was curious, not indifferent.

“Okay, this is it!” Chuck’s amplified voice soared from outside—“Three bands, three histories—these kids all know each other. Once friends, maybe more. Tonight, they’ll settle it for real!” The crowd’s roar shook the green room’s glue. Chuck kept feeding the flames: “Walking the Line—Aerosmith grit, straight from the shop. Crown Jewels—Queen’s polish, Juilliard future. Rumours—Fleetwood Mac, harmonies that sound like Sunday morning.” Laughter, applause, expectation. Nick felt the story being sold: working-class, well-heeled, and those who wouldn't pick sides. Joe Pacenka's big hand landed on Nick’s shoulder: “Do it like Dan’s here, kid.” Nick, throat tight, could only nod.

Stephen hoisted his bass; David gripped his sticks tighter; Mark flexed his fingers copper-wire tight. Their path to the door was a slow-rolling union of sweat, duct tape, and stubborn hope—the faith of people who build with their hands and never let go.

The door swung open, letting in an unfiltered tide of heat and breath and eight hundred hopes. The stage’s glare felt like a searchlight—Nick’s eyes picked out Maxanne Sartori at her judge’s post, Chuck Morrison poised like a circus ringleader beside his clipboard. This was the moment on which everything balanced.

Jackie, almost ghostly, rallied his own band behind him. Elizabeth stood, Ace and Max gathered their gear. Patty slid her bare feet back into sandals—grounded returning to flight. Three bands, three lives rewritten, one night, the invisible frequency that once connected them all now jittery, uncertain.

The stage lights did not forgive. Nick walked forward no matter.
