# CHAPTER 26: THE BATTLE BEGINS

---

The green room smelled like fear and Aqua Net. Nick Johnson stood in the corner farthest from Jackie Marsh, which wasn't far enough—maybe eight feet of scuffed linoleum and four years of silence between them. Still too close.

Someone had tried to make the room nice once. There was a cracked mirror with bulbs around it, three of them dead. A couch that looked like it had survived the Kennedy administration. Folding chairs with L-S DRAMA DEPT stenciled on the back. A mini-fridge humming in the corner, unplugged, full of someone's forgotten Cokes from 1976. The whole space was maybe twelve by fifteen, and right now it held three complete bands, seventeen teenagers, two teachers, and enough nervous energy to power the building.

Nick checked his guitar for the seventh time. Duct tape holding the input jack. Check. Spare pick wedged behind the strings at the headstock. Check. Chrome Aerosmith belt buckle catching the light. Check. He still smelled like the auto shop—WD-40 and sweat and the particular kind of grease that never completely washes off. His jeans had a stain on the knee from where he'd knelt on the bay floor. He'd tried to scrub it out. Grace had tried. The stain stayed.

Stephen Murphy was leaning against the wall, eyes closed, fingers moving through invisible bass lines. David Cassel spun a drumstick between his fingers, dropped it, picked it up, spun it again. Mark Pianka kept wiping his hands on his jeans like he could wipe the sweat away permanently. Joe Pacenka stood behind them, arms crossed, face carved from tire rubber and bad coffee, watching his boys the way a man watches something he built with his own hands.

Across the room, Jackie Marsh sat perfectly still.

That was the thing about Jackie—even now, even here, he sat like his father was watching. Posture straight. Hands folded. Not a hair out of place. He wore a button-down shirt, for Christ's sake. Baby blue. Pressed. Who wore a pressed shirt to a Battle of the Bands? 

Elizabeth sat next to him, violin case across her lap, watching her brother with something that looked like worry. Ace Callahan cracked his knuckles, stretched his arms, cracked them again. Max Wesslen had his eyes closed, headphones on, nodding to something only he could hear.

And in the middle of the room, taking up no space and somehow filling it completely, Patty Lally sat barefoot on the couch.

She'd kicked her sandals off the moment they'd walked in. That was Patty. Bare feet on cold linoleum, wheat-colored hair down to her waist, looking at everyone like she could see straight through the nerve endings to whatever was underneath. Robin Bosshard sat next to her, guitar across his lap, Swiss accent making everything sound philosophical. Kristy Wesslen was rebraiding her hair for the third time. Steve Bankuti drummed patterns on his thighs, silent, steady.

The door opened. Chuck Morrison walked in, and the temperature in the room dropped five degrees.

Chuck Morrison was WBCN's morning drive guy, and he knew it. Six-foot-two, hair feathered like he'd just walked out of a shampoo commercial, wearing a leather jacket that probably cost more than Nick's mother made in a month. He had a clipboard. He had a smile. He had the kind of voice that sounded like it was coming through speakers even when it wasn't.

"Alright, rockers!" Too loud. Too much. "We've got a full house out there. Eight hundred people. Standing room only in the back. Fire marshal's having a coronary."

He laughed. Nobody else did.

"Okay, let's talk order. We've got four warmup acts, then you three. The Jesters kicked us off—nice kids, a little too nice, you know?" He winked. Nobody knew. "Then Detroit Rock City is setting up now—KISS tribute, full makeup, the works. After them we've got The Dead Heads, and let me tell you, if they play 'Dark Star' for more than ten minutes, someone's pulling the plug."

A couple nervous laughs. Patty smiled. Nick didn't.

"Then we've got Talking Heads Talking from Boston Latin—those kids are weird, but they're interesting weird, you know? And then—" Chuck paused for effect, loving himself. "Then we get to the main event. You three."

He consulted his clipboard like he didn't already have it memorized.

"Walking the Line, you're up first. Crown Jewels, you're second. Rumours, you bring it home." He looked up. "Questions?"

"How long do we get?" Jackie's voice. Precise. Controlled. Not a single contraction.

"Fifteen minutes. Four songs max. Don't go over. The judges are timing." Chuck pointed toward the door. "Speaking of which, they just arrived. You want to see who's deciding your fate?"

He opened the door wider. Through it, Nick could see the auditorium. Not the crowd—the lights were too bright for that—but the stage. The amps. The microphone stands. And in front of it all, a table.

Three people sat there.

The woman in the middle, Nick recognized immediately. Everyone did. Maxanne Sartori. WBCN's afternoon drive. The one who'd broken Aerosmith when nobody else would play them. She wore the same headphones she'd worn in every photo Nick had ever seen—big, padded, held together with electrical tape. Her denim jacket looked older than Nick. She was writing something in a notebook, not looking up, not performing for anyone.

Next to her, Carter Alan—younger, sharper, leaning back in his chair like he was bored already. And on the other side, Bob Walker from Strawberries Records, the guy who controlled studio time like it was gold.

"The prize," Chuck said, voice dropping to mock-serious, "is five hundred dollars and one hundred hours of studio time at Strawberries' facility. You could record an actual album. You could be the next—"

"We know what the prize is." Nick heard his own voice. Flat. Working-class flat.

Chuck's smile flickered. "Right. Okay. So. Sit tight. Opening acts are almost done, then it's showtime."

He left. The door clicked shut.

Silence.

Then, from beyond the door, muffled but unmistakable: the opening riff of "Detroit Rock."

Someone in Detroit Rock City hit a chord that was almost in tune. The crowd roared. Through the green room's wall, it sounded like the ocean—all white noise and low rumble, punctuated by the thump of a kick drum that was definitely too loud.

"They're gonna blow the speakers," David muttered.

"They're gonna blow something," Stephen said.

Joe Pacenka finally spoke. "Focus on your own show." His voice was sandpaper and midnight shifts. "They do their thing. You do yours."

Nick nodded. Checked his guitar again. Duct tape, pick, belt buckle. All still there. All still holding together, barely.

Across the room, Jackie stood. Walked to the wall. Put his hand flat against it. Testing something. Feeling the vibration of the music through plaster and wood. When he turned back, his face was unreadable.

Elizabeth touched his arm. "You okay?"

"I am fine."

Two words. No contraction. Not fine.

Patty was watching them both. Nick felt her eyes, didn't look. Couldn't look. If he looked at Patty, he'd remember summer camp. He'd remember when Jackie was his best friend. He'd remember the middle school talent show when they'd won by combining Aerosmith and Queen and proving that oil and water could mix if you cared enough.

He didn't want to remember.

Detroit Rock City finished. Applause. Not huge, but respectable. Chuck Morrison's voice boomed through the monitors: "Let's hear it for Detroit Rock City! Gene Simmons, eat your heart out!"

More applause. Then silence.

Then the unmistakable opening notes of "Dark Star."

"Oh Christ," Mark whispered. "Here we go."

The Dead Heads were technically proficient. Nick could hear that even through the wall. They had the tone right, that dreamy Grateful Dead float. But "Dark Star" was a jam song. It could go three minutes or thirty. And from the sound of it, these kids were aiming for thirty.

Five minutes in, Robin looked at Patty. "This is still the same song, yes?"

"Same song."

"How long?"

"However long they want."

Eight minutes in, someone in the audience coughed. You could hear it through the monitors. The song kept going.

Ten minutes in, Chuck Morrison's voice cut through: "Okay, let's give it up for The Dead Heads! Beautiful playing, guys, beautiful—"

The music stopped. Confused silence. Then applause, lighter than before.

"That's gonna hurt them," Ace said.

Jackie didn't respond. He was sitting again, hands folded, staring at something no one else could see.

Talking Heads Talking came on next. Weird, angular, the bass line like someone solving a math problem in real time. The crowd didn't know what to do with it. You could hear the confusion through the wall. But there was something there—something smart, something different. When they finished, the applause was puzzled but genuine.

Then Chuck Morrison's voice again. Bigger now. Building to something.

"Alright, Lincoln-Sudbury, are you ready for the REAL show?"

The crowd roared.

"We've got three bands. Three very different bands. And here's the thing—these kids know each other. Some of them used to be friends. Some of them used to be more than friends. Tonight, they're gonna settle it the only way that matters: on stage."

Nick's jaw tightened. Stephen caught his eye, shook his head slightly. Don't.

"First up, we've got Walking the Line—Aerosmith covers, raw and real and loud. These guys rehearse in an auto shop. They play like it."

Laughter from the crowd. Not mean. Curious.

"Then we've got Crown Jewels—Queen covers, technical perfection, the works. This band's got a kid who's heading to Juilliard. Juilliard, people."

Different laughter. Impressed. Distant.

"And finally, Rumours—Fleetwood Mac, harmony and heart. They rehearse in a church, and you can hear it."

Applause already starting.

Nick felt something twist in his chest. Chuck Morrison was selling them. Selling the story. Working-class vs. upper-class vs. the one who wouldn't choose sides. It was all narrative. All performance. And underneath it, Nick could feel the real thing—the years, the silence, the death of his father, the death of a friendship, the way Jackie had just stopped looking at him one day and never started again.

"Walking the Line," Chuck's voice boomed. "You're up in two minutes."

Joe Pacenka put a hand on Nick's shoulder. Squeezed once. "Play it like Dan's watching."

Nick nodded. Couldn't speak.

Stephen picked up his bass. David grabbed his sticks. Mark flexed his fingers. They moved toward the door as a unit—practiced, prepared, held together by duct tape and auto shop time and the kind of stubborn working-class faith that said if you built something with your own hands, it mattered.

The door opened.

Sound hit like a wall.

Eight hundred people. Hot lights. The smell of sweat and popcorn and teenage dreams colliding. Maxanne Sartori at the judges' table, pen poised, watching. Chuck Morrison with his microphone and his leather jacket and his clipboard of fates.

Nick stepped toward the stage.

Behind him, Jackie Marsh stood. Elizabeth beside him, Ace and Max gathering their things. Across the room, Patty Lally slipped her sandals back on. Bare feet to leather. Grounding to flight.

Three bands. Three different stories. Same town. Same night. Same frequency they'd lost somewhere between childhood and now.

The stage lights were white and merciless.

Nick walked into them anyway.
