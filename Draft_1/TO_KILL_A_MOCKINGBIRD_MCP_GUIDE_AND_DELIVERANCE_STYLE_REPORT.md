# To Kill A Mockingbird: Fiction Writing MCP Guide & Deliverance-Style Report

**Purpose:** Apply all MCPs to fiction writing using Harper Lee's *To Kill A Mockingbird* (1960) as the teaching example, in the same structure as the Marathon Man and Deliverance reports.  
**Teaching Example:** *To Kill A Mockingbird* by Harper Lee  
**TKAM Context:** Scout (narrator, child); Jem (older brother); Atticus (father, lawyer); Tom Robinson (wrongly accused Black man); Boo Radley (recluse); Bob Ewell (accuser); Mayella Ewell; Calpurnia; Miss Maudie; Dill. Maycomb, Alabama, 1930s. Atticus defends Tom; verdict guilty; Tom killed escaping; Bob Ewell attacks Scout and Jem; Boo saves them and kills Ewell; sheriff and Atticus agree to say Ewell fell on his knife; Scout walks Boo home and “stands in his shoes.” Themes: racial injustice, empathy, loss of innocence, moral courage, the mockingbird (innocents destroyed).

**Unified Point of View:** Atticus should take the case. He is assigned it; refusing would contradict everything he teaches Scout and Jem about courage and fairness. The novel’s moral spine and the children’s loss of innocence depend on his choice. All five brothers agree.

### Question 2: Why doesn’t Boo Radley appear in person until the end?

## Part IV: Expanded TKAM Examples (10 Sentences Each) – Key MCPs

### Knowledge Graph Memory MCP

**Fiction Use (10 sentences):** Knowledge Graph Memory MCP stores characters, relationships, plot threads, and world-building in a graph so you can query them. For *To Kill A Mockingbird*, you would store Scout (narrator, child, grows from innocence to understanding), Jem (older brother, more damaged by the verdict), Atticus (lawyer, moral center, takes Tom’s case), Tom Robinson (accused, innocent, killed escaping), Boo Radley (recluse, saves children, mockingbird), Bob Ewell (accuser, attacker), and Mayella. You would store relationships: Atticus defends Tom; Boo protects Scout and Jem; Ewell attacks them. You would store timeline: trial → verdict → Tom’s death → Ewell’s threat → attack → cover-up. When you ask “Who knows Boo killed Ewell?” the graph returns: Scout, Jem, Atticus, Sheriff Tate—and they agree not to say. When you ask “When does Scout understand Boo?” you get: on the porch, after the attack. This keeps story truth consistent across chapters and revisions.

**TKAM Example (10 sentences):** *“What are Scout’s unresolved conflicts at the end?”* The Knowledge Graph returns: (1) The verdict—she saw the system fail. (2) Tom’s death—another mockingbird destroyed. (3) The attack—she was in the costume, couldn’t see. (4) Boo—she finally meets him and “stands in his shoes.” The graph tracks Scout’s arc: innocent → witness to injustice → survivor → empathy. It maintains timeline: trial, verdict, Tom’s death, Ewell’s menace, Halloween, attack, Boo’s rescue, cover-up, walk home. By querying the graph before writing each scene, you ensure Scout’s knowledge, Atticus’s choices, and the mockingbird motif stay aligned. The graph is your single source of truth for the novel.

### Sequential Thinking MCP (Reference)

**Fiction Use (10 sentences):** Sequential Thinking MCP forces stepwise reasoning so plot logic and character decisions are explicit. For *To Kill A Mockingbird*, when you ask “Why does Atticus accept the cover-up?” the MCP breaks it down: (1) Boo killed Ewell to save the children. (2) Exposing Boo would put him on display and harm him. (3) “It’s a sin to kill a mockingbird.” (4) The sheriff can report Ewell fell on his knife. (5) Atticus’s duty to the law is balanced by his duty to protect the innocent. Each step can be checked against the manuscript. When you ask “Is the guilty verdict necessary?” the MCP lays out: theme of racial injustice, children’s loss of innocence, Tom’s tragedy, and the novel’s moral argument. It reduces plot holes by making cause-and-effect visible. The chain of reasoning becomes part of your editorial record.

**TKAM Example (10 sentences):** *“Should the novel end with the cover-up?”* Sequential Thinking produces: (1) Boo is a mockingbird—harmless, damaged by the town. (2) Exposing him would repeat the sin. (3) Sheriff Tate can report Ewell fell on his knife. (4) Atticus resists, then accepts when Scout’s safety and Boo’s are at stake. (5) Scout’s walk home with Boo requires no arrest. (6) The ending pays off the “stand in his shoes” theme. (7) Lee’s moral logic: mercy and protection over rigid legality. (8) Conclusion: End with the cover-up. (9) The chain supports the final choice. (10) You can cite it if you later question the scene.

### Sequential Thinking Ultra

**Fiction Use (10 sentences):** Sequential Thinking Ultra adds bias checks, quality metrics, and branching. For *To Kill A Mockingbird*, when you ask “Is the guilty verdict necessary?” Ultra explores paths: guilty (theme of injustice, 96%), acquittal (happier but weaker, 35%). It scores each for consistency with Lee’s themes and 1930s Alabama. It flags biases: e.g., are you assuming guilty because it’s “expected”? Evidence supports it. It flags when choices are driven by convenience. Quality metrics give confidence scores. Branching lets you explore alternatives without losing the main argument.

**TKAM Example (10 sentences):** *“Is the guilty verdict necessary?”* Ultra explores: (1) Guilty verdict—scores 96% for thematic fit, 94% for historical plausibility; bias check: not confirmation bias, narrative and theme require it. (2) Acquittal—scores 35%; would soften the book’s critique. (3) Hung jury—scores 55%; possible but less clear. Ultra’s recommendation: Guilty is necessary. The bias detector notes: we are not overemphasizing tragedy for its own sake; the novel has established Maycomb’s racism and the trial’s stakes. Also: ensure the verdict lands through the children’s eyes—thematic impact depends on their loss of innocence.

### Sequential Thinking MAS (Multi-Agent System)

**Fiction Use (10 sentences):** Sequential Thinking MAS uses five agents—Factual, Emotional, Critical, Creative, Synthesis—for multi-angle analysis. For *To Kill A Mockingbird*, when you ask “Should Atticus take the case?” the Factual agent checks: he is assigned the case; his character is established; refusal would be out of character. The Emotional agent evaluates: What do the children learn? What does the town feel? The Critical agent checks: thematic consistency, plot necessity. The Creative agent suggests alternatives (e.g., another lawyer)—all weaker. The Synthesis agent combines views. Each agent surfaces issues the others miss.

**TKAM Example (10 sentences):** *“Should the novel end with the cover-up?”* MAS produces: Factual: Boo killed Ewell; only the children and Atticus and Tate know; Tate has discretion. Emotional: Boo has been harmed by the town; exposing him would be another injury; Scout’s walk home is the empathy payoff. Critical: Mockingbird motif requires protecting Boo; the lie is the novel’s final moral choice. Creative: Truth would force Boo into the spotlight and destroy the ending. Synthesis: All agents converge—end with the cover-up. The MAS output gives confidence that the decision is sound across logic, emotion, theme, and structure.

### Cognee MCP

**Fiction Use:** Graph-RAG that finds links between concepts you didn’t explicitly create. For *To Kill A Mockingbird*, it can surface that the mockingbird motif connects to Tom and Boo, that the trial connects to the children’s loss of innocence, that “standing in his shoes” connects to Atticus’s earlier lesson and Scout’s final scene with Boo.

**TKAM Example:** *“How does the mockingbird motif connect to the novel’s themes?”* → Cognee traces: Mockingbird (innocents who do no harm) → Tom (destroyed by the system) → Boo (destroyed by the town’s gossip, protected by the cover-up) → “It’s a sin to kill a mockingbird” → Scout’s understanding. It makes the symbol’s meaning explicit for revision and thematic consistency.

## Part VI: Orchestration Flow (TKAM Example)

