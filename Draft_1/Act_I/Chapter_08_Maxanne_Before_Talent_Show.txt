CHAPTER 8: BEFORE THE TALENT SHOW

Three hours and forty-seven minutes left.

I'm sitting here in Studio B, the clock on the wall ticking down, and it hits me: I've been feeding you the end of the story without ever serving up the start. The Battle of the Bands in 1979—that's the big finale, sure, but tonight we need to rewind the tape. Not the first garage jam, not even the friendship between Nick and Jackie, but the instant two best friends looked at a middle school talent show and thought, hell, why not give it a shot?

Because that's the thing about music—it's not just about talent or technique or even passion. It's about the moments when you decide to step out of the shadows and into the light, when you choose to share something that matters to you with people who might not understand it, might not appreciate it, might not even care.

I take a sip of coffee—gone cold hours ago, but it's fuel either way—and I think about May 15, 1975. The day Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh stopped being just two kids who played music together and became something that scared the hell out of everyone who thought they understood how the world was supposed to work.

May 15, 1975. The annual Ephraim Curtis Middle School Talent Show. Open to all middleschoolers who dare, one winner, and a gymnasium full of parents who'd spent the afternoon convincing themselves that their children were special without being too different, talented without being too unusual, competitive without being too ambitious.

Outside the booth windows, Boston glitters in the October darkness, but I'm seeing Sudbury in May, the kind of spring afternoon that makes thirteen-year-olds believe anything is possible.

Now, I need you to understand something about middle school talent shows in suburban Massachusetts in 1975. They weren't about discovering new talent or celebrating artistic expression. They were about maintaining the social order, about proving that the kids who were supposed to win would win, that the system worked exactly the way it was designed to work.

I reach for the cassette on my desk, the one labeled "Demo - Nick/Jackie - Not Authorized - March 1979." But tonight I'm not playing that tape. Tonight I'm telling you about the day they decided to make it, about the moment when two best friends looked at each other and said, "Why not us?"

Nick and Jackie had been playing music together for almost two years by then.

The engineer kid out there is scrolling through social media, double-tapping photos while I'm trying to tell a story that matters. The irony makes me smile. He grew up thinking you could make music on a laptop, that all you needed was the right software. He never saw what four cinderblock walls and a concrete floor can do when you have nothing left to lose and everything to prove.

But here's the thing about garage sessions—they're safe. They're contained. They're for people who already understand what you're trying to do. The talent show was different. The talent show was public. The talent show was for everyone—parents who'd never heard rock and roll, teachers who thought music was something you learned from sheet music, kids who'd never been to a garage session and wouldn't understand why two boys with acoustic guitars mattered.

I pause, letting the silence breathe. Radio silence. Just for a moment.

The decision to enter the talent show happened on a Tuesday afternoon in April. Nick was in the garage, working on a new riff he'd heard on WBCN—something by Led Zeppelin that he was trying to figure out on his acoustic guitar. Jackie was sitting on Dan's workbench, watching Nick's fingers move across the frets, recognizing the classical structure beneath the rock and roll surface.

"We should enter the talent show," Jackie said.

Nick looked up from his guitar, surprised. "What talent show?"

"The middle school one. May 15th. Mrs. Reed mentioned it in music class today."

Nick went back to his riff, but the idea taking hold. 

"Why would we do that?"

"Because we're good," Jackie said, and there was something in his voice that surprised both of them. "Because we're really good, and nobody knows it except us."

I glance down at my glass, letting the moment settle.

That was the moment, right there. Not when they started playing music together, not when they became best friends, but when Jackie said "nobody knows it except us" and Nick realized he was right. They were good. They were really good. And the garage sessions had been their secret, their safe space, their proof that different approaches could work together instead of against each other.

But secrets don't change the world. Secrets don't challenge the social order. Secrets don't make parents question whether their children are being prepared for the right kind of success.


They spent the next three weeks preparing. Not practicing—preparing. Because this wasn't about getting better at what they already did. This was about figuring out how to translate garage session magic into something that would work in a middle school gymnasium, with fluorescent lights and folding chairs and parents who'd never heard of Freddie Mercury.

Nick wanted to do covers. "Walk This Way" by Aerosmith, "Bohemian Rhapsody" by Queen—songs that would prove they understood rock and roll, that they weren't just kids playing acoustic guitars in a garage.

Their final choice was brilliant. A mashup of "Bohemian Rhapsody" and "Walk This Way"—Queen meets Aerosmith, classical training meets garage band raw power. It was everything they were, everything they'd become, everything they were trying to prove about the power of collaboration.

But here's the thing about mashups—they're risky. They require the audience to understand both songs, to appreciate the skill required to blend them, to recognize that what they're hearing isn't just performance but creation. And middle school talent shows aren't designed for risk. They're designed for safety, for predictability, for the kind of excellence that doesn't challenge anyone's assumptions about how the world is supposed to work.

The day of the talent show, Nick and Jackie arrived at school with their guitars and their arrangement and absolutely no expectations. They weren't trying to win—they were trying to share. They weren't trying to prove anything to anyone else—they were trying to prove something to themselves. That their friendship mattered. That their music mattered. That different approaches could create something bigger than individual talent.

Karen Bowers was there too, of course. Karen Bowers was always there. Blonde, perfectly prepared, with a voice trained by private instructors who charged more per lesson than Grace Johnson made in a day at Friendly's. Her stage presence suggested she'd been born for spotlights, her technique reflected years of coaching designed to eliminate any trace of regional accent or personal quirk that might interfere with commercial appeal.

She was performing "Tomorrow" from "Annie." Of course she was. Because Karen Bowers represented everything suburban communities thought they wanted: discipline, investment in proper training, predictable excellence. She sang like she was auditioning for roles she was destined to win, performed like she was doing everyone a favor by sharing her obvious talent.

An ambulance wails down Tremont as I drum my fingers on the battered studio desk. Behind the glass, my engineer—a college kid in a Bruins hoodie—peers out from his fortress of mixing boards and empty Red Bull cans, eyeing me the way you watch a pot that might boil over.

But here's what Karen Bowers didn't understand—and what Nick and Jackie did. Music isn't about technical perfection. It's about connection.

Nick and Jackie weren't trying to be perfect. They were trying to connect.

And that's what scared the hell out of everyone who thought they understood how the world was supposed to work.

I crack my knuckles and lean into the microphone one more time.

The middle school gymnasium was packed—parents with cameras, kids with attitudes, teachers hoping for the best and expecting chaos. The fluorescent lights cast that familiar institutional glow that makes everything look slightly unreal.

Nick and Jackie were scheduled to perform sixth in the eight-act lineup. Karen Bowers was scheduled to close the show, the traditional spot for the inevitable winner, performing "Tomorrow" from "Annie" with polish that made everyone else feel like amateurs.

But sometimes, very occasionally, something happens that nobody sees coming. Sometimes two kids with acoustic guitars step onto a stage and change everything.

And that's exactly what happened on May 15, 1975, in the Ephraim Curtis Middle School gymnasium, when Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh stopped being just two kids who played music together and became something that scared the hell out of everyone who thought they understood how the world was supposed to work.

Clock on the wall says I'm running out of night and out of bourbon, too. Out there, Boston sprawls under neon and streetlamp, all lost hopes and half-remembered melodies, while my voice drifts from this booth to whoever's listening—a ghost on the FM dial, clinging to stories some folks would rather forget.

You want the truth? Here it is: the real revolution never happened on a record label's timeline, and it damn sure didn't have a sponsor. Sometimes harmony loses to noise, and the noise comes wearing a tie and a fixed smile. That's the story I've carried, and the one you need to hear.

But first, we need to talk about what happened when Nick and Jackie stepped onto that stage and proved that sometimes the most dangerous thing you can do is be exactly who you are.
