## CHAPTER 31: THE ANNOUNCEMENT & AFTERMATH

### **The Judges' Deliberation**

As Walking the Line stormed offstage, sweat still shining on their faces, the four judges huddled together at their table beneath the splatter of house lights. Their score sheets were a mess—numbers, doodles, half-scribbled comments, a couple of nicotine smudges—and none of it could capture what had just gone down.

Maxanne Sartori stared at her crumpled sheet. The columns for "Technique," "Stage Presence," "Originality"—they suddenly felt like a joke. “How the hell do you give a number to something like that?” she muttered, her Cambridge vowels flat and weary.

Carter Alan was chewing his pen and drawing an endless row of guitar necks up the margin. “I’ve seen all the Boston garage legends, man—The Cars, Mission of Burma, even Aerosmith at Mother’s. But this? This was… different. This wasn’t even about the contest anymore.”

Bob Walker from Strawberries Records was checking his battered Timex but not because he wanted to leave. He was thinking about the words he'd use to spin this for the label back in Kenmore Square. “You know they’re gonna ask who won, but how do I explain that the whole town just got lit up like a J. Geils encore?”

And Chuck Morrison—always moving, jittery as the MBTA at rush hour—kept pacing behind the table, a bundle of nerves, eyes wild. “Jesus,” he blurted. “We’re not picking a winner here. We’re picking what Lincoln-Sudbury even is after tonight.”

Maxanne looked at the others, pages trembling in her hands. “What if we’re not supposed to judge it?” she said, barely more than a whisper. “What if we just… acknowledge what happened?”

### **The Impossible Decision**

The four exchanged glances. Every one of them knew it: they weren’t just deciding the Battle of the Bands—they were cracking open the walls that split Lincoln and Sudbury, the ones that separated the rich kids from the strivers, the haves from the have-nots, the inside crowd from the outliers. Everything the radio, the bands, the town itself cared about, right there in that hush.

"How do you stack up soul against skill?" Carter finally asked, sounding more like a kid from Somerville than Boston's next FM legend.

"Or chemistry against practice hours?" Bob added, flipping his score sheet like it might have written the answer on the back.

Chuck’s pacing picked up, sneakers squeaking out his impatience. “How do you choose between the band that’s tight and the band that just made fifty parents cry on their damn feet?”

Maxanne let her pen drop. “We don’t pick. We recognize.”

### **The Announcement**

Chuck Morrison grabbed the mic as if it weighed a hundred pounds and swaggered to the front of the stage, band shirts and denim barely hiding that barely-contained lightning inside him. The hush of the gymnasium was thick—no one wanted to break the spell. A few mutters—“Is this for real? Who’s it gonna be?”—surfaced from the back, mingling with the scent of sweat, donuts, and spent dreams.

He looked out at the rows of bleachers, at the teachers, rockers, burnouts, jocks, every kid whose life had just gotten rearranged by three songs and a couple of rough-edged truths.

“Alright, listen up!” Chuck thundered, his Boston drawl crackling through the battered PA. “Nobody expected tonight to go down like this. Not us—definitely not you.”

A nervous laugh fluttered up. Some of the parents shifted uncomfortably. Chuck grinned.

“You all saw it—tonight wasn’t just some Battle of the Bands. It was the moment this place remembered what music’s supposed to do. It didn’t just light up the gym—it tore down the lines we built between us. That’s what happens when it’s real.”

He let that hang, sweat glimmering down his neck. “So, after way too much arguing and not enough scoring, the judges have reached a decision. But first—I want you to remember what walked in the room. For once it wasn’t North versus South, rich versus poor, comics versus quarterbacks. It was Lincoln-Sudbury and it was Boston, and it was all of us, together.”

Another beat—a whole auditorium leaning forward.

“Okay. The winner of the first annual WBCN Battle of the Bands…—and if you know what’s good for you, you’ll keep playing like you played tonight forever—”

He grinned, milking it, waiting until the tension almost snapped.

“—is… Rumours!”

The place exploded—screams, stomps on the bleachers, Patty’s crew going nuts in the back. But it wasn’t just for the band. It was for what they all got to feel, if only for a night. This wasn’t about a trophy. It was Boston, for real. And it rocked.

### **The Aftermath**

As Patty walked onto the stage to accept her prize, something began to happen in the auditorium. The social boundaries that had defined Lincoln-Sudbury for years began to dissolve completely. Kids from the northeast section started talking to kids from the southwest section. The divisions that had separated them—quarters and comics, advantages and disadvantages, privilege and struggle—began to feel artificial, temporary, like costumes they'd been wearing without realizing it.

Grace Johnson stood up and walked over to Dr. Marsh, who was sitting in the front row with Elizabeth and Ace. They'd never spoken before, but now they were talking about their sons, about the music they'd just heard, about the possibility that maybe the divisions weren't as real as they'd thought.

Sarah Morrison, who'd been sitting in the northeast section with the other kids from the wrong side of Hop Brook, stood up and walked over to Tommy Morrison, who'd been sitting in the southwest section with the other kids from the right side of Hop Brook. They were cousins, but they'd never talked at school because they'd been assigned to different sections, different worlds, different versions of reality.

Lisa Henderson, who'd been sitting in the northwest section with the other kids who didn't know which side they were supposed to be on, stood up and walked over to Danny Kowalski, who'd been sitting in the southeast section with the other kids who were trying to figure out where they belonged. They'd never met before, but now they were talking about music, about the possibility that maybe the divisions weren't as real as they'd thought.

The auditorium was transforming. The Battle of the Bands was becoming something else entirely—a reminder of what was possible when people stopped competing and started connecting.

### **The Recognition**

As the applause died down, Nick and Jackie looked at each other across the auditorium. They'd both lost the Battle of the Bands, but they'd both won something much more important. They'd both been reminded of what music was supposed to be about—connection, not competition. They'd both been reminded of what they'd once had, what they'd lost, what might still be possible.

"She remembers," Nick whispered, looking at Patty, who was standing on stage with her prize, looking out at the audience with the kind of understanding that only comes from someone who'd been there when it all began.

"She remembers," Jackie whispered back, looking at Nick, understanding that they'd both been wrong about what the other represented, that they'd both been wrong about what the divisions meant, that they'd both been wrong about what was possible.

The frequency still existed. They just had to be brave enough to find it.
