## CHAPTER 28: JACKIE'S PERFORMANCE

### **Crown Jewels (8:20 PM)**

There comes a hush before the good bands go on, the kind that drapes itself over an auditorium like a heavy wool blanket. At 8:20 sharp, the Crown Jewels strode out into it, Jack Marsh front and center, carrying his Martin D-28 with a grip so familiar it might as well have been his birthright. But this evening—unlike the others—there was an extra step to everything Jackie did, the way a craftsman checks his work twice if he knows the boss is watching.

Up in the front row sat Dr. Marsh, spine straight as a ruler, tie knotted with engineer’s precision, jaw set the way only fathers with high hopes and little patience did. If he approved of the setlist, it didn’t show; if he disapproved, even less. Beside Jackie, Elizabeth stood with her violin, eyes pinched in concentration. Ace and Max completed the tableau: four kids on the edge between youth and a world that wanted them to pick sides—its or their own.

Jack ran his hand once over the piano, the keys gleaming beneath the stage lights, the same piano he’d been told was meant for “serious” students. For a moment he caught sight of his reflection in the lacquer—a boy with too many expectations hung on his frame. He squared his shoulders. The only thing to do now was play.

### **"Killer Queen"**

Jackie counted them in—one, two, three, four—and the opening bars of “Killer Queen” snapped through the gym with a precision so crisp you could spread it on toast. There was nothing loose about the way his fingers danced through the chords; the discipline was visible, owed to years of lessons and careful practice. But then came the sideways grin—a flash of that mischief that craved a song with swagger. 

Elizabeth’s violin slithered into the melody, adding a silkiness Queen themselves would have tipped their hats to. For those three minutes, Jackie embodied the song’s sly spirit, hunched over the keys with the air of a man both performing for the crowd and for someone in particular who would never admit to being impressed. Every time he hit an improvisation—a glissando, a flourish—Jackie threw a glance over his shoulder at his bandmates, daring them to keep up. He didn’t miss that Dr. Marsh’s hands stayed tightly laced, but by the end of the song, even the old man couldn’t suppress a nod.

The applause hit like a strong wind—tidy but forceful. Jackie smiled, already leaning in for the next song, the passion burning bright but anxious beneath his collar.

### **"Somebody to Love"**

No break, only a small bow of Jack’s head, and he rolled right into “Somebody to Love.” This one, he knew, could go either way. The notes called for not just polish but a kind of ache—not pure heartsickness, but a yearning for something he could never name outright. The band followed his lead, Elizabeth’s harmonies rising up behind him.

Jackie knew how his father wanted the song: steady tempo, every note struck as written, voice clear as daylight and never threatening to crack. But that’s not how the record sounded. That’s not even what Jackie loved about it. Halfway through the first chorus, he let the polish go. He closed his eyes, pulled the microphone close, and let something softer slip out—a faint tremor in the highest notes, a catch in the timing that suggested he’d been up late living instead of rehearsing.

A flicker crossed Dr. Marsh’s face. Not anger, not quite; maybe it was confusion, maybe a memory. Jackie didn’t care. He pounded the piano with a righteous energy, his whole frame flexing at the final chorus, a sixteen-year-old boy pleading not just for love, but to be heard for the music that lived in him and not the perfection expected from him. The audience responded more slowly this time, unsure if they’d heard a mistake or something honest. The applause followed, filling up the hush he left in the air.

### **"Bohemian Rhapsody"**

Jackie didn’t step back. He lifted his chin, rolled his sleeves, and signaled for the band to follow him into madness. “Bohemian Rhapsody.” The one his father had said was too much—too gaudy, too self-indulgent, not “serious” music. But tonight, that was the point.

The first lines came quiet, almost conversational, his voice threading through the lyrics like a confession. Elizabeth’s violin shadowed the melody, Ace tapping a slow funeral march on the snare. Jackie was more than himself now—he was the center of something bigger, part of a complicated machine built out of risk and harmony.

The ballad passed; drums crashed in, Max’s bass thrummed the room’s bones. Suddenly Jackie was roaring, the rock-and-roll gospel spilling out of him unfiltered. Gone was the caution, the eye to the front row. He banged the keys until the pitch bled into emotion, singing as if the whole crowd might finally hear what pulsed beneath his surface: the hunger for something wild and unscripted, the joy too fierce for technical neatness.

As the operatic lines hit, Jackie reached for the near-impossible falsetto and found it. Maybe he'd been holding his breath all year for a chance to let it out. The band swelled, Elizabeth’s violin ascending so sharp and high it nearly stung. The auditorium sat glued, their faces alive with something between alarm and awe.

By the final chord, Jackie was spent, chest heaving—not in the tidy way his father practiced breathing exercises, but like a kid who’d sprinted out of a burning house. The silence that followed was total, save the echo of the last note in the rafters. For a second, he couldn’t tell if the performance had landed or if he’d just leapt alone off a ledge.

Then someone stood, clapping so hard their wedding ring flashed in the lights. More followed. All at once, the crowd was on its feet—some shouting, some just standing open-mouthed. Dr. Marsh looked up at the stage, hands unclenched at last, as if realizing his son had finally given him something unrepeatable.

Jackie didn’t smile. He took in the crowd—all those faces, all those expectations—and for the first time in years, didn’t think about what came next. For better or worse, he’d left everything on the stage, heart bare as the piano keys. Win or lose, he’d played the music he loved, the way he needed to play it.

Somewhere in the wings, Nick watched, unreadable as ever.

For Jackie, that was enough. For now.



---

**Transition:** As Crown Jewels left the stage, the energy in the auditorium was electric. Jackie had just proven that privilege could be transformed into art, that technical excellence could carry emotional truth. But the night wasn't over yet. Walking the Line was about to take the stage, and Nick Johnson was about to prove that authenticity could be just as powerful as perfection.
