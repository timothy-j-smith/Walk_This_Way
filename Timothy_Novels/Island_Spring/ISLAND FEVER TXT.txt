ISLAND FEVER

A NOVEL

TIMOTHY J. SMITH


Chapter 1: The Naming of Things
You know I'm feeling pretty hungry and everything. It's Wednesday. Maggie's sailing with her ex-boyfriend and some college friends around the islands of Penobscot Bay. I guess I'm too young to hang with that crowd, but Maggie said I act older than I am. I don't believe that really—I don't feel older, I feel younger—but it wouldn't be cool for the ex to be seeing me with Maggie. She likes to hold me, and I like to be held.
My name is Calvin Shipley, but everyone calls me Oak—short for Oakley, which was my grandfather's name. Dad started calling me that when I was little because he said I was "sturdy as an oak tree," but I think it stuck because it's easier than Calvin. Sounds less formal. Less like the son of Dr. Shipley, cancer researcher and weekend warrior.
The thing is, nobody knows the real names of these islands. Not the way me and Sissy named them. We think other people should call them by our names because they make a whole lot more sense—Rock Island, Half Moon Island, Deer Island, One Tree Island—instead of whatever boring names some dead politicians stuck them with. St. Agnes, McGovern, Kennedy, Lewis. Who gives a damn about those guys anyway? I got to see all the islands from Dad's little prop plane two years ago, flying up the coast while he searched for some fishing spot he'd read about in the New Yorker. He likes to think of himself as this model outdoorsman, all adventurous and everything, but he leads too comfortable a lifestyle. Reading in his adjustable leather chair with those tiny glasses perched on his nose, he could never make it alone in the wilderness. Not really.
This island though—Doughnut Island—it's different. Shaped like a ring with a hole in the middle where Horse Pond sits, all dried up and swampy now, mosquitoes thick as politicians at election time. The woods are dark even in daylight, white birch trees with bark that peels off like tissue paper, soft and papery between your fingers. When you strip away the dead outer layers, the wood underneath is pale as bone. Moss grows thick on everything, and there's this dampness that gets into your bones and stays there, like a chill you can't shake.
The island has its own smell—salt and seaweed and something else, something organic and alive that's both beautiful and slightly rotten. It's the smell of things growing and dying at the same time, the eternal cycle that Mom's always talking about in her therapy voice. The nose is a short connection to the brain, and every breath here imprints itself in your memory.
My parents call this their third trip here. They always point out the exact campsite where Sissy was supposedly conceived on their honeymoon eighteen years ago, and they add proudly—especially when Sissy's around—that they were both virgins at the time. This always makes me feel sick hearing them talk this way. I only feel lucky the comment's not directed at me.
But I think they know Sissy's not a virgin anymore. I've never asked her, but you can tell by the way she carries herself. She's got this confidence now, this awareness of her body that wasn't there before. Or at least she did until Sam the Red Head started hanging around. Now she acts like she's never been with a guy before, all giggly and fake, like she's auditioning for the role of innocent girlfriend.
The island strips away all our suburban pretenses. No running water except what we pump from the well that tastes of minerals and earth. No electricity, just kerosene lanterns that cast dancing shadows and smell of burning oil. Just the Coleman stove and whatever we can scavenge from the sea. It forces us all together in ways that feel dangerous, like pressure building in a pot with the lid screwed down too tight. You can feel something coming, something that's been building for months, maybe years. The island doesn't let you hide from it.
Chapter 2: The Arrangement
So here I am, stuck for three weeks on Doughnut Island with Mom, my sister Sissy, and her boyfriend Sam the Red Head. That's what I call him—Sam the Red Head—because it pisses Sissy off, and because his hair is this aggressive orange that catches fire in sunlight like a torch. His real name is Samuel something-or-other, but I refuse to dignify him with his full name until he proves he deserves it. Which may be never.
Dad will helicopter in on weekends because he claims he has to work, but I think he's still having an affair with Miss Pantyhose, or doing something else peculiar. I like that word—peculiar. Dad used it about me once when he thought I couldn't hear, asking Mom if she thought I was "peculiar." The way he said it made it sound like a disease, something that needed to be diagnosed and treated.
The second weekend here will be my parents' twentieth anniversary, and this was Mom's idea of how to spend their "second honeymoon," as she calls it. I think Mom goes to therapy because she loves to talk about "the twelve steps" and "full cycles" and "the big picture," like she's reading from some self-help manual. She probably thinks Dad's affairs are out of his system, and that we never knew about any of them. But kids aren't stupid. We see things. We hear things. We store them away like photographs in an album we never wanted to keep.
I think she'd like to have an affair of her own—she's jealous about Sissy's and Dad's freedom—but she's too afraid to step out, to make something happen. She's trapped by her own idea of who she's supposed to be. She's not happy. You can smell it on her like perfume gone rancid.
Most of the time she sits around the campsite reading Cosmopolitan, sometimes staring off into an ocean trance. Her eyes get glassy, like she's seeing something the rest of us can't—or maybe seeing nothing at all, which might be worse. She's got this way of disappearing while still being physically present, like a ghost haunting her own life.
At night she asks me to boil water on the Coleman stove, then dump it into a makeshift tub I constructed with a waterproof tarp and driftwood logs. The tub isn't very deep, but it does the trick. I even have to mix in cold water to cool it off because the metal gets so hot it could cook lobster. By mistake one evening I see my mother's breasts for the first time that I can remember—pale and soft in the lamplight, so different from the controlled, dressed version of her I'm used to. I'm embarrassed and wish I hadn't seen them, but the image burns itself into my brain like a brand. It makes her seem vulnerable in a way that scares me.
The worst part is sharing a tent with Sissy and Sam the Red Head. That's a major drag except I plan to sneak out and stay up late with Maggie when she comes ashore for the bonfires. Sissy won't tell if I don't tell about her and Sam doing their thing. Their thing involves a lot of heavy breathing and muffled sounds that make my skin crawl and my ears burn. It's like listening to something private and desperate, something that should happen behind thicker walls than canvas.
Sam the Red Head thinks he's God's gift to women. He's got these large freckles scattered across his shoulders like rust stains, and this way of talking that changes when he's around girls—gets all soft and syrupy like maple sap. He douses Sissy with compliments that just make me sick. "Your hair looks GREAT in a bun." "You're so much prettier than the girls at Cornell." Sissy eats it up, responds with these fake "You really think so?" replies that sound nothing like the sister I've known my whole life.
I call him Sam the Red Head because I know it pisses Sissy off. She's trying to make an impression on this "senior to be" while having just finished her freshman year. Sam wants to get inside my sister's pants—at least that's what my friend Derek told me, and Derek knows all about this kind of stuff. Even though Sissy and I fight most of the time, and I hate her materialistic tendencies, I don't want anyone barging into our family before being tested. Before proving they can handle the particular kind of dysfunction we specialize in.
The funny thing is, Mom found Sissy's birth control pills hidden underneath a floorboard in her bedroom. Sissy thought she had the best hiding spot in the world, but Mom's the best damn snoop alive. She's got senses like a bloodhound when it comes to finding things we don't want found. She also discovered a home pregnancy test crudely sewn into one of Sissy's old Raggedy Ann dolls, stuffed right into the head like some kind of contraband brain. Sissy claimed it was for her friend Angel, who did get noticeably bigger a few months later, so Mom let her off the hook. Thank God. Can you imagine a life of permanent grounding? That would really stink, especially when you'd have to put up with a sister like mine acting all lovesick and fake.
But Sissy's not that bad really. She's actually like my best friend when she's mad at Mom for snooping, which is most of the time. It's only when her boyfriends are around that she turns into this person I don't recognize.
Chapter 3: The Peculiar Science of Family
My mom and dad are quite a sight together. I don't know exactly what brought them together, because I've never seen the chemistry. They're like two elements that shouldn't mix but somehow created this unstable compound that's been slowly breaking down for years.
One time Dad was real mad at Mom and said that even though she'd supposedly given up smoking while pregnant with me, he'd found cigarette butts and empty packs tucked away with the Christmas ornaments while searching for his college yearbooks. He didn't bring this up very often—it was his best ammunition, his ace in the hole—so he kept it hidden in his grouch box until the time was right. Mom had no defense except tossing her spiked heel at his head and hoping for the best.
Recently though, she yelled something back about "Miss Pantyhose," and I think the world stopped because everything went completely quiet. Dad just said, "That isn't fair, we've agreed to put that behind us." I guess they both have their aces, and he doesn't bring up the smoking incident anymore.
This really doesn't bother me—Mom smoking and all. She gave it up long ago, and I think I've turned out okay just the same. But I wonder if Dad thinks there's something wrong with me anyway. Maybe that's why he called me peculiar. Maybe he thinks the smoking damaged something in my brain, made me different from other kids. The truth is, I do think differently than most people. I see patterns where others see chaos. I notice things that seem invisible to everyone else. I give everything names that make sense to me, even if they sound strange to others.
Now I can't tell if my parents are going through a breakup or a reconciliation. To me there seems to be a very fine line between giving it all up and walking away, and rededicating yourself to making it work. I don't think it will ever truly work between them, but I wish they'd be happy, together or apart. Their bickering is like background noise now, this constant low-level tension that makes everyone's nerves raw.
Most of the time they never say a whole hell of a lot to each other, but you'd think they'd had a full conversation. Their thought patterns must travel through the air, clog up the airwaves, tire me out. After sitting around a few minutes with them, I feel like everything has been discussed when really nothing has been said at all. It's like watching two people speak in code, and I'm the only one who doesn't have the decoder ring.
Chapter 4: The Bonfire
In the distance, around the bend of the island, I can see an orange light bouncing just above the horizon like a signal fire from another world. I hear distorted voices carried on the night wind—laughter and music and the clink of bottles against rocks. They've come once again, so I decide to walk counterclockwise along the rocky coast, back toward the dock where I caught twenty-one crabs earlier but let eight of them go. The bigger crabs, I figured, were smarter because they were harder to catch. The smaller ones didn't know any better, and I was sure to get a handful of them.
The larger crabs I helped prepare, along with Sissy and Sam the Red Head, for dinner. We steamed them on a thin sheet of rock placed over a driftwood fire, piled damp seaweed on top so it insulated the crabs, protecting their shells from cracking under the intense heat. The seaweed smelled like the ocean's basement, all salt and decay and hidden life.
Mom and Dad each had a two-pound lobster, steamed alive. I watched those lobsters wheeze and bubble until their greenish-maroon shells turned almost pink, like they were blushing from embarrassment. They dipped their lobster tails in real butter, wore lobster aprons that made them look like children playing dress-up, and sipped white wine from what Mom calls her "sexy wine glasses." Everything about the way they eat feels like a performance, like they're trying to convince themselves they're still the same people who honeymooned here eighteen years ago.
I like walking along the rocky coast at night. Jumping from rock to rock in the moonlight, watching my shadow dance on the water. The rocks are warm under my feet from absorbing sunlight all day, and they crack and pop when the cold night air hits them. It sounds like the island is talking to itself, carrying on conversations in a language made of temperature and time.
At the bonfire I look out at the huge pile of driftwood ablaze against the darkness. The flames reach up toward the stars like desperate fingers, throwing sparks into the night that die before they reach the water. The ranger—Mac Herron—allows fires on the rocky beach because high tide will put them out, and because he understands that people need fire the way they need air. Something about gathering around flames connects us to something ancient and necessary.
Mac's been the ranger here for fifteen years, shows up every day in the same khaki pants, emerald shirt, silver badge, and ranger hat. He's got this quiet demeanor that makes you want to tell him things, like he's carrying secrets for half the people who visit this island. I learned later that he died at sea a few years after this summer, but that night he was just a reassuring presence moving through the crowd, making sure everyone stayed safe.
All are welcome on the island as long as you pick up your garbage after staying. Some come to camp for weeks, living without running water or electricity like voluntary castaways. Others come for day hikes through the different sections of the island—the pine woods, the mud flats, the rocky peaks where you can see the mainland wavering like a mirage. And others just anchor their boats offshore and paddle in for cookouts, like tourists visiting their own wildness.
That's what the college kids do. This summer, more than any I can remember, college kids have discovered the island like it's some kind of promised land. Group after group throughout the week comes for bonfires and feasts. They must all be connected somehow because there's this trend of throwing a torch wrapped with bark into the water. They all do this, like some primitive ritual passed down through fraternity bloodlines.
Tonight when I arrive, there are more college kids than usual—close to twenty of them. The air tastes of salt and smoke and beer and something else, something electric that makes my skin prickle with anticipation. Their voices get louder as the night wears on, their movements looser, their inhibitions dissolving like sugar in rain. I watch as they pass bottles and cans, their faces glowing in the firelight like masks.
This certainly beats hanging around my own family camp, where conversations die in mid-sentence and everyone stares at their food like it might reveal the secrets of the universe.
One of the college kids tries jumping the bonfire. A tall kid with sandy hair and the kind of confidence that comes from never really failing at anything important. Everyone tells him not to, especially the women, but their protests only spur him on more. It's like watching a train wreck in slow motion—you know it's going to end badly, but you can't look away.
After convincing himself this feat is possible, he lands smack in the middle of the flames. His clothes catch fire, and for a moment he's a human torch dancing against the night sky. His buddies toss him into the ocean, and they all have a good laugh about it afterward, except for the jumper, who ends up with singed eyebrows and second-degree burns on his hands that will leave scars for the rest of his life.
"That's my ex," Maggie explains later, and there's something in her voice when she says it—not concern exactly, but not indifference either. Something complicated, like she's watching the physical manifestation of why their relationship ended.
That's when I really notice Maggie for the first time. Maggie Dionne. She's different from the others, carries herself with this quiet confidence that doesn't need to prove anything. She's watching the flames dance, and the light catches her face in a way that makes something twist in my chest, like a muscle I didn't know I had suddenly coming to life.
I've never thought much about girls until now, but watching Maggie, I understand why people write songs about this feeling. Why they do stupid things and make promises they can't keep and stare at the ceiling at three in the morning thinking about another person's breathing.
The night air carries the sound of their laughter across the water, mixing with the eternal rhythm of waves against rocks. Somewhere in the distance, a loon calls out its lonely song, and I think about how some sounds are so beautiful they make you ache inside, make you feel both full and empty at the same time.
Chapter 5: The Education of Oak Shipley
"Women like to be listened to," Maggie tells me the next night as we sit on the rocks away from her crowd. She pronounces "women" with this slight emphasis, like it's a word she's still getting used to saying about herself. "They like to feel heard, not just answered."
We're sharing a bag of marshmallows she brought from the yacht, roasting them over a small driftwood fire I built between two large rocks. The marshmallows turn into golden taffy in the heat, stretching long and sweet. Everything tastes better when you're hungry from island life, when you've been working all day catching crabs and hauling water and building lean-tos like some kind of voluntary castaway.
"What else?" I ask, because I genuinely want to know. My friend Derek thinks he knows everything about girls, but Derek's never actually been with one. He just talks like he has, the way some people talk about foreign countries they've only seen in magazines.
"They like to feel safe," Maggie continues, poking at the fire with a stick. Sparks dance up into the darkness like tiny prayers. "Not just physically safe, but emotionally safe. Like they can say anything and not be judged for it."
I think about Mom sitting in her ocean trance, staring at nothing with those glassy eyes. I don't think she feels safe at all. I think she feels trapped, like a crab in one of my traps with no way out except through something that might kill her.
"They also like surprises," Maggie adds. "Little ones. Like finding a perfect shell or having someone remember something they said three days ago."
I've been storing pictures in my mind all summer—Maggie's laugh, the way she moves her hands when she talks like she's conducting an invisible orchestra, how she tilts her head when she's thinking about something serious. I guess that's what she means about remembering. The brain stores things whether you want it to or not, files them away in places you didn't know existed until something triggers them loose.
"And they like to be touched," she says, and something in her voice changes, gets softer and more serious. "But only when they want to be touched. That's the most important thing. Consent isn't just saying yes—it's wanting to say yes."
She's sitting close enough that I can smell her skin—salt and sunscreen and something underneath that's just her, something I would recognize in complete darkness. My body wants to grow now. I can feel it happening, see it changing in ways that embarrass and excite me. I'm holding it back as best I can, but there are too many new hairs appearing, too many new sensations and urges I don't know what to do with. I only like the hair on my head. I want to hold on a little bit longer to being a kid, but sitting here with Maggie, I understand why growing up might not be entirely terrible.
"Your family seems complicated," she says, and it's not really a question.
"Yours isn't?"
She laughs, but it's not a happy sound. It's the laugh people make when the alternative is crying. "My parents got divorced when I was twelve. Now they compete over who can make me happier during their custody weekends. It's exhausting being someone's prize."
"At least they're trying."
"Sometimes trying too hard is worse than not trying at all. At least when people don't try, you know where you stand."
I think about Dad flying in on weekends like some kind of medical superhero, bringing lobsters and wine and acting like he's so happy to see Mom. The word "acting" being key. Everything about my parents feels like a performance lately, like they're following a script for a play neither of them wants to be in but can't figure out how to leave.
"I used to think that crabbing off the dock was the best thing in life," I tell Maggie. "Or sitting in the tall yellowed grass behind Sea Shell Beach, watching clouds form and disfigure. But now I don't know for sure."
She looks at me with those eyes that seem older than her nineteen years, and I feel like she's seeing something in me that I don't even know is there yet. Something that might be worth developing, worth becoming.
"Growing up is weird," she says. "You spend your whole childhood wanting to be older, and then when it starts happening, you want to slow it down. Like you suddenly realize that childhood wasn't preparation for life—it was life, and you traded it away for something you're not sure you actually want."
That's exactly it. That's exactly what I couldn't put into words before. The feeling that I'm standing at the edge of something vast and unknown, and once I step over the line, there's no going back to the safety of not knowing.
When we walk back toward our respective camps later, Maggie takes my hand. Just reaches over and intertwines our fingers like it's the most natural thing in the world. Her palm is warm and slightly rough from sailing rope, with these small calluses that speak of competence and strength. I've never held a girl's hand before, and I'm surprised by how right it feels, like my hand was designed specifically to fit with hers.
But underneath the good feeling is something else—a cold sensation in my midsection that I can't quite name. It's both bad and good at the same time, like being afraid and excited and guilty all rolled into one confusing emotion. Like I'm crossing some invisible boundary I can't uncross, entering territory that will change me in ways I might not be ready for.
I think about my parents and their telepathic non-conversations, about Sissy and Sam's desperate heavy breathing in the tent, about all the ways love seems to twist people into shapes they don't recognize. Part of me wants to run back to childhood, where the biggest worry was whether I caught enough crabs for dinner and whether Dad would let me stay up late to watch the stars.
But another part of me, a part that's growing stronger every day, wants to see where this path with Maggie leads. Even if it scares me. Even if it changes everything. Even if it means leaving behind the safety of being someone's child and becoming someone who might break another person's heart, or have his own heart broken in return.
The night wraps around us like a promise and a threat, and I can hear the future calling from somewhere beyond the horizon, patient and inevitable as the tide.

I want to protest, to argue that they could still work things out if they just tried harder, if they went to counseling, if they remembered why they fell in love in the first place. But sitting here in the wreckage of their final argument, I realize that what I'm feeling isn't surprise—it's relief. The tension that's been building in our family for years has finally been released, and the truth is simpler and cleaner than the elaborate fiction we've been maintaining.

They're not angry anymore, just tired. Tired of pretending, tired of fighting, tired of trying to love each other in ways that don't come naturally to either of them. They sound like people who've been carrying a heavy load for a long time and have finally gotten permission to set it down.
Around three-thirty in the morning, the wind suddenly stops.

But as we walk, I realize something unexpected: I'm not afraid. Not of the storm, not of the dangerous hike, not even of my parents' divorce. For the first time in years, the adults in my life are being honest about what's really happening, and somehow that honesty makes everything else feel manageable.
The cabin, when we finally reach it, is exactly what Dad promised—a solid structure built to withstand whatever nature can throw at it. In the morning, when Hurricane Alexis has finally exhausted itself and moved on to terrorize some other part of the coast, we emerge from the cabin to survey what's left of the world we thought we knew. The island looks like it's been redesigned by someone with a completely different aesthetic vision—trees rearranged, shorelines redrawn, familiar landmarks erased or relocated according to principles that have nothing to do with human convenience or sentiment.
Our campsite is gone. Not damaged—gone, as if it never existed. The tent platforms have been swept clean, the fire ring filled with sand and debris, the dock reduced to a few splintered pilings sticking up from the water like the ribs of some ancient sea creature.
But we're alive, all four of us, and our family's most important conversation has finally happened. The terrible honesty of hurricane night has created a new baseline for all our interactions. We're still polite to each other, still loving in our complicated ways, but nobody is pretending anymore that our problems don't exist or that they can be solved through good behavior and careful emotional management.
"The Coast Guard will be looking for survivors today," Dad says, surveying the transformed landscape. "We should build a signal fire on the highest point of the island."
It's the first practical suggestion he's made since the storm that doesn't involve trying to control outcomes beyond his influence. We work together to gather dry wood and create a column of smoke that can be seen from miles away, a beacon that says we're here, we survived, we're ready to be rescued from whatever experiment in family dynamics this week has turned out to be.
The helicopter finds us around noon, its crew efficient and professional and completely uninterested in the emotional archaeology that's been happening alongside our physical survival. They load us into the aircraft without ceremony, treating us like any other family that made questionable decisions about hurricane preparedness and got lucky enough to survive the consequences.
From the air, Doughnut Island looks like a different place entirely—scarred by the storm, stripped of its familiar landmarks, reduced to its essential geological structure. Horse Pond is still there, still fresh water in the middle of salt, but the channel that briefly connected it to the ocean has been filled in again, leaving the pond isolated and separate as it was before the storm forced everything to change.
But it's still recognizably itself. Still the same basic shape, still surrounded by the same ocean, still home to whatever wildlife managed to survive nature's violent redecoration. It will recover, probably within a few years, and new visitors will come to enjoy its beaches and forests without knowing about the family that once fell apart and put itself back together in a different configuration on its shores.
Families are like that too, I think. They weather disasters, adapt to new circumstances, rebuild themselves in forms that work better than what came before. The shape changes, but the essential structure remains.
Chapter 10: Rockland
We spend four days in a motel in Rockland while my parents meet with lawyers and make arrangements for our return to Boston. The Anchor Inn isn't particularly charming—thin walls, scratchy bedspreads, the smell of industrial cleaning products trying to mask decades of other people's temporary lives—but it represents civilization after a week of camping, hot showers and television and food that doesn't require catching or gathering.
Sissy and I spend our time exploring the small coastal town, eating ice cream that tastes like childhood, walking along the harbor where working boats bring in their daily catch of lobster and fish and the dreams of men who make their living from the sea. We talk about what our lives will look like when we get home, how different everything will be now that our parents have finally acknowledged what we've all known for years.
"Are you sad?" she asks me one afternoon as we sit on a bench overlooking Rockland Harbor, watching the boats navigate between the moorings with the casual expertise that comes from years of practice.
"About the divorce? Not really. Are you?"
"I thought I would be. But mostly I feel relieved. Like we can finally stop pretending everything is fine when it obviously isn't."
"Do you think they'll be happier apart?"
"I think Mom will be. She's already different—more like herself, if that makes sense."
It does make sense. Even in the four days since the hurricane, Mom has seemed more present, more engaged with the world around her. The desperate hunger that characterized her behavior during the bonfire incident has been replaced by something calmer, more purposeful. She's making plans instead of just reacting to whatever crisis presents itself.
"What about Dad?"
"Dad will be fine. He's got his work, and work has always been more important to him than family anyway."
This sounds harsh, but I know Sissy doesn't mean it cruelly. It's just an honest assessment of Dad's priorities, the kind of truth we can finally speak aloud now that everyone has stopped pretending about the fundamental dynamics of our family system.
"Will you miss him? When we're living with Mom full-time?"
"I'll miss the idea of him. The father I always wanted him to be. But the actual father we had? The one who was never around, never really present even when he was physically there? I won't miss that very much."
I understand what she means. The Dad who took me crabbing and taught me to skip stones was always a weekend visitor in our house, someone who showed up occasionally between hospital emergencies and research projects. Maybe now that he doesn't have to pretend to be a full-time father, he can be better at being a part-time one.
On our last night in Rockland, Mom takes me for a walk along the waterfront while Dad and Sissy pack our belongings for the drive home. The evening air smells of salt and fried clams and that particular mixture of diesel fuel and seaweed that characterizes working harbors everywhere.
"How are you doing with all this?" she asks.
"Okay, I think. Better than I expected."
"Good. I was worried that you'd blame yourself somehow. You have a tendency to take responsibility for things that aren't your fault."
"Maggie told me the same thing."
"She sounds like a smart girl. I'm sorry I didn't get to know her better under different circumstances."
We walk in comfortable silence for a few minutes, listening to the sound of rigging clanging against masts in the marina. The boats rock gently in their slips, each one representing someone's dream of escape or adventure or simply a different way of moving through the world.
"Mom," I say finally, "are you going to be okay? On your own, I mean?"
"I think so. For the first time in years, I'm looking forward to finding out who I am when I'm not trying to be what someone else needs me to be."
"What if you don't like who you are?"
"Then I'll work on becoming someone I do like. The wonderful thing about being alive is that you can always change, always grow, always become more than you were before."
This is the most hopeful thing I've heard her say in years, and it makes me realize how much energy she's been spending on just surviving her unhappiness instead of addressing it directly.
"Will you go back to school?" I ask. Mom had been working on a master's degree in library science when she got pregnant with Sissy, but she never finished it.
"Maybe. I've been thinking about it. Or maybe I'll do something completely different. I have options now that I didn't have before."
"What kind of options?"
"Well, I don't have to worry about what your father thinks of my choices anymore. I don't have to plan my life around his schedule, his needs, his idea of what a doctor's wife should be like."
There's no bitterness in her voice when she says this, just a kind of wonder, as if she's discovering that doors she thought were permanently closed have been standing open all along.
Chapter 11: Boston and New Beginnings
We drive back to Boston on a Thursday afternoon in late August, the four of us packed into Dad's station wagon with our salvaged belongings and a cooler full of lobsters that Mom insisted on buying from a dock-side vendor in Rockland.
"If we're going to have our last family dinner," she announced, "it should be something special."
The drive takes four hours through New England countryside that's just beginning to show the first hints of autumn color. For the first time in years, it's not uncomfortable to be trapped in a car with all of them. The usual tension is gone, replaced by something that feels almost like friendship—the relaxed camaraderie of people who've survived a crisis together and discovered they're stronger than they thought.
We talk about practical things—what needs to be done to the house, how the school year will work, when Dad will move into his new apartment—but underneath the logistics, there's a sense of relief that's almost celebratory. We're celebrating honesty, courage, the end of a long period of unhappiness that everybody was too polite or too scared to acknowledge.
Our house in Newton looks smaller than I remembered, as if the island has changed my sense of scale along with everything else. Dad carries our bags inside while Mom starts water boiling for the lobsters, and for a few minutes it's possible to pretend we're just a normal family returning from a normal vacation.
But normal isn't what we are anymore, and pretending isn't something we do. Within an hour, Dad is on the phone with the hospital, catching up on a week's worth of medical emergencies, while Mom sorts through the mail that accumulated in our absence, making two piles—one for her, one for him—in preparation for lives that will soon be lived separately.
"Sissy!" Mom calls from the kitchen. "Come help with dinner!"
We gather around the dining room table for what Mom has declared our "last family dinner," though it doesn't feel like an ending so much as a transition into something different. The lobsters are perfect—sweet and tender, served with drawn butter and fresh corn that Mom bought at a farmstand on the drive home.
"This is good," Sissy announces, holding up a perfectly extracted piece of tail meat. "We should have lobster more often."
"When you're grown up with your own house, you can have lobster whenever you want," Mom tells her.
"Will you have lobster whenever you want?" I ask Mom.
"I might. I might develop expensive tastes now that I don't have to worry about setting a good example of frugal household management."
Dad looks up from his lobster with a slight smile. "I never asked you to be frugal."
"You didn't have to ask. It was part of the job description for doctor's wife—be supportive, be economical, be invisible when necessary."
"You were never invisible," Dad protests.
"Not to you, maybe. But to myself? I disappeared so gradually I didn't even notice it was happening."
This is the kind of conversation that would have ended in argument a week ago, but tonight it's just observation, honesty without accusation. Dad nods like he understands what she means, even if he doesn't necessarily agree with her interpretation.
After dinner, we clean up together, washing the good china by hand and putting it back in the cabinet where it will wait for the next special occasion. It's the most relaxed family time we've had in months, which is ironic considering we just spent the evening discussing the end of our parents' marriage.
Chapter 12: High School and Learning to Be Myself
The school year begins with unseasonably warm weather that makes everyone restless and eager for the kind of autumn that exists more in calendar dates than actual temperature. I'm assigned to mostly honors classes at Newton North, surrounded by other kids who are academically focused but socially awkward in various interesting ways.
It's a good fit. My particular brand of awkwardness—the intense observation, the pattern recognition, the tendency to notice things that most people ignore—blends in with their different but equally non-standard approaches to navigating teenage social dynamics. Nobody seems particularly interested in analyzing anyone else's family situation, which comes as a relief since explaining divorce and family dysfunction was not high on my list of conversation topics for making new friends.
Mom drives me to school on the first day, which is a change from previous years when Dad dropped me off on his way to the hospital. Her car smells different—less like antiseptic and medical urgency, more like the floral perfume she's started wearing and the coffee she drinks from a travel mug with "World's Okayest Mom" printed on the side.
"Are you nervous?" she asks as we pull into the student parking lot.
"A little. But good nervous, not bad nervous."
"What's the difference?"
"Good nervous means you're excited about what might happen. Bad nervous means you're afraid of what might happen."
She nods like this distinction makes perfect sense to her. "I'm good nervous about today too. I have a job interview this afternoon."
"Really? Where?"
"The Newton Public Library. They need someone part-time to help with children's programs and collection development. It would let me finish my master's degree while working."
This is the first I've heard about Mom looking for work, but it makes sense. She needs something to fill the hours that used to be occupied by managing Dad's schedule and maintaining their social obligations as a medical couple.
The day passes in the usual blur of first-day logistics—new locker combinations, unfamiliar classrooms, teachers explaining their expectations and grading policies with the weary enthusiasm of people who've given the same speech a hundred times before. But there's something different about how I move through it all, a confidence that wasn't there in previous years.
Maybe it's because I've learned over the summer that I can survive difficult situations without falling apart. Maybe it's because I no longer feel responsible for managing other people's emotions along with my own. Or maybe it's just that I've finally accepted that being different isn't necessarily being wrong—sometimes it's just being more honest about what everyone else is pretending not to notice.
At lunch, I sit with a group of kids from my AP Biology class, and for the first time in my academic career, I don't feel like I have to hide how much I know or pretend to be less interested in the subject matter than I actually am. When the conversation turns to genetics and inheritance patterns, I find myself explaining concepts that I learned from reading Dad's medical journals, and instead of rolling their eyes or changing the subject, my classmates actually seem interested.
"How do you know all this stuff?" asks a girl named Sarah who's planning to major in pre-med.
"My dad's a cancer researcher. I've been reading his journals since I was twelve."
"That's so cool. My parents think science is boring."
It occurs to me that this might be the first time anyone has ever described something about my family as "cool," and it feels surprisingly good. Maybe having a dad who's more interested in cellular biology than family dynamics isn't entirely a disadvantage. Maybe growing up in a household where intellectual curiosity was valued, even if emotional intelligence was sometimes lacking, gave me tools that other kids don't have.
Chapter 13: Weekends with Dad
Dad's new apartment is a two-bedroom unit in a modern building in Cambridge that smells like fresh paint and the dreams of people starting over. Everything is beige and practical, furnished with items that prioritize function over comfort or personality. It looks like a place where someone lives temporarily while waiting for real life to begin.
"What do you think?" he asks as he shows us around during our first weekend visit.
"It's nice," Sissy says diplomatically. "Very... clean."
"It's temporary," Dad explains, though he doesn't say what it's temporary until. "Once I figure out what I actually need, I'll look for something more permanent."
My room has a desk, a bed, and a dresser, all in the same beige color scheme as the rest of the apartment. There's one window that looks out onto a parking lot, and the walls are completely bare except for the standard-issue prints that came with the furnished unit.
The truth is, this doesn't feel like home and probably never will. It feels like a hotel room where I'm staying temporarily, a place that exists solely for the purpose of fulfilling visitation requirements. There's nothing wrong with it, but there's nothing particularly right about it either.
But something interesting happens during these weekend visits. Without the pressure of trying to be a full-time husband and father, Dad becomes more relaxed, more like the person I imagine he might have been before adult responsibilities turned him into a walking medical textbook. He actually listens when I talk about school, asks questions about my friends and interests that go beyond just checking boxes on some mental list of parental obligations.
"Are you happy here?" I ask him during our third weekend visit, while Sissy is in the bathroom getting ready for dinner.
"Happy is probably the wrong word. But I'm comfortable, and comfortable is better than the alternative."
"What's the alternative?"
"Staying in a situation that wasn't working and making everyone miserable, including myself."
This is more emotional honesty than Dad usually offers, and I realize that living alone has forced him to develop a vocabulary for feelings that he never needed when Mom was managing all the interpersonal aspects of their shared life.
"Do you miss us? During the week, I mean?"
"I miss you kids. I don't miss the constant feeling that I was failing at being the husband and father everyone needed me to be."
"Were you failing?"
He considers this question seriously, which is more than he would have done six months ago when his standard response to personal questions was to change the subject to something more professionally comfortable.
"I was trying to be someone I'm not," he says finally. "Someone who could balance a demanding career with the kind of emotional availability that healthy relationships require. It turns out I'm better at one of those things than the other."
"Which one?"
"The career part. The medicine. The research. The problems that can be solved through knowledge and careful analysis."
"What about the other part? The relationship part?"
"I'm still learning. Living alone has taught me things about myself that I didn't know when I was married. Maybe someday I'll meet someone who wants the same kind of life I want, someone who understands that I show love through actions rather than words."
This gives me hope for him in a way I didn't expect. The idea that he's not just giving up on human connection, but trying to understand how to do it better, more honestly, in ways that don't require him to pretend to be someone he's not.
Chapter 14: Therapy and the Architecture of Understanding
The suggestion comes from Maggie, who calls on a Thursday evening in October, catching me completely off guard because I assumed our summer friendship was just that—a temporary connection limited to the time and place where we met.
"Oak! I got your number from directory assistance. I hope you don't mind me calling."
"Of course not. How are you? How's college?"
"Good. Busy. I'm taking a psychology class about family systems, and it's making me think about our conversations on the island."
"What kind of thinking?"
"About how families develop patterns that seem normal to the people inside them but are actually pretty dysfunctional. And about how kids often end up feeling responsible for fixing problems that aren't theirs to fix."
This description fits my family so perfectly that it makes me uncomfortable, like she's been studying us as a case study in family dysfunction. "Are you analyzing us for a class project or something?"
"No, nothing like that. I'm just... I learned some things that I thought might be helpful for you to know."
"Like what?"
"Like the fact that there are therapists who specialize in working with teenagers whose families are going through major transitions. People who can help you figure out which feelings are yours and which ones belong to other people."
The idea of therapy makes me immediately defensive, though I'm not sure why. "I'm not crazy."
"Neither am I, but I started seeing a counselor last month to help me deal with some stuff about my parents' divorce. It's not about being crazy—it's about having someone trained to help you sort through complicated emotions."
"What kind of stuff?"
"Like why I felt responsible for their happiness when I was twelve. Like why I keep dating guys who need me to fix them. Like how to have relationships with people without trying to manage their problems for them."
These issues sound familiar in ways that make my chest feel tight with recognition. After we hang up, I think about this conversation for days. The idea that there's a difference between what I actually feel and what I think I'm supposed to feel is revolutionary to me. I've spent so much energy trying to have the right emotions about my parents' divorce, about Dad's weekend visits, about Mom's new job and new independence, that I haven't stopped to consider what my authentic feelings might be.
I mention the conversation to Mom while we're making dinner together on Sunday evening. She's preparing a recipe she found in a cookbook she bought specifically because Dad always said he didn't like "experimental cooking"—some kind of Thai curry that fills the kitchen with unfamiliar spices and makes the whole house smell like adventure and possibility.
"Maggie thinks I should talk to someone about the divorce," I tell her.
"What kind of someone?"
"A therapist. Someone who works with teenagers whose families are changing."
Mom stops stirring the curry and looks at me seriously. "How do you feel about that idea?"
"Scared. But also maybe interested."
"What scares you about it?"
"What if they tell me there's something wrong with me? What if they want to put me on medication or diagnose me with something that makes me feel broken?"
"What if they help you understand yourself better? What if they give you tools for handling difficult emotions in healthier ways?"
"Is that what therapy is supposed to do?"
"I think therapy is supposed to help you figure out what you need in order to be healthy and happy. Different people need different things."
"Have you ever been to therapy?"
"I'm starting next week, actually. I want to understand why I stayed in an unhappy marriage for so long, and what I can do differently in future relationships."
This is news to me, but it fits with Mom's general project of figuring out who she is when she's not trying to be what someone else needs. Adding therapy to the list seems consistent with her approach to rebuilding her life from the ground up.
Two weeks later, I'm sitting in a comfortable office that looks more like someone's living room than a medical facility, talking to Dr. Sarah Chen about why I think I might benefit from therapy.
"Tell me about your family," she says, which seems like an impossibly broad question.
"Which part? The part where my parents got divorced last summer, or the part where I've always felt responsible for keeping everyone happy?"
"Let's start with the second part. What does feeling responsible for everyone's happiness look like in your daily life?"
I describe the patterns I've noticed in myself—the way I monitor other people's moods, the way I try to prevent conflicts before they start, the way I feel guilty when family members are upset even when their emotions have nothing to do with me.
"When did you first notice yourself doing these things?"
"I don't know. It feels like I've always done them."
"Can you think of a specific example from when you were younger?"
I tell her about a time when I was eight or nine, when Mom and Dad had a fight about money while we were on vacation. I spent the rest of the trip trying to be the perfect child—helpful, cheerful, no complaints about anything—because I thought that if I was good enough, they would stop being angry with each other.
"Did it work?"
"For that trip, maybe. But the underlying problem didn't go away just because I was well-behaved."
"What did you learn from that experience?"
"That I could influence other people's moods by managing my own behavior."
"And what did you conclude from that learning?"
"That it was my job to manage other people's emotions."
Dr. Chen nods like this progression makes perfect sense to her. "That's a very common pattern for children who grow up in families with a lot of tension. They become hypervigilant about emotional atmospheres and develop strategies for managing other people's feelings."
"Is that bad?"
"It's not bad or good—it's adaptive. You developed skills that helped you survive in a difficult situation. But now that the situation has changed, you might want to evaluate whether those skills are still serving you or whether they're preventing you from developing in ways that would be healthier for you."
This is a completely different way of thinking about my behavior than anything I've considered before. Instead of seeing my hypervigilance and emotional management as character flaws or signs of dysfunction, Dr. Chen is describing them as survival skills that made sense in context but might need updating as circumstances change.
"What would healthy development look like?" I ask.
"Learning to take care of your own emotional needs without trying to control other people's emotional responses. Learning to set boundaries between your feelings and other people's feelings. Learning to ask for what you need instead of trying to earn it by being perfect."
The sessions continue weekly throughout the fall, and gradually I begin to understand the difference between caring about people and taking responsibility for their choices. I learn that my parents' divorce isn't a failure on my part, but a success in their recognition that they deserve to be happy even if that means being apart.
I learn that my unusual way of seeing patterns and connections isn't a disorder to be corrected, but a strength to be developed. Dr. Chen explains that many people with autism spectrum traits—she never uses the word "disorder"—become excellent scientists, artists, researchers, and therapists because their brains process information differently than neurotypical minds.
"You notice things other people miss," she tells me during one session. "You see systems and patterns and connections that aren't obvious to most people. That's not something to be ashamed of—that's something to be cultivated."
"But it makes me feel different from other kids."
"You are different from other kids. The question is whether you're going to see that difference as a problem to be solved or as a gift to be developed."
Slowly, over months of conversations, I begin to see myself not as the family member who failed to keep everyone together, but as someone who learned to read emotional atmospheres with unusual accuracy—a skill that might serve me well in whatever career I eventually choose.
Chapter 15: Spring and New Growth
By spring, our new family configuration has settled into rhythms that feel sustainable rather than temporary. Mom has finished her master's degree and gotten a full-time position at the Newton Public Library, where she develops children's programming and manages collection development with the kind of enthusiasm she never showed for maintaining our household social calendar.
Dad has moved into a larger apartment closer to the hospital, one that actually reflects his personality rather than just his need for functional furniture. He's started dating someone—another doctor, a radiologist named Dr. Martinez who shares his love of classical music and molecular biology. They seem genuinely compatible in ways that he and Mom never were, both of them content with quiet evenings and conversations about work that they find mutually fascinating.
Sissy has broken up with Sam the Red Head and is dating a sophomore from MIT who treats her intelligence as an asset rather than a threat to his masculinity. She's declared a major in environmental engineering and talks about graduate school with the kind of focus that suggests she's finally figured out who she wants to become when she grows up.
And me? I'm learning to be myself without apology or excessive explanation. My grades are good, I've made friends who appreciate rather than tolerate my particular way of seeing the world, and I've stopped feeling responsible for managing everyone else's emotional weather.
The anniversary of our last summer on Doughnut Island passes without ceremony, but I find myself thinking about the lessons I learned there. About the difference between holding on and letting go. About the courage it takes to tell the truth when the truth changes everything. About the way natural systems adapt to disruption by finding new forms of stability.
In therapy, Dr. Chen and I have been exploring what I want to do with my life beyond just surviving my family's transition. I'm drawn to marine biology, to the study of complex ecosystems and the ways organisms adapt to changing environments. There's something appealing about spending my career studying the kind of resilience and adaptation that I've watched my own family demonstrate over the past year.
"You know," Dr. Chen says during one of our sessions, "the skills you developed for managing family dynamics—reading emotional cues, understanding system interactions, seeing patterns that others miss—those would serve you well in any field that involves understanding complex relationships."
"You mean like family therapy?"
"Maybe. Or research psychology. Or marine ecology. Or any number of fields where success depends on understanding how different elements of a system interact with each other."
The idea that my unusual brain might be an asset rather than a liability is still revolutionary to me, but I'm beginning to believe it might be true. Maybe being different means having something valuable to offer rather than having something wrong that needs to be fixed.
Epilogue: Ten Years Later
I'm twenty-four now, finishing my graduate degree in marine biology at Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution. My thesis research focuses on the way island ecosystems adapt to environmental disruption—storms, rising sea levels, invasive species, climate change. It's work that requires exactly the kind of pattern recognition and systems thinking that used to make me feel like an outsider in high school.
Last summer, I returned to Doughnut Island for the first time since Hurricane Alexis. The island has recovered completely, looking almost exactly as it did during our family vacations, but I see it differently now. I understand the ecological relationships that create its apparent stability,Mom and Dad exchange a look that contains twenty years of shared history—love and disappointment and accumulated resentments and probably some things I'm too young to understand. It's the look of two people who once thought they wanted the same things from life and have spent two decades discovering how wrong they were about that fundamental assumption.
"What's happening," Dad says slowly, "is that your mother and I have realized we're not the same people we were when we got married. We want different things now, need different things, and we're not sure we can give each other what we need while staying married."
"So you're getting divorced," Sissy says. It's not a question.
"We're considering it," Mom answers. "We're going to spend the rest of this vacation talking about what that would mean, what it would look like, how to do it in a way that's best for everyone involved."
The rain continues to drum against the canvas walls of our shelter, creating a rhythm that sounds like time passing, like moments accumulating into hours and hours into the kind of day that changes everything permanently. I think about all the mornings I've woken up in this tent, all the family breakfasts we've shared, all the conversations we've had about nothing important while avoiding the conversations that might actually matter.
"Will we still be a family?" I ask, voicing the fear that's been growing in my chest since last night.
"Yes," Mom says immediately. "Just a different kind of family. One that's honest instead of polite, real instead of perfect."
"What does that mean, exactly?"
"It means your father and I will probably live in different houses, but we'll still both be your parents. It means holidays and birthdays might look different, but they'll still happen. It means we'll have to learn new ways of being related to each other, but we'll figure it out as we go."
This sounds reasonable in theory, but I can't help thinking about all the divorced families I know from school, how complicated their lives seem with custody schedules and step-parents and the constant negotiation of which parent gets which holidays. It might be better than what we have now, but it's definitely going to be more complicated.
"Are you sad?" I ask.
Both parents consider this question seriously, like they haven't actually thought about their emotions in terms of simple categories like sad or happy.
"I'm sad that we couldn't make our marriage work," Dad says finally. "But I'm not sad about stopping trying to force something that isn't working."
"I'm sad about the time we wasted trying to be people we're not," Mom adds. "But I'm not sad about finally being honest with each other."
The conversation continues in this vein for another hour—practical questions about where everyone will live, how school will work, what happens with the house and the cars and all the accumulated stuff of twenty years of shared life. It's strange how divorce, which seems like this huge emotional catastrophe, actually involves a lot of mundane logistics about who gets which furniture and how to divide up kitchen appliances.
But underneath the practical discussion, I can hear something else: a kind of exhausted relief, like they're both tired of pretending to be happier than they actually are. They're not angry at each other anymore, just worn down by years of trying to love each other in ways that don't come naturally to either of them.
Around noon, the rain stops and the sun comes out, turning the wet landscape into something that sparkles and steams in the sudden heat. Dad suggests that we all take a walk around the island, get some fresh air, see how the storm changed the familiar geography.
The island looks different after the rain—cleaner somehow, like it's been washed and dried and reset to some original condition. The colors are more vivid, the air smells cleaner, and there's this sense of renewal that makes everything feel possible in ways it didn't yesterday.
We walk in a loose group, not really together but not separated either, each of us processing the morning's conversation in our own way. Dad points out changes in the shoreline where the rain has carved new channels in the sand. Mom collects shells that the storm washed up, examining each one before deciding whether it's worth keeping. Sissy and I mostly just walk, listening to our parents talk about geology and tides and the way natural systems respond to disruption.
It's the most relaxed family time we've had in months, which is ironic considering we just spent the morning discussing the end of our parents' marriage. But maybe that's what honesty does—it creates space for people to be themselves instead of whatever role they think they're supposed to play.
We're walking along the shore near Horse Pond when I notice something I've never seen before: a narrow channel connecting the freshwater pond to the ocean, carved by last night's rain and the high tide that followed. For the first time since we've been coming to this island, salt water and fresh water are mixing, creating something that's neither one nor the other but somehow both.
"Look at that," Dad says, following my gaze. "The storm created a connection that wasn't there before."
"Will it stay?" I ask.
"Probably not. The next high tide will likely wash sand back into the channel, and the pond will be separate again. But for now, they're part of the same system."
I think about this as we continue our walk—the idea that things can be temporarily connected, that barriers can be removed and reformed, that systems can change and adapt and find new ways of working even when their original structure gets disrupted.
Maybe families are like that too. Maybe divorce doesn't mean the end of all connection, just a different kind of connection that allows everyone to be themselves without having to fit into roles that don't actually suit them.
Chapter 7: Maggie and the Science of Growing Up
That afternoon, while Mom and Dad continue their careful negotiations about the future of their marriage, I find myself walking toward the part of the beach where the college kids have set up their temporary civilization. I tell myself I'm just exploring, but really I'm hoping to find Maggie and continue the conversation we started last night.
The rain has washed the beach clean, erasing yesterday's footprints and fire circles and leaving a blank slate that looks like it's waiting for new stories to be written in sand and charcoal and the temporary architecture of young people claiming space for themselves.
I find Maggie sitting alone on a piece of driftwood, reading a paperback book with a cover that shows a woman in a flowing dress standing on a cliff overlooking the ocean. She looks up when she hears my footsteps on the wet sand, and her smile suggests she's been hoping I would show up.
"How's your family doing today?" she asks, closing the book and giving me her full attention.
"Different. Better, I think, but definitely different."
"Different how?"
I tell her about the morning's conversation, about my parents' decision to consider divorce, about the strange relief that seems to have settled over our campsite now that everyone has stopped pretending everything is fine.
"That must be scary," she says when I finish.
"It is. But it's also... I don't know. Honest? For the first time in years, everyone is saying what they actually think instead of what they think they're supposed to think."
"Honesty can be scary and liberating at the same time."
We walk down to the water's edge, where the waves are smaller today, gentler, like the ocean is apologizing for last night's drama. The air smells of salt and seaweed and that particular clean scent that follows rain, like the world has been scrubbed and rinsed and hung out to dry.
"Can I ask you something?" I say.
"Sure."
"Last night you said it's not my job to keep my family together. But if it's not my job, then what is my job? What am I supposed to do while all this is happening?"
Maggie considers this question seriously, picking up a flat stone and testing its weight in her hand. "I think your job is to figure out who you are when you're not trying to manage other people's emotions. To learn what you actually like and want and need, instead of just responding to what everyone else needs from you."
"I don't know how to do that."
"Nobody does, at first. It's like learning a new language or a musical instrument—you have to practice before you get any good at it."
She skips the stone across the water—five skips before it sinks. I find another stone and try to match her technique, managing only three skips before mine disappears into the gray-green water.
"It's all in the wrist," she says, demonstrating the motion without actually throwing anything.
"Everything seems to be in the wrist. Dad says that about stone skipping too."
"Maybe your dad and I have that in common. What else does he say about things?"
I think about this. "He says trees that bend don't break. He says that being a doctor means making difficult decisions with incomplete information. He says that life is a series of problems to be solved rather than experiences to be enjoyed."
"That last one sounds kind of sad."
"Yeah. I think Dad sees life as work rather than adventure. Everything has to have a purpose, a goal, a measurable outcome."
"What about you? Do you see life as work or adventure?"
"I don't know yet. Maybe both? I like solving problems, figuring out how things work, understanding the patterns that most people don't notice. But I also like... this." I gesture toward the water, the sky, the general experience of being somewhere beautiful with someone who doesn't need me to be anything other than myself.
"This?"
"Talking to someone who doesn't expect me to fix their problems or manage their emotions or be more normal than I actually am."
Maggie nods like this makes perfect sense to her. "You know what I think?"
"What?"
"I think you're at that age where you get to start choosing who you want to become instead of just being whoever other people need you to be."
"That sounds terrifying."
"It is terrifying. But it's also exciting. You get to experiment, try different versions of yourself, see what fits and what doesn't."
A seagull lands on the sand near our feet, eyeing us hopefully for food we don't have. It has this patient, expectant expression that reminds me of the way adults look at teenagers—like they're waiting for us to do something interesting or embarrassing or both.
"Maggie," I say, "can I tell you something that might sound weird?"
"Everything interesting sounds weird at first."

Sissy's right, of course. Mom is an adult, technically speaking, and adults are supposed to be responsible for their own choices and their own consequences. But the adult in question is currently wearing high heels on a camping trip and bringing wine to a college party, which suggests that her decision-making capabilities might be temporarily compromised by whatever crisis she's working through.
"What if she does something really embarrassing?"
"Then she'll have to live with the embarrassment. And maybe that's what it takes for her to understand that some behavior has consequences that can't be undone."
This sounds like something a therapist might say, all reasonable and supportive of personal growth through natural consequences. But it's harder to apply that philosophy when the person making questionable choices is your mother and the consequences will affect your entire family's reputation on an island where everyone knows everyone else's business.
The afternoon stretches ahead like a held breath, full of possibilities that range from mildly uncomfortable to genuinely disastrous. Somewhere on this island, my mother is discovering whether the fantasy she's constructed about herself and her place in other people's lives bears any resemblance to reality.
And somewhere in Rockland, my father is pretending that work is more important than the family crisis he's avoiding, that cancer research justifies his absence during the exact moments when his presence might actually make a difference.
I check my watch and realize it's only two o'clock. We have hours left before this day is over, hours for everything to go wrong in ways I can't predict or prevent. All I can do is wait, and watch, and try to remember that sometimes the most loving thing you can do for someone is let them learn their own lessons, even when those lessons are painful for everyone who has to witness the learning process.
Chapter 5: The Bonfire and the First Glimpse of Truth
The sun sets over Penobscot Bay like a dying ember, painting the water in shades of orange and crimson that would be beautiful if I wasn't so worried about what Mom might be doing at the college campsite. The evening air carries the smell of wood smoke from their bonfire, mixed with the sounds of laughter and music and the particular kind of chaos that happens when young people gather around fire and alcohol and the freedom of being away from anyone who might judge their choices.

The bonfire is bigger than usual tonight, built from driftwood that's been collecting on the beach all summer and dried by weeks of sun and salt air. The flames reach up toward the darkening sky like desperate fingers, throwing sparks into the night that die before they reach the water, each one a brief moment of light that exists just long enough to remind you that everything beautiful is temporary.
There are maybe fifteen college students arranged in a loose circle around the fire, some sitting on logs, others on blankets, a few standing with bottles in their hands and conversations that seem more performed than genuine. The whole scene has this quality of deliberate casualness, like everyone's trying very hard to look like they're not trying at all.
And there, sitting on a piece of driftwood between two guys I don't recognize, is Mom.
She's changed clothes since this morning—now wearing a sundress that shows more skin than she usually displays in public, her hair loose around her shoulders in a way that makes her look younger but also somehow desperate. She's holding a plastic cup that's obviously not her first drink of the evening, and she's talking with the kind of animated gestures that suggest she's been the center of this conversation for a while.
"—so I said to him, David, just because you're a doctor doesn't mean you understand everything about life," she's saying, her voice carrying across the beach with perfect clarity. "There's a difference between clinical knowledge and actual experience, you know?"
The college kids are listening with the kind of polite attention you give to adults who are saying things that make you uncomfortable but you're too well-raised to interrupt. A few of them catch my eye when they notice me approaching, their expressions suggesting they recognize me as connected to this woman who's been entertaining them with stories about her marriage.
"Oak!" Mom calls out when she spots me, waving me over with enthusiasm that seems disproportionate to the situation. "Come meet my new friends! Everyone, this is my son Oakley. Isn't he handsome?"
The level of embarrassment this produces is almost physical, like someone just turned up the gravity and made it harder to stand upright. A few of the college kids smile politely, but I can see them processing the information that this middle-aged woman telling intimate stories about her husband is someone's mother, which probably makes her confessions more uncomfortable rather than less.
"Hi, Mrs. Shipley," says a girl I recognize from previous bonfire nights—probably Maggie, though I'm not certain. "We were just hearing about your family's adventures on the island."
"Oh, call me Diane, please. Mrs. Shipley makes me sound so old and married." Mom laughs at this like she's made a clever joke, though the humor seems to escape everyone else. "I was just telling everyone about the creative differences my husband and I have been having lately."
Creative differences. This is apparently Mom's euphemism for whatever arguments she and Dad have been having, dressed up in language that makes their marital problems sound like artistic disagreements between collaborators rather than the slow-motion collapse of a twenty-year relationship.
"Mom," I say carefully, "Dad will probably be back soon. Maybe we should head home for dinner."
"Oh, don't be such a worry-wart, darling. I'm having the most interesting conversation I've had in years. Did you know that Derek here is studying psychology? We've been discussing the nature of midlife transitions and the importance of authentic self-expression."
Derek—I remember the name now from previous encounters—grins at me in a way that suggests he's been encouraging Mom's confessions for his own entertainment rather than out of genuine interest in her psychological development. He's the kind of person who finds other people's pain amusing, which makes him exactly the wrong audience for Mom's current emotional state.
"Yeah, your mom's been telling us some pretty wild stories about marriage and compromise and what happens when people stop being honest with each other," Derek says, clearly enjoying the discomfort this statement produces. "Fascinating stuff from an anthropological perspective."
The way he says "anthropological" makes it clear that he sees Mom as a specimen rather than a person, an interesting example of middle-aged breakdown that he can study and discuss with his friends later. I want to tell him that real people's pain isn't entertainment, that families falling apart isn't material for his amateur psychology experiments, but saying that would just create more drama and give him more material to analyze.
"Mom," I try again, "I think you might want to slow down on the wine. You haven't eaten much today."
"I'm fine, sweetheart. Better than fine, actually. I'm finally having conversations with people who find me interesting instead of just useful."
This comment lands like a stone in still water, creating ripples of awkwardness that spread across the entire group. Several of the college kids suddenly find their drinks very interesting, while others exchange glances that communicate their shared understanding that they're witnessing something private and painful that was never meant for public consumption.
That's when I notice her. Sitting slightly apart from the main group, watching the whole scene with an expression that combines concern and understanding in a way that suggests she's seen this kind of thing before. Dark hair that catches the firelight, eyes that seem older than her face, something about her presence that feels different from the others—calmer, more self-contained, less interested in the performance aspects of college social life.

"Good things, I hope."
"Mostly. She's very proud of you. Says you're unusually mature for your age, very observant about people and situations."
The way she says this makes it sound like a compliment rather than the kind of backhanded praise adults usually offer when they mean "weird but harmless." For the first time since I arrived at the bonfire, I feel like someone is actually seeing me rather than just processing my presence as part of the awkward family drama they're witnessing.
"Can I talk to you for a minute?" she asks, gesturing away from the fire toward a quieter section of beach.
We walk down to the water's edge, where the waves lap against the rocky shore with the eternal rhythm that makes human problems seem both significant and insignificant at the same time. The moon is almost full, bright enough to see by, turning the water silver and making everything look like a scene from a movie about people having important conversations in beautiful settings.

She's crying now, tears running down her cheeks and reflecting the firelight like little streams of liquid flame. It's painful to watch and impossible to look away from, like witnessing an accident in progress that you can't prevent but somehow feel responsible for not stopping.

And that's when I understand that this isn't just about too much wine or midlife crisis or the normal problems that families work through over time. This is about something breaking that can't be fixed, something ending that maybe should have ended years ago but nobody had the courage to admit it.
The college kids are definitely uncomfortable now, shifting in their seats and making those subtle movements that suggest a group exodus is being planned. Derek has finally stopped grinning, maybe realizing that real human pain isn't as entertaining as he thought it would be.

She looks at me with eyes that are bright with tears and wine and something that might be relief. "Okay, sweetheart. Okay. Let's go home and pretend we know what that means."
We walk back to our campsite in silence, Mom leaning on my arm for support that's both physical and emotional. Behind us, the bonfire continues to burn, but the voices have gotten quieter, more subdued, like people processing something they weren't prepared to witness.
When we get to our tent, Dad still isn't back from his important phone calls. Mom disappears into her section without saying goodnight, and I can hear her moving around in there, probably changing clothes and trying to pretend that nothing significant just happened.
But something did happen. Something important and irreversible and probably necessary, even though it was painful for everyone involved.
For the first time in my life, I heard my mother tell the truth about her marriage. And the truth, it turns out, is that some things can't be fixed no matter how much love you throw at them, no matter how hard you try to be good enough to earn the happiness that everyone deserves but not everyone gets.

ISLAND FEVER
TIMOTHY J. SMITH
Chapter 1: The Architecture of Names
You know I'm feeling pretty hungry and everything. It's Wednesday, and the fog hangs over Penobscot Bay like God's own dirty laundry, thick enough to taste. Maggie's out there somewhere sailing with her ex-boyfriend and his college friends, threading between the islands that dot this stretch of Maine coast like stepping stones across eternity. I guess I'm too young to hang with that crowd—fourteen to their nineteen feels like the difference between planets—but Maggie said I act older than I am. I don't believe that really. I don't feel older. Most days I feel younger, like I'm moving backward through time while everyone else rushes forward into whatever complicated mess adults call living.
My name is Oakley Shipley—Oak for short—named after my grandfather who died in Korea before I was born. Dad says I'm "sturdy as an oak tree," but I think he just likes the symbolism. Trees that bend don't break, he always says, though he's never been particularly good at bending himself. I've been Oak to everyone except teachers who insist on reading "Oakley" from their attendance sheets like it's some kind of formal pronouncement, a name that carries more weight than I ever asked it to bear.
I have this thing about names. I like giving them to things that don't have proper ones, or changing them when the official names make no goddamn sense. It's like creating a map of the world that actually works, where everything is labeled according to what it really is instead of what some committee of dead politicians decided to call it two hundred years ago. Names have power—they shape how you see things, how you remember them, how you feel about approaching or avoiding them.
The thing is, nobody knows the real names of these islands except me and Sissy. We've mapped them all according to what they actually look like, what they actually do—Rock Island for the chunk of granite that rises straight out of deep water like a cathedral spire, Half Moon Island for the crescent-shaped beach that appears at low tide, Deer Island for the family of white-tails we spotted there last summer, One Tree Island for the solitary pine that somehow grows out of solid stone. Names that tell you exactly what you're looking at instead of honoring some forgotten bureaucrat. St. Agnes, McGovern, Kennedy, Lewis—what do those names tell you about the actual places? Nothing. They're just words floating around without connection to reality, like most of what adults call important.
I got to see all the islands from Dad's little Cessna two years ago, flying up the coast while he searched for some legendary fishing spot he'd read about in the New Yorker. From a thousand feet up, you can see the patterns that make sense—how the currents flow between the islands like liquid highways, where the deep water runs dark blue against the pale green shallows, why the birds choose certain rocks for nesting and avoid others completely. It's a whole geography of logic that only becomes visible when you get enough distance from it.
Dad likes to think of himself as this model outdoorsman, all L.L. Bean competence and masculine self-reliance, but he leads too comfortable a lifestyle for any of it to be real. Reading in his adjustable leather chair with those wire-rimmed glasses perched on his nose, surrounded by medical journals and the particular smell of important men doing important work, he could never make it alone in actual wilderness. Not really. He's playing at being rugged while staying completely insulated from anything that might actually test him.
But this island—Doughnut Island—it's different from his weekend warrior fantasies. The name fits perfectly because the whole thing is shaped like a ring, maybe three miles across, with a hole punched right through the middle where Horse Pond sits. And here's the strangest thing: Horse Pond isn't connected to the ocean at all. It's fresh water, fed by underground springs that bubble up from some deep aquifer, surrounded completely by salt water but somehow staying sweet. Like the island is keeping a secret at its heart, something pure in the middle of all that brine.
The pond is maybe half a mile across at its widest point, shallow enough in most places that you can wade across it if you don't mind the muck between your toes. But there are spots—dark holes where the springs feed up from below—where nobody's ever found the bottom. Dad says it's probably connected to underground rivers that run all the way to the mountains, water that's been traveling through limestone caves for decades before finally bubbling up here in the middle of the Atlantic.
The woods around Horse Pond are different too—darker, more ancient-feeling than the scrub pine and birch that cover most of Maine islands. There are oaks here that must be two hundred years old, their branches twisted into shapes that look almost deliberate, like someone trained them to grow in patterns that mean something if you know how to read them. The undergrowth is thick with ferns and moss, and there are patches where the ground stays damp even in the driest weather, where mushrooms grow in perfect fairy rings and the air tastes of decomposition and new growth happening simultaneously.
When I walk through those woods, my feet sink into decades of accumulated leaves, each step releasing the smell of things rotting and things being born. It's the smell of time itself, of the endless cycle that most people spend their lives trying to ignore. The nose is the shortest path to memory, and every breath in those woods stores itself in some deep part of my brain, filing itself away with all the other sensory information I collect without meaning to.
Everything here has texture and weight that you have to experience directly to understand. The birch bark peels off in long strips that feel like parchment, revealing smooth white wood underneath that's soft as skin. The rocks along the shore are worn smooth by centuries of waves, each one shaped by forces too patient and persistent for human comprehension. My feet have toughened up since we arrived three weeks ago—thick pads of callus that let me walk anywhere without shoes, feeling every surface directly through skin that's learned to read stone and sand and pine needles like braille.
Most people lose that connection early, insulate themselves from the world with rubber and leather and the general assumption that comfort is more important than sensation. But skin knows things that shoes can't learn. It knows the difference between granite warmed by sun and granite cooled by shade, between sand that's been under water and sand that's been exposed to air, between moss that grows on the north side of trees and moss that grows on the south.
The island has its own weather system too, different from the mainland fifteen miles away. Mornings start with fog that rolls in from the deep ocean, thick enough that you can't see your hand in front of your face, thick enough that sound travels differently—voices from a hundred yards away seem to come from right next to your ear, while your own footsteps disappear into gray silence. Then the sun burns through around ten o'clock, revealing the world in pieces like a photograph developing in slow motion. First the trees, then the water, then the other islands floating in the distance like dreams you half-remember from the night before.
By afternoon, the wind picks up from the southwest, carrying the smell of the mainland—car exhaust and hot asphalt and that particular staleness of too many people living too close together. But when evening comes and the wind dies, all you smell is ocean and pine needles and the clean scent of things that grow without asking anyone's permission.
My parents call this their third trip to Doughnut Island, though they always make it sound like some kind of pilgrimage to a sacred site instead of just a family vacation that's gotten too expensive to change. They point out the exact campsite where Sissy was supposedly conceived on their honeymoon eighteen years ago—a patch of ground near the southern shore that looks exactly like every other patch of ground on the island—and they add proudly, especially when Sissy's around, that they were both virgins at the time.
This always makes me feel sick in ways I can't quite name. It's not the sex part that bothers me—I understand the basic mechanics, have figured out most of what adults do when they think kids aren't paying attention. It's the way they announce it, like they're trying to prove something about their purity, their innocence, their qualification for some contest nobody else is entering. Like announcing your sexual history to your children somehow makes you more real, more worthy of respect.
But I think they know Sissy's not a virgin anymore, haven't been for years probably. You can tell by the way she carries herself now, this awareness of her body that wasn't there when she was younger. She moves like someone who knows secrets, who's crossed into territory that the rest of the family only talks about in careful euphemisms. Or at least she did until Sam the Red Head started coming around. Now she acts like she's never been with a guy before, all giggly and uncertain, like she's auditioning for the role of the girlfriend she thinks he wants instead of being whoever she actually is.
People change themselves for other people all the time. They reshape their personalities like clay, molding themselves into whatever form they think will make them lovable. I watch it happen and I don't understand the logic. Why would you want someone to love a fake version of you? Wouldn't that mean they don't actually love you at all, just some performance you've learned to give?
The island strips away those kinds of performances. No running water except what we pump from the well that tastes of minerals and something deeper, something that's been underground for decades before rising to meet the light. No electricity, just kerosene lanterns that cast dancing shadows and smell of burning oil and make every evening feel like we're living in some earlier century when people had to create their own light. Just the Coleman stove and the campfire and whatever we can catch or gather from the sea and forest.
It forces us all together in ways that feel dangerous, like being trapped in a pressure cooker with the safety valve removed. You can feel something building here, something that's been accumulating in our family for months, maybe years. The island doesn't let you hide from it behind television or telephone calls or the thousand small distractions that let suburban families avoid looking directly at each other. What's left is just who you really are, stripped of pretense and convenience, and for some people that's not a very comfortable thing to face.
Chapter 2: Sam the Red Head and the Mathematics of Dysfunction
So here I am, committed to three weeks on Doughnut Island with Mom, my sister Sissy, and her boyfriend Sam the Red Head. That's what I call him—Sam the Red Head—because it serves multiple functions in the complex algebra of family annoyance. First, it pisses Sissy off, which I enjoy more than I probably should but not more than any normal brother enjoys irritating his sister. Second, his hair is this aggressive orange that catches fire in sunlight like a warning beacon, the kind of color that nature uses to signal toxicity in certain species of frog. Third, and most importantly, I refuse to dignify him with his full name until he proves he deserves it, which given current evidence may happen sometime around the heat death of the universe.
His real name is Samuel Morrison, and he's what my mother would call "from a good family," which in Mom's vocabulary means his parents have money and send him to the right schools and probably own a house with more than one bathroom. But having money doesn't make you a good person any more than lacking money makes you bad. It just changes the type of problems you have and the resources available for solving them or ignoring them entirely.
Sam thinks he's sophisticated because he drinks beer instead of Coca-Cola and can pronounce the names of wines he's never actually tasted. He talks about things like "vintage years" and "notes of oak" while drinking whatever cheap swill the other college kids brought on their yacht, performing adulthood like it's a role he's rehearsing for some audition he'll never actually have to give. Everything about him is performance, this elaborate charade designed to convince everyone—especially himself—that he's already the man he hopes to become someday.
I can see through it because I watch how people behave when they think nobody's paying attention. Sam's different when he's talking to other guys—looser, cruder, more honest about his actual level of sophistication, which is roughly equivalent to a golden retriever who's learned to walk upright and open beer bottles. But around Sissy, he becomes this carefully constructed version of collegiate maturity, all sensitive conversation and meaningful glances and bullshit about how much he values intelligence in women.
Dad will helicopter in on weekends because he claims the hospital can't spare him for three full weeks, but I think he's still carrying on with Miss Pantyhose. That's my name for his research assistant, this woman who calls our house sometimes and hangs up when anyone except Dad answers the phone. Her real name is probably something aggressively normal like Jennifer or Susan, but Miss Pantyhose fits better because it describes what she represents—something artificial and uncomfortable that makes your skin itch and promises benefits it never actually delivers.
I like that word Dad used about me once—peculiar. He asked Mom if she thought I was "peculiar," like it was some kind of medical condition that might require treatment or at least careful monitoring. The way he said it made it sound dangerous, like being different was a threat to the natural order of things, a deviation that needed to be corrected before it spread to other members of the family.
But I think being peculiar might be the most honest way to exist in a world that's fundamentally strange. At least peculiar people aren't pretending that normal makes sense when normal is just a collective agreement to ignore how weird everything actually is. Normal people see a family spending three weeks on an island and call it a vacation. Peculiar people see the same family and recognize it as an experiment in forced intimacy that's probably going to end badly for everyone involved.
The second weekend here will be my parents' twentieth anniversary, and this whole trip was Mom's idea of how to spend their "second honeymoon," as she calls it with the kind of determined optimism that signals approaching disaster. The first honeymoon apparently worked out well enough to create Sissy, so maybe she's hoping for similar magic, some kind of atmospheric alchemy that will transform their failing marriage into something worth preserving.
But you can't repeat experiences like that. Time moves in one direction only, and people change whether they want to or not, like it or not, notice it or not. The couple who honeymooned here eighteen years ago no longer exists. They've been replaced by two middle-aged people who've spent two decades discovering that they want different things from life and have fundamentally incompatible ideas about how to get them.
I think Mom goes to therapy, though she's never said so directly. She loves to talk about concepts like "the twelve steps" and "full cycles" and "the big picture," like she's reading from some self-help manual written by people who've never met her but somehow understand exactly what's wrong with her life. She probably thinks Dad's affairs are out of his system now, that whatever was broken in their marriage has been fixed by the simple passage of time and the application of proper psychological techniques.
But people don't work like broken appliances. You can't just replace a defective part and expect everything to function normally again. Marriages especially resist that kind of mechanical thinking, because they're built on foundations of emotion and history and shared experience that can't be repaired, only acknowledged or ignored.
She'd like to have an affair of her own—I can see it in how she looks at other men sometimes, this hungry curiosity about what she's missing, what experiences have been denied to her by the circumstances of her life. She's jealous of Sissy's freedom to experiment, of Dad's apparent ability to do whatever he wants and then come home acting like nothing happened. But she's too afraid to step outside the boundaries of her role as doctor's wife, too invested in her identity as the responsible parent to risk the chaos that would come from actually pursuing what she wants.
She's not happy. You can smell it on her some days, this sour dissatisfaction that no amount of perfume can cover. It's the smell of dreams deferred so long they've started to rot, of compromises that turned into prisons so gradually she didn't notice the bars being installed.
Most of the time she sits around our campsite reading magazines that promise to teach her how to have the life she already has but better—Redbook, Good Housekeeping, Cosmopolitan—publications that exist to make women feel inadequate about their current situations while offering solutions that require purchasing products or services. She stares off into ocean trances, her eyes getting glassy and distant like she's watching movies of alternate lives where she made different choices, married different men, became different versions of herself.
Sometimes I catch her looking at Dad when he's not paying attention, and her face goes through these micro-expressions—love, hatred, disappointment, hope, resignation—like she's cycling through every emotion she's ever felt about him in the space of three seconds. It's like watching someone have an entire relationship in fast-forward, all twenty years of marriage compressed into a moment of unguarded observation.
She has this way of disappearing while still being physically present, like a ghost haunting her own existence. It's not just daydreaming—it's deeper than that, more systematic. Like she's practicing being somewhere else so intensely that she almost succeeds in actually going there.
At night she asks me to boil water on the Coleman stove, then dump it into a makeshift bathtub I constructed using a waterproof tarp and driftwood logs. The engineering required to make it hold water without leaking was actually pretty interesting—you have to understand weight distribution and hydrostatic pressure and how different materials expand when they get wet. The tub isn't very deep, maybe eighteen inches, but it works well enough for someone to sit in and feel temporarily civilized.
I have to mix in cold water from the well to cool the heated water down to something human skin can tolerate, because the Coleman stove gets the metal pot hot enough to cook lobster. One evening, by accident that I immediately wish I could undo, I see my mother's breasts for the first time that I can remember. She's stepping into the tub, and the kerosene lantern throws her shadow against the canvas wall of our shelter while also illuminating her directly for just a moment.
Her breasts are pale and soft, different from the controlled, properly dressed version of her I'm used to seeing. They look vulnerable in a way that makes my stomach feel strange, like I've witnessed something private and important that was never meant for my eyes. The image burns itself into my brain like a photograph I never asked to take, impossible to forget and inappropriate to remember.
It makes her seem human in a way that scares me. Parents aren't supposed to have bodies that age and sag and show the effects of time and childbearing. They're supposed to be these stable, predictable forces that keep the world organized according to comprehensible rules. Seeing her naked, even accidentally, reminds me that she's just a person making things up as she goes along, same as everyone else.
The worst part of this whole arrangement is sharing our tent with Sissy and Sam the Red Head. It's like being forced to live inside someone else's relationship, to witness all the private moments that should happen behind doors thicker than canvas and wishful thinking. Their section is separated from mine by a piece of fabric so thin I can hear everything—every whispered conversation, every shift in breathing, every sound they make when they think I'm asleep.
At night I listen to them doing what Sissy calls "being intimate," which sounds more medical than romantic, like something that requires proper equipment and sterile conditions. It involves a lot of heavy breathing and whispered negotiations and sounds that make my ears burn with embarrassment. It's like being forced to eavesdrop on something urgent and desperate, something that should happen behind walls thick enough to contain whatever emotions are being expressed.
The sounds they make aren't like the movies or the books that Derek sometimes shows me. They're more awkward, more tentative, less graceful than Hollywood would have you believe. Sometimes Sissy makes these small noises that could be pleasure or discomfort or just the sound of someone trying very hard to feel something she thinks she's supposed to feel. It makes me wonder if most adult experiences are like that—people performing emotions they think they should have rather than expressing whatever they actually feel.
Sam thinks he's God's gift to women, which is ironic because he's clearly still figuring out what women actually want beyond what he's learned from movies and magazines and the kind of locker room conversations that teenage boys mistake for wisdom. He's got these large freckles scattered across his shoulders like rust stains on white metal, and this way of talking that changes when he's around girls—gets all soft and syrupy like he's performing masculinity instead of just being male.
He douses Sissy with compliments that sound like they came from some manual: "Your hair looks GREAT in a bun." "You're so much prettier than the girls at Cornell." "I love how you laugh at my jokes." It's all surface-level observation, commentary about her appearance or her reactions to him. He never compliments her mind or her ideas or the things that make her uniquely herself rather than just another pretty girl who's willing to laugh at his attempts at humor.
Sissy eats it up anyway, responds with these fake "You really think so?" replies that sound nothing like the sister I've known my whole life. She's performing too, playing the role of the grateful girlfriend who's just thrilled to be chosen by someone as sophisticated as a college sophomore with a trust fund and a summer house. It's like watching two actors who learned their parts from different plays trying to perform a scene together.
I call him Sam the Red Head because I know it annoys Sissy, but also because names should describe reality rather than aspirations. His red hair is the most obvious thing about him, more noticeable than his personality or his intelligence or his character. When I look at him, that's what I see first—this aggressive orange that doesn't apologize for taking up space, like a traffic cone that's learned to walk upright and make conversation about wine it's never tasted.
Chapter 3: The Weight of Family Secrets
The morning arrives gray and thick, fog rolling in from the deep Atlantic like smoke from some enormous fire burning just beyond the horizon. Everything is muffled and strange, sounds traveling differently through air thick enough to taste. Even familiar landmarks disappear—the dock, the other islands, the mainland itself vanishing into gray nothing until Doughnut Island feels like the only solid thing left in the world.
I wake to the sound of my parents having what they probably think is a quiet argument in their tent, but canvas walls don't muffle much and I've developed excellent hearing for family dysfunction. Their voices rise and fall in patterns I recognize—Mom's tone suggesting accusation or disappointment, Dad's lower register carrying denial or justification. Then silence. Then the whole cycle starting again, like some kind of emotional loop they can't break out of.
Sissy emerges from our tent section looking like she slept badly, her hair tangled and dark circles under her eyes that suggest she spent the night listening to the same soundtrack of marital discord. We've both become meteorologists of our parents' relationship, sensing storms hours before they actually break, reading the atmospheric pressure of family dinners and car rides and casual conversations about nothing in particular.
"They've been at it since five-thirty," she whispers, joining me at the camp stove where I'm trying to make coffee without producing enough noise to wake the rest of the island. The Coleman stove hisses and pops, blue flames dancing underneath the dented aluminum pot that's seen too many camping trips and accumulated too many memories of family vacations that didn't quite work out as planned.
"What's it about this time?"
"Something about last night. Mom came back to the tent around midnight, and I guess Dad wasn't happy about how long she'd been gone."
I think about Mom at the bonfire, standing at the edge of the college kids' circle like someone trying to work up the courage to ask for admission to a club she's too old to join. There was something desperate in her posture, something hungry that made me uncomfortable to witness. Not because there's anything wrong with wanting to feel young and interesting and valued by people who don't know your history, but because the hunger was so naked, so obvious to everyone except her.
"She's changing," I tell Sissy. "Mom, I mean. She's becoming someone different."
"People don't just change overnight, Oak. Maybe she's just showing us who she's always been underneath all the doctor's wife stuff."
This is a disturbing thought—that the mother we've known for our entire lives might have been a performance, a role she was playing while her real self waited in the wings for permission to take center stage. If that's true, then nothing about our family has ever been what it seemed. We've been living with a stranger who was pretending to be our mother, and now the stranger is getting tired of pretending.
The fog begins to lift around eight o'clock, revealing the island in sections like a photograph developing in slow motion. First the trees emerge from the gray, then the rocky shoreline, then the other islands floating in the distance like half-remembered dreams. By the time Dad emerges from the tent, fully dressed despite the early hour and carrying his leather briefcase like armor against whatever chaos the day might bring, you can see all the way to the mainland.
"Morning, kids," he says, but his voice sounds strained, like he's been using it for purposes other than normal conversation. His hair is perfectly combed despite sleeping in a tent, and his shirt looks like it was pressed by someone who understands the importance of maintaining professional standards even in the wilderness.
"Where are you going?" Sissy asks, though we both know the answer.
"I need to make some calls. The hospital wants me to review some test results, and there's a conference call about the Morrison grant application." He gestures toward the mainland with his briefcase, like it contains evidence of his indispensability. "I'll take the boat over to Rockland, find a phone, be back this afternoon."
This is his standard escape plan—invent a medical emergency that requires his immediate attention, then disappear for several hours while the rest of us deal with whatever family crisis he's avoiding. It works because nobody can argue with cancer research, nobody can say that saving lives is less important than staying present for your own family's problems.
"What about Mom?" I ask, though I already know this question will make him uncomfortable.
Dad pauses in the act of organizing his papers, and for a moment his professional mask slips enough to show the exhaustion underneath. He looks older than his forty-five years, worn down by years of trying to solve everyone's problems except the ones that actually matter to the people he lives with.
"Your mother is sleeping. Let her rest. She had a... difficult night."
The way he says "difficult" makes it sound like a medical diagnosis rather than an emotional state. Everything becomes clinical when Dad wants to avoid dealing with it directly. Mom doesn't have feelings—she has symptoms. The family doesn't have problems—we have pathology that can be treated with proper care and professional distance.
After he leaves, taking the small motorboat across water that's still choppy from the night's wind, Sissy and I are left alone with whatever mood Mom will be in when she finally emerges from the tent. This has become our normal routine—Dad disappears at the first sign of emotional complexity, Mom becomes unpredictable, and we figure out how to navigate the psychological minefield without setting off any explosions that might damage what's left of our family's reputation for having everything under control.
"I'm going to make breakfast," Sissy announces, which is her way of establishing dominion over at least one aspect of our morning. She's gotten good at this over the years—creating small islands of normalcy in the chaos of our family's dysfunction, maintaining routines that provide the illusion of stability even when everything else is falling apart.
I watch her work, arranging bacon in the cast iron skillet with the precision of someone who needs everything to be exactly right because so much of her life is exactly wrong. The bacon sizzles and pops, sending up wisps of smoke that smell like comfort and routine and Saturday mornings when our biggest worry was whether we'd have enough syrup for pancakes.
Mom finally emerges around ten o'clock, wearing sunglasses even though the fog hasn't completely lifted and the day is more gray than bright. Her hair is hidden under a silk scarf tied in a way that suggests she saw it in a magazine and decided it looked sophisticated, European, like something a woman with a more interesting life might wear. She moves carefully, like someone who's afraid that sudden movements might trigger pain she's not ready to acknowledge.
"Good morning, darlings," she says, but her voice sounds hollow, like it's coming from the bottom of a well. "What a beautiful day."
It's not particularly beautiful—still gray and damp, with the kind of chill that gets into your bones and stays there until the sun finally decides to do its job properly. But Mom has always had this ability to declare reality instead of acknowledging it, to insist that things are the way she wants them to be rather than the way they actually are.
"Dad went to Rockland," Sissy tells her, offering a cup of coffee that Mom accepts but doesn't drink, just holds between her hands like she's trying to absorb warmth through the ceramic.
"Of course he did." There's no surprise in her voice, just the weary resignation of someone who's learned not to expect different behavior from people who've already shown her exactly who they are. "Well, that gives us girls some time to ourselves, doesn't it?"
The way she says "us girls" makes me feel excluded even though I'm standing right there, like she's drawing an invisible circle around herself and Sissy that has no room for male perspectives or teenage observations about what's really happening in our family. It's like she's trying to create an alliance based on gender that ignores the fact that Sissy and I have been each other's primary support system for years.
"What do you want to do today?" Sissy asks carefully, in the tone she uses when she's trying to assess Mom's emotional weather without triggering any storms.
"I thought we might visit those college students again. They seemed like such interesting young people."
There's something in Mom's voice when she says this that makes my stomach clench with anxiety. It's the same tone she uses when she's planning to buy something expensive that we can't afford, or when she's decided to redecorate the living room without consulting anyone else about color schemes or budget limitations. It's the voice of someone who's made a decision and is now looking for ways to justify it to people whose opinions don't really matter but whose cooperation she needs.
"I don't think they're expecting visitors," I say, though I already know this reasonable objection will have no effect on whatever plan she's hatching.
"Oh, but I'm sure they wouldn't mind. Young people love having older folks around to share wisdom and experience."
This is so far from reality that I don't know how to respond without being openly rude to my mother. College students don't want middle-aged women crashing their parties and offering unsolicited life advice. They want to be left alone to make their own mistakes without adult supervision or commentary. But Mom has convinced herself that her presence would be welcome, even beneficial, like she's some kind of youth counselor instead of a forty-two-year-old woman having what appears to be a midlife crisis on a remote island in Maine.
Chapter 4: The Ranger and the Reckoning
Mac Herron shows up every morning at exactly eight-thirty, like he's following some internal clock that's been calibrated to island time and the rhythms of people who live according to natural light instead of artificial schedules. Same khaki pants worn soft from years of use, same emerald green shirt with the Maine State Park Service patch, same silver badge that catches the early sunlight like a signal mirror, same ranger hat that looks like it's weathered a few serious storms but held up fine through all of them.
He's got this quiet way of moving through space that makes you want to tell him things you've never told anyone else, like he's carrying secrets for half the people who visit this island and has room for a few more in whatever mental vault he uses to store other people's confessions. His presence is calming in a way that has nothing to do with authority and everything to do with competence—the kind of person who knows how to handle emergencies without creating drama, how to solve problems without making them worse.
I watch him sometimes from my spot on the rocks near the dock, where I like to eat breakfast while planning my day's exploration of the island's hidden places. Usually it's just cold cereal with powdered milk mixed with well water that tastes like minerals and earth and something deeper, something that's been underground for decades before rising to meet the light. Mac walks the perimeter of the island every morning, checking on campsites, picking up trash that inconsiderate people leave behind like calling cards of their presence, making sure fire pits are properly extinguished and won't reignite when the wind changes direction.
He moves with purpose but without hurry, like someone who understands that most problems solve themselves if you give them enough time and the right kind of attention. He stops to talk with the Bird Watchers—this elderly couple who show up every summer with binoculars that seem to have become permanent parts of their faces, like some kind of evolutionary adaptation. The old man has knees bent at wrong angles from decades of hiking, pale white legs like a heron, but he can identify any bird call from fifty yards away and tell you not just the species but the specific behavior it's communicating. His wife keeps a detailed journal of every species they spot, with notes about feeding patterns and migration routes and the subtle changes in population that most people would never notice.
Mac knows everyone's story without having to ask intrusive questions or make people uncomfortable with unwanted attention. He knows which campers are here to save their marriages and which ones are here to end them. He knows which teenagers are drinking too much and which college kids are sleeping with people they shouldn't be sleeping with. He knows which families are one argument away from complete implosion and which ones are just going through the normal friction of people forced to live in close quarters without the usual distractions of television and separate bedrooms and the thousand small escapes that make family life bearable.
But he keeps it all to himself, stores the information without redistributing it or using it to make anyone feel judged or observed. That's what makes him trustworthy—he collects data about human behavior the way a scientist collects specimens, for understanding rather than manipulation. In a place like this, where privacy is limited and secrets are hard to keep, having someone who understands the value of discretion is essential for maintaining the kind of atmosphere where people can relax enough to show you who they really are.
I learn years later that Mac dies at sea the winter after this summer, his fishing boat found empty and drifting off the coast of Nova Scotia with no distress signal and no evidence of what happened. The Coast Guard speculates about equipment failure, medical emergency, maybe just a wave that caught him off guard and swept him overboard before he had time to react. But that morning, watching him make his rounds with that quiet competence, he seems like the most permanent thing on the island, more solid and reliable than the granite foundations that anchor everything else.
"Morning, Oak," he says when he reaches our campsite, his voice carrying the kind of genuine warmth that most adults reserve for other adults. He's one of the few people who remembers to call me by my preferred name instead of reading "Oakley" off some official list and deciding that formality is more appropriate than familiarity.
"How's the crabbing been?"
"Pretty good yesterday. Caught twenty-one, but I let eight of them go."
"The small ones?"
"No, the big ones. I figured they were smarter because they were harder to catch. Seemed unfair to punish them for being good at staying alive."
