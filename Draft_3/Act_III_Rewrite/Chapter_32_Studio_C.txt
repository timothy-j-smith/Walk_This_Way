CHAPTER 32
STUDIO C

The hallway to Studio C smelled like tape oxide and stale cigarettes. Maxanne walked ahead of them, her boot heels cracking on the linoleum, not looking back to see if they followed. She'd been waiting in the lobby when Grace's Buick pulled into the lot — standing under the WBCN sign with her arms crossed and her leather jacket zipped to her throat, looking less like a radio legend and more like a woman who'd just bet something she couldn't afford to lose.

Grace stayed in the car. She'd said "Go" the way she said everything — once, without elaboration — and Nick had gotten out and found Jackie already standing on the sidewalk, his hands in his peacoat pockets, breath clouding, waiting.

It was 2:14 AM.

Studio C was smaller than Nick expected. He'd imagined something palatial — walls of equipment, a mixing board the size of a car, the kind of room where Aerosmith or the Cars or the J. Geils Band laid down the tracks that Maxanne spun into gold. Instead: a room the size of his mother's living room. Acoustic panels on the walls like quilted armor. Two microphones on boom stands, facing each other, four feet apart. A window into the control room where a kid in a Red Sox cap sat behind a board that was, in fact, the size of a small car.

"Mike," Maxanne said, knocking on the glass. "They're here."

The kid looked up. Twenty-five, maybe. Wire-rimmed glasses. The kind of enthusiasm that radiated off him like heat from an engine. He gave a thumbs up through the glass and started flipping switches. Lights changed on the board — red, green, amber — and the room began to hum with the particular frequency of electronics waking up.

"Two mics," Nick said. "Where's the amp?"

"DI box." Maxanne pointed to a small metal rectangle on the floor near the right microphone, a cable snaking from it to the wall. "Plug your guitar straight in. Mike will handle the tone from the board."

"I've never played through a DI."

"Tonight you have."

Jackie stood in the center of the room, turning slowly. An upright piano sat against the far wall — not a Casio with cracked keys, but a Yamaha U3, its black lacquer reflecting the overhead lights in long bright streaks. He lifted the fallboard. The keys were perfect. Weighted. Responsive. He pressed middle C and the note bloomed through the room with a clarity that made the garage version sound like it had been played through a blanket.

"Sit," Maxanne said, pointing Jackie to the piano bench and Nick to a stool near the DI box. "Tune up. Mike needs five minutes for levels."

She walked into the control room and closed the door. Through the glass, Nick watched her lean down to Mike's ear and say something. Mike nodded. His hands moved across the board.

Nick plugged into the DI box. Without an amp, his guitar sounded thin and naked through the monitor speakers — all string and no body, like a voice without a chest behind it. He played a chord. The monitors shaped it, added warmth, gave it room. Mike's hands on the board. The kid knew what he was doing.

Jackie ran scales. His fingers moved on the Yamaha the way a swimmer moves in open water — long strokes, effortless, the technique so internalized it had become invisible. The piano responded like it had been waiting for him.

Maxanne's voice came through the overhead speakers, studio-clear: "Here's the deal. One take. No overdubs. No punch-ins. What you played in that garage — play it here."

Nick looked at Jackie through the space between the two microphones. Four feet. "She was listening?"

Jackie's hands stopped on the keys. "How?"

Maxanne: "Grace's kitchen window was open. I called the house ten minutes before I called you."

Nick thought about that. His mother's kitchen window, cracked two inches because the radiator made the room too hot. The sound of their playing carrying through the gap, across the yard, through the phone line to Maxanne's ear. Like a frequency searching for a receiver.

"One take," Jackie said.

"One take," Maxanne confirmed.

Mike's voice, younger and less steady: "Levels are good. Whenever you're ready."

Nick and Jackie looked at each other. Four feet of studio air between them. Two microphones. Two instruments. Thirty years of music between them if you counted forward from this moment, which neither of them could do.

"You start," Nick said.

Jackie put his hands on the keys. He closed his eyes. For three seconds, nothing. Then:

D minor. The chord that had opened the door in the garage. But here, on the Yamaha, in the acoustic treatment of Studio C, the chord didn't just open a door. It knocked down a wall. The overtones spiraled into the room and hung there, suspended, ringing, alive.

Nick's hands found the bass line. "Sweet Emotion" — the riff he'd played a thousand times in the garage, at rehearsals, alone at night when sleep wouldn't come and music was the only thing that kept the dark from getting too close. But he played it different now. The DI stripped away the distortion, the amp growl, the feedback that masked imperfection. What was left was the melody underneath — clean, exposed, vulnerable. The bones of the song without the muscle.

Jackie heard it and responded. His left hand picked up the riff, doubled it an octave higher on the piano, and for four bars they played the same line in unison — guitar and piano, the same phrase, the same breath. Then Jackie's right hand drifted upward, and the Queen melody entered.

"Is this the real life." Not sung — played. The piano carried the vocal line the way a river carries a leaf, without effort, without destination. And underneath it, Nick's guitar held the Aerosmith groove, steady and patient, a foundation the melody could trust.

The songs merged. Not blended — that was too gentle a word. They merged the way tributaries merge, each one losing its name but not its current, the combined flow stronger than either stream alone. The "Sweet Emotion" groove became the heartbeat. The "Bohemian Rhapsody" melody became the breath. And in the space between — the harmonic space that no textbook described and no theory explained — something new existed. Something that belonged to neither Aerosmith nor Queen but to two boys in a studio at 2 AM who had found, in the overlap of their separate obsessions, a sound that was theirs.

Nick's eyes were closed. He didn't need to see the fretboard. His fingers knew the way. The riff evolved — "Walk This Way" crept in, Tyler's strut translated into guitar, and then the Aerosmith and Queen fragments wove together, trading phrases, answering each other, carrying on the conversation they'd started in a garage when they were thirteen and didn't know enough to be afraid.

Jackie opened the piano up. The Yamaha's full range — bass to treble, thunder to glass — and he used all of it. The operatic section of "Bohemian Rhapsody" arrived not as theater but as architecture, each chord a room inside a house the two of them were building in real time. Galileo. Figaro. Magnifico. The words were absent but the structure was there, and Nick's guitar sang the counter-melody that Brian May had written for three overdubbed guitars, except Nick played it on one, and the single line cut through the piano chords like light through a prism.

Four minutes. The music breathed. It stumbled once — Nick's fingers slipped on the twelfth fret, caught themselves, turned the mistake into a bend that Jackie matched with a crushed note on the piano — and the stumble became part of the song, a crack that let the light in, proof that this was live and real and happening now and would never happen again in exactly this way.

The hard rock section hit. Nick dug in. Jackie hammered the keys. For thirty seconds they played loud enough to rattle the acoustic panels, the monitors feeding back the sound in a loop that built and built until it crested and broke, and then —

Silence.

Two beats of silence.

Jackie played the final chord. D minor again, where they'd started, but this time with the sustain pedal down, the note ringing into the room, fading like the last light of a day neither of them wanted to end.

Nick lifted his hands from the strings. The monitors hummed. The tape rolled.

Four minutes and forty-three seconds.

---

Through the glass, Mike sat motionless, his hands flat on the board. His mouth was open. Maxanne stood behind him, one hand on the back of his chair, the other pressed flat against the glass.

She pressed the talkback button. "Stay there."

She walked into the studio, crossed to the tape machine, and rewound. The reels spun backward with a whirring sound that filled the room like a held breath being taken back.

"Listen," she said, and pressed play.

The sound came through the monitors — their own playing, returned to them, both familiar and strange the way a recording always is, because a recording captures not just the music but the room, the air, the moment, and hearing it played back is like watching yourself through a window.

Nick heard his guitar and didn't recognize it. The DI had captured something the amplifier always hid — a clarity in his phrasing, a tenderness in the way he bent notes, a quality he'd been taught to bury under volume because volume was what rock guitar was supposed to be. Without the distortion, he sounded like himself. Not like Joe Perry. Not like Jimmy Page. Like Nick Johnson, seventeen, playing in the middle of the night because the music asked him to.

Jackie heard his piano and felt his chest tighten. The Yamaha had captured everything — the classical training, the rock instinct, the war between them, and the moment where the war ended and the two traditions stopped fighting and started listening to each other. He sounded like no one he'd been trained to sound like. He sounded like what he'd been afraid to become.

The recording ended. The monitors hissed. Tape silence.

Jackie pressed his fingers to his eyes. His shoulders drew up. When he dropped his hands, his face was wet.

"This is what we could have been," he said.

Nick looked at him. The wetness on Jackie's face caught the studio lights.

"We could still be this," Nick said.

Maxanne leaned against the tape machine. She crossed her arms. She looked at both of them with the expression of a woman who had been in rooms where music happened for thirty years and knew exactly what she'd just witnessed and exactly what it cost.

"You could walk away from everything," she said. "Juilliard. The Army. Forget the plan. Choose this."

The room changed. The air thickened. The hum of the monitors became the only sound, and it sounded like a question that had been asked a hundred thousand times in a hundred thousand rooms where young musicians sat across from the choice between what they were and what they were supposed to become.

Nick looked at the clock on the wall. Digital. Red numbers. 3:17 AM.

He thought about Grace.

She was in the parking lot. Right now. At 3:17 in the morning, in a Buick with a dying timing belt, wearing a Friendly's uniform she'd been in since six that morning. Twenty-one hours. She'd driven him here without asking why, without complaining, without mentioning that she had to be back at the restaurant in five hours for the Sunday brunch shift that she'd volunteered for because Sundays paid time and a half.

She'd stood up at the PTA meeting when Dr. Taub tried to cut the music program. She'd stood alone — the only parent in the room in a work uniform, the only one without a college degree, the only one who couldn't cite studies or statistics — and she'd said, "My son plays guitar. That's all I know. But you should hear him." And the room had gone quiet, and the program had survived.

She'd confronted Dr. Taub again at the school board meeting when the Battle of the Bands funding was threatened. She'd walked up to the microphone during public comment and said, "This matters," and when Dr. Taub asked her to elaborate, she said, "No. It just matters," and sat down, and three people in the audience had started clapping.

She worked doubles so Nick could have a childhood that wasn't consumed by the same survival calculus that consumed hers. She cashed her checks and paid the mortgage and kept the house Dan had built and never once said the words that Nick knew she thought every night: I can't do this alone.

He couldn't tell her he was walking away from college. He couldn't tell her he was giving up the Army — the tuition, the steady pay, the health insurance, the path out of the working-class bargain that had killed his father — to chase a sound in a studio at three in the morning. He couldn't walk out to that parking lot and lean into the Buick's window and say, "Mom, I'm going to be a musician," and watch her face do the thing it did when she was terrified but wouldn't show it.

He couldn't. Not because he didn't want to. Because she had already given everything, and asking for more was a kind of theft he wasn't capable of.

Jackie sat at the piano bench. His hands rested on his thighs, away from the keys, as if touching them might make the decision for him.

He thought about his father.

Fourteen years of lessons. Every Saturday, the drive from Sudbury to Boston — forty minutes each way through Route 9 traffic, Dr. Marsh's Volvo smelling like leather and the Bach cantatas he played on the tape deck because he said Bach was the foundation of everything. Fourteen years of recitals, competitions, auditions. The Juilliard application his father had helped him write — three drafts, each one more careful than the last, each one a collaboration between a son who played and a father who had stopped playing and never said why.

Dr. Marsh had saved for Juilliard since Jackie was five. A separate account. Monthly deposits. Jackie had found the bankbook once, in the desk drawer in his father's study, and the column of numbers had stretched across three pages, twelve years of deposits, and next to each one, in his father's precise handwriting, the date and the amount and nothing else. No notes. No annotations. Just the numbers. As if the act of saving were its own explanation.

His father had given up the guitar. Packed it in a case and put it in the attic and chosen the career, the stability, the rational path. And every day since, he'd been pouring whatever he'd sacrificed into his son — not the same dream, but a version of it, translated into classical training and Juilliard and the life of a professional musician that Dr. Marsh believed was the safe version of the dangerous thing he'd wanted.

He was wrong. Jackie knew that. His father believed in the wrong version of him — the recital version, the competition version, the version that played Chopin with impeccable technique and empty hands. But the belief itself was real. The fourteen years were real. The drives, the deposits, the three drafts of the application. His father had built a bridge from his own abandoned dream to his son's future, and Jackie couldn't burn it. Not even for this.

"I can't," Nick said.

Jackie closed his eyes. "Me either."

The words fell into the room like stones into water. The ripples spread to the walls and came back changed.

"It's not giving up," Nick said. His voice was rough. He cleared his throat and it didn't help. "It's choosing the people who already chose us."

Maxanne pushed off the tape machine. She walked to the window that looked into the control room, where Mike sat with his headphones around his neck, watching them through the glass with the expression of a man who understood he was witnessing something he'd spend years trying to describe.

"That's what I told myself in 1980," Maxanne said. Her back was to them. Her reflection floated in the glass — translucent, superimposed over the mixing board and the meters and the blinking lights of the equipment that recorded and preserved and broadcast other people's music into the dark.

The room held still. The tape machine had stopped. The reels were motionless.

Jackie stood. He looked at Nick. Nick looked back. Two years of silence. One night of music. A recording on a tape that might never be played again.

"If things were different," Jackie said.

"Yeah," Nick said.

Jackie extended his hand. Nick took it. They shook — one firm grip, held for two seconds, released. Not a hug. A hug would have been too much. A hug would have acknowledged what they were losing, and neither of them could afford to acknowledge it and still walk out of the room.

Nick went through the studio door. Jackie went through the control room. Separate exits. The hallway split and they walked in opposite directions, toward opposite ends of the building, toward the separate lives that were waiting for them the way debts wait — patient, certain, compounding.

---

Maxanne stood alone in Studio C. The acoustic panels absorbed her reflection. The microphones stood on their boom stands, angled toward empty chairs, pointing at the space where the music had been.

Mike appeared in the control room window. He pressed the talkback button.

"What do we do with this?" he asked. He held up the tape reel — seven inches of magnetic tape, four minutes and forty-three seconds, everything.

Maxanne looked at the reel in his hands. She thought about Aerosmith in 1973 — five guys in a van, playing a club in Kenmore Square, and her at the board, knowing before the first chorus ended that they were going to change everything. She'd been right. She'd played their demo on WBCN and the phones had lit up and the rest was history, the kind of history that happened because one person in one room at one moment said yes.

This was different. This wasn't a band she could break. This was a recording of a thing that no longer existed — a partnership dissolved, a harmony abandoned, a choice made in the dead space between 3 AM and dawn. You couldn't break a band that had already broken itself.

"We preserve it," she said. "Because somebody needs to remember this was possible."

Mike handed her the reel through the pass-through window. She held it in both hands. Light. Lighter than it should have been for what it carried.

She put the tape in her purse. She turned off the studio lights. She walked out of WBCN into the 3 AM dark, where the parking lot held one car — Grace Johnson's Buick, engine idling, headlights off — and two empty spaces where two boys had already gone.
