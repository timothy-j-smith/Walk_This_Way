# ACT I

Prologue: THE LAST VOICE OF BOSTON ROCK,  AUGUST 2009

Four hours. That's what I've got left.

Four hours before WBCN becomes just another frequency in the graveyard of American radio. Four hours before the suits flip the switch and turn us into automated Christian contemporary—because apparently Jesus rocks harder than Freddie Mercury ever did. Four hours before fifty-six years of breaking bands, of late-night conversations with musicians who poured their souls into three-minute songs, of watching Boston transform from a city of steel and sweat into whatever the hell it's become now—four hours before all of that gets erased like a bad demo tape.

I'm Maxanne Sartori. Sixty-five years old, sitting in Studio B with a bourbon that costs more than most people's car payments, watching the ON AIR sign blink red like a dying heartbeat. Can you believe it? The station called and brought me back. This one last time, and I'm in the same studio where I watched Steven Tyler hear his own voice on the radio for the first time back in '73—the look on his face when he realized that raw, untamed sound was actually going out to thousands of people, that it was real, that it mattered. The same turntables that spun the first singles from bands who went on to fill stadiums. The same microphone that's carried my voice through the 70's, the years of chaos, rebellion, and music that made parents be wary of what was on the horizon.

"Maxanne?" The program director's voice crackles through the intercom like a bad phone connection. The Kid's twenty-eight, wears sneakers that cost more than my first apartment—a studio in the Back Bay I rented with my first WBCN paycheck—and thinks rock history started with Nirvana. "You've got four hours left. Make them count."

Make them count. 

Make them count. As if four hours could contain what WBCN meant to a city that lived and breathed rock and roll. As if I could summarize fifty-six years in some tidy narrative arc with a beginning, middle, and end. As if the story I'm about to tell could be wrapped up with a happy ending and a lesson learned. It just doesn't work that way I'm afraid. 

But here's the thing about endings—they make liars of us all. You promise to go out with dignity, say something profound, make those final moments matter. And then you find yourself sitting in a radio booth with a bottle of Pappy Van Winkle that Steven Tyler himself sent when he heard about the station closing. 'For the first lady who ever gave a damn,' the note said. Even after all these years, even after becoming one of the biggest rock stars in the world, he still remembers.

I adjust the headphones—the same Sony MDR-7506s I've worn since 1971, held together now with electrical tape and pure stubbornness. The console lights pulse expectantly. Outside the booth windows, Boston glitters in the October darkness—all glass towers and gentrification where dive bars used to breed bands, where the Rat used to host shows that would make your grandmother blush, where kids with nothing but dreams could still believe they had a shot at changing the world.

"This is Maxanne Sartori, and for the next four hours, you're listening to the final broadcast of WBCN, 104.1 on your FM dial. The Rock of Boston. The station that launched Aerosmith when they were just kids from New Hampshire with more attitude than money. The station that gave J. Geils his first shot, that broke The Cars when they were still just a garage band, that made Boston believe it could rock as hard as anywhere else."

I take a sip of bourbon—another violation of modern radio protocol, but who's going to fire me? The warmth spreads through my chest. The good kind that makes you tell stories you've been carrying too long.

"But tonight isn't about the bands that made it. Tonight is about the ones that didn't, and there were many. And tonight is about one I could never get out of my head after all these years - it's about two kids from Sudbury, who should have changed everything but got caught in the crossfire of America's class war. Tonight is about the night I watched two potential stars die in a high school auditorium, when two seventeen-year-old boys who should have been allies became rivals over a question that seems quaint now: What is authentic rock and roll? But of course it was deeper than rock, much deeper.  It always is. 

The silence stretches across the city like a held breath. In the engineering booth, the kid they've assigned to babysit my final show looks up from his phone. Maybe something in my voice warns him this won't be the nostalgia trip he expected.

March, 1979. Disco was gasping its last breath. The Bee Gees had spent three years convincing America that falsetto voices and dance floors were the future of music. 'Saturday Night Fever' had turned every bar into a discotheque, 'Stayin' Alive' had replaced actual living, and 'Night Fever' had given us all a nasty cold we couldn't shake. But authentic rock and roll was fighting back. Fighting back hard. And the Clash were about to teach America that punk had something to say too. MTV was three years away from making image matter more than sound. We were living in the last moments when two kids with nothing but talent and passion could matter as much as marketing budgets.

I spin my chair to face the wall of albums, each one a memory, a story, a moment when music mattered more than metrics. The vinyl spines catch the booth's amber light, each one a ghost. My fingers find the right cart without looking—muscle memory, my hands knowing these grooves better than my own face in the mirror.

"Nick Johnson lived in a house where the mortgage was late and dreams were luxuries you couldn't afford. His father had died in a construction accident two years earlier, crushed beneath a load of steel beams when a crane cable snapped—the kind of industrial tragedy that makes page three of the Metro section and gets forgotten by everyone except the families who eat silence for dinner afterward. His mother worked doubles at Friendly's, came home smelling like fryer oil and other people's happiness, and never once complained about her son spending grocery money on guitar strings."

I can see the kid in the WBCN engineering booth scrolling through his phone now, probably updating his status to "babysitting some old lady's final radio show." He has no idea what a garage means to rock and roll. He grew up with GarageBand, thinking music could be manufactured in a bedroom with the right plugins. He never felt four walls, concrete floor, and kids who had nothing to lose.  
                 
"Jackie Marsh lived less than a mile away as the crow flies, but between them stretched Hop Brook—named a century ago for its hops, the old mill long gone to modernization. The brook divided Sudbury like a scar, separating the haves from the have-nots. In winter, kids from both sides skated the same nearby pond trying to emulate Bobby Orr's graceful glides across the ice, but when the ice melted, they returned to their proper territories. Jackie's father a Trauma Psychiatrist and a professor at Boston University.  A social climber.  He published papers about childhood trauma and yet was ignorant to what actually evolved around him.

I pause, letting the silence breathe. Radio silence used to terrify program directors, but I learned long ago that sometimes the most powerful thing you can do is shut up and let the music speak.

"These boys lived in the same town, went to the same school, walked the same streets. But America designs its suburbs specifically to prevent these collisions—invisible lines drawn by property values, school districts, the careful architecture of keeping the classes in their proper lanes. But music has always been democracy's wild card, the wavelength that cuts through all the bullshit boundaries we build."

Through the booth window, I can see the billboard across Boylston Street advertising luxury condos where The Rat used to host bands too raw for radio. Twenty-four hundred square feet starting at 2.2 million—you could buy the entire block of Lansdowne Street for that in '77. The Ramones played that club back when Joey was so tall he had to duck under the pipes backstage, and the sound system was held together with duct tape and prayers.

"I met them both in the spring of 1979, about two months before the Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School Battle of the Bands. I was there as a judge, WBCN sponsoring the event, back when we could afford to care about local music. Back when Clear Channel hadn't swallowed half the FM dial and algorithms hadn't replaced intuition. This was WBCN's first regional competition, and it was a big deal—not just for the kids, not just for Sudbury, but for WBCN. We were scouting talent, sure, but we were also building our audience, steering more kids to our channel, which meant more paid advertisements and a stronger foothold in the Boston music scene. The success of this first Battle would determine whether we'd sponsor more in the coming years."

I reach for the cassette on my desk, feeling the weight of it in my hands. The plastic case is warm from my grip, the label slightly faded, but still cataloged in it's perfect place 30 years later. "Demo - Nick/Jackie - un-Authorized - March 1979" written in block letters.  My letters. The tape itself has that old-cassette smell—plastic and magnetic oxide and the faint trace of cigarette smoke. I can still remember that day, March 20, 1979, shortly after the Battle of the Bands, when they both came to this very studio and proved that harmony was still possible, even after everything that had happened.

"This tape I am holding is the only recording that exists of Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh playing together. The shred of evidence that we chose division over music. The rest of the story? That's what the next four hours are for. So pour yourself something strong, turn off your IPhone, and let me tell you how the Battle of the Bands became a war and about "The Band That Never Was."

The ON AIR sign pulses. Four hours left to tell the truth, four hours left to make sense of 30 years of watching America choose noise over harmony.

I crack my knuckles and lean into the microphone one more time.

"But first, we need to talk about a garage on Dutton Road, and why the ghost of a dead construction worker might have been the only adult who understood what was really at stake ... "

I close my eyes and let the memory take me back. March 1979. Cold enough that you could see your breath, but the kind of cold that felt alive, promising that spring was coming whether winter liked it or not. That's when I first drove out to Sudbury, following rumors about a kid who played guitar like he was trying to resurrect the dead.

CHAPTER 1: NICK'S WORLD — THE GARAGE

Maxanne Sartori drove up to the Johnson house in her powder-blue Karmann Ghia, the car bottoming out twice on potholes the town never fixed on the working-class side of Hop Brook. Sparks kicked off the undercarriage. She didn't care. She'd heard rumors about a kid on Dutton Road—a kid with a voice that could stop you mid-dial-turn, playing in a garage where the sound punched you in the chest before you opened the door.

The Johnson house squatted like it was tired: mustard paint peeling in long strips, gutters sagging, front steps listing to port. The lawn had surrendered to New England months ago, brown grass poking through dirty snow like stubble on a dead man's face. The bones of the house were good—wide clapboard, solid frame, the kind of place that had been built by someone who knew what plumb meant. It looked like it had once been something more.

But from the detached garage came something alive. Distorted power chords hit the cold March air and traveled through the concrete foundation, through the frozen ground, up through the soles of Maxanne's boots and into her chest. She felt it before she heard it clearly. That was always the test.

Grace Johnson answered the door in her Friendly's uniform, polyester clinging to her frame, name tag reading GRACE in that cheerful corporate font that lies about everything. She smelled like fryer oil and coffee and the particular exhaustion of a woman working her second double this week.

She studied Maxanne—bell-bottoms, oversized sunglasses, standing on the cracked front step like a visitor from a planet where people bought clothes that didn't come from the Jordan Marsh clearance rack.

"You're that lady from WBCN. The one who broke Aerosmith."

"Guilty as charged."

"Nick's in the garage." Grace paused. Something crossed her face—not quite hope, more like the memory of hope, and the fear of what it cost. "Don't fill his head with dreams we can't afford."

"Mind if I listen?"

Grace held her gaze a beat longer than comfortable. "He's been at it since school let out. Won't stop until dinner."

Maxanne crossed the yard toward the garage. The sound grew with each step—bass thrum in the frozen dirt, a snare crack that echoed off the neighbor's siding. The garage door hung open six inches at the bottom, enough to see slivers: a bare bulb swinging on its cord, the silhouette of a kid bent over a guitar, four concrete walls holding more noise than they were built for.

She ducked under the door.

Inside, the air was different—warmer, thicker, heavy with the smell of sawdust and motor oil and the metallic bite of old concrete. Dan Johnson's tools hung on a pegboard along the back wall, arranged by size with a precision that spoke of a man who respected his instruments. Claw hammers, box wrenches, a socket set with an empty slot where the 9/16ths used to live—the one he'd had in his pocket that November morning when the cable snapped. Maxanne noticed something else on the pegboard, tucked behind a pipe wrench: a flattened guitar pick, tortoiseshell, worn thin. And below it, barely visible behind a rusted coffee can, the spine of a spiral notebook with staff lines printed on the pages. Sheet music. Dan Johnson's private language, hidden in plain sight among his public one.

Nick Johnson stood in the center of the garage, mid-solo, fingers moving on a pawn-shop guitar held together with electrical tape at the input jack and faith everywhere else. Twenty-five dollars at the Goodwill in Framingham. New strings from Store 24 when he could scrape together four bucks from mowing lawns. The frets were worn smooth in the first position from a year of playing the same progressions until his calluses cracked and bled and hardened into something useful.

Three other kids backed him—Lincoln boys, Maxanne would learn later, from the even-smaller town that shared a high school with Sudbury but not much else. They stopped when they saw her. That teenage terror of being caught caring about something. The music died mid-note like a bird dropping out of flight.

"Don't stop on my account," Maxanne said, settling onto a paint can. "Play it like you mean it."

They traded looks—four boys deciding whether to trust this stranger from the world of actual radio. Nick decided for them. He launched back into "Dream On" with a fury that made the wrenches rattle on their hooks.

"Every time that I look in the mirror," he sang, "all these lines on my face getting clearer..."

His voice cracked on the high notes. Not from poor technique—from carrying too much for seventeen years to hold. The garage walls seemed to lean in. On the pegboard, a claw hammer vibrated against its nail, buzzing at a frequency only the concrete could hear.

For a moment, Nick's eyes drifted to the tools. The music faltered—half a beat, barely noticeable, the way a runner flinches passing the spot where someone fell. Then his fingers found the riff again and drove it home.

When they finished, the silence was the kind that follows truth. The last note hung in the air. Dust motes froze mid-float.

"What's your band called?" Maxanne asked.

Nick shrugged. "Don't have a name yet. We're just... playing."

"You got a demo?"

The other boys looked at Nick. He shook his head. "Can't afford studio time."

Maxanne pulled out her card. WBCN, 104.1 FM. Back when that rectangle of cardstock meant something. "Come by the station. We'll find some time."

Nick hesitated. His eyes moved between the card and the tools on the wall—between what was being offered and what had been taken.

"Why?" he asked. "Why would you help us?"

Maxanne stood, knees protesting. "Because Steven Tyler played his first gig in a garage not much bigger than this one. The only difference between him and you is somebody opened a door."

She paused at the garage door, looking back at four boys holding instruments the way drowning people hold wreckage. She wanted to say more. She didn't. Some things you wreck by explaining.

Walking back to the Karmann Ghia, she heard the music start again behind her. "Sweet Emotion"—that bass line crawling out of the garage and across the frozen yard like something that refused to stay buried. Four boys with nothing to lose. The sound carried across Dutton Road, past the potholed asphalt and the bare trees, toward Hop Brook, where it died against the far bank like every other signal that tried to cross from this side to the other.

Maxanne didn't know it yet, but she was about to meet the other half of the equation. Less than a mile away, on the other side of that brook, a boy sat at a Steinway piano preparing for a recital, trapped in a cage that cost more than Nick Johnson's house.

CHAPTER 2: JACKIE'S WORLD - THE GILDED CAGE

Jackie Marsh woke at 6:47 AM—three minutes before his alarm, the way he always did. His bedroom was the kind of perfect that only happened when adults designed spaces for children they wanted to be proud of. The walls were painted a color his mother called "Dove Gray," the bookshelves held alphabetized volumes he'd actually read, and his piano sat in the corner like a judge waiting to render verdict.

Everything had its place. The tuxedo hung on the closet door, pressed and ready for tonight's recital. Sheet music stacked in performance order on the piano bench—Chopin, Bach, Debussy, Rachmaninoff, and the Mozart that would close the evening with appropriate classical perfection. His guitar—the electric one his father pretended not to know about—stored in its case under his bed, gathering dust.

He sat up and looked at the framed photo on his nightstand: himself at age seven, hands positioned perfectly on piano keys, his mother standing behind him with the kind of smile that suggested she'd finally gotten something right. The photo had won a prize in some parenting magazine—"Classical Excellence: Nurturing Your Child's Musical Gift." Jackie remembered the photographer making him hold the position for twenty minutes while they adjusted the lighting.

Through his bedroom window, he could see toward the other side of Hop Brook—not that he was looking in that direction. Not that he was thinking about someone who used to be his friend before high school taught them they belonged in different worlds. Not that he was remembering garage sessions that felt like a lifetime ago, before everything changed.

Tonight was the recital. Tonight, he would sit in front of his parents' friends and his father's colleagues and play Chopin and Bach and Debussy like he'd been trained to do since he was five years old. Tonight, everyone would applaud politely and tell Dr. Marsh that his son was remarkably talented, and Dr. Marsh would accept the compliments like tributes to his excellent parenting.

And tonight, if he had the courage, Jackie was going to do something that would remind everyone—including himself—that somewhere beneath all this carefully constructed perfection was a kid who still believed Freddie Mercury understood something that Mozart never did.

Even if there was nobody left who would understand why that mattered.

---

The same week Maxanne Sartori discovered Nick Johnson's garage, Dr. Richard Marsh had called the station personally—a rare gesture, since university professors usually delegated such tasks to their secretaries. But Dr. Marsh wanted to ensure Maxanne understood just how significant his son's recital would be.

"Jackie trained at the New England Conservatory prep division," he'd said, his voice carrying the careful pronunciation of someone who'd worked hard to lose whatever accent he'd grown up with. "He's being considered for early admission to Juilliard."

Maxanne had wanted to decline. Classical recitals were not her beat. But something in Dr. Marsh's voice—a father's pride laced with something else, almost fear—compelled her to agree. Maybe it was recognizing that same desperation she'd heard in Grace Johnson's "don't fill his head with dreams we can't afford." Different words, same underlying terror that music might ruin a child's future.

---

By seven o'clock that evening, the Marsh house stood transformed. The living room had become a concert space—folding chairs in perfect rows, a rented Steinway grand piano that cost more than Grace Johnson made in a year, champagne chilling in silver buckets for the afterward. The audience was prep school parents and university faculty, the kind of people who applauded with their fingertips and discussed summer houses on the Cape between selections.

The Marsh house stood on the good side of Hop Brook like it had been placed there by a decorator: Colonial perfection, black shutters against white clapboard, a lawn that would be green come spring. You could walk from Nick Johnson's garage to this front door in twelve minutes, but you'd have to cross Hop Brook—and that narrow stream might as well have been the Atlantic.

Jackie stood in the upstairs bathroom, adjusting his bow tie for the third time. His hands were steady—years of performance training had taught him how to control the physical symptoms of nerves—but his mind was racing. In two hours, he could be back in this bathroom, everyone gone, his parents satisfied that he'd performed exactly as expected. Or he could do what he'd been thinking about for weeks, and everything would change.

The thing about that recital, Maxanne would reflect later in her radio booth, was that it showed her the other half of the tragedy.

---

Jackie Marsh entered the living room at precisely 7:30 PM in a tuxedo that had been tailored to fit him perfectly, seventeen years old but carrying himself like someone who'd been preparing for this moment since birth. He was beautiful in that way that good nutrition and orthodontics and SAT tutors can manufacture. But when he sat at the piano, something changed.

He began with Chopin's Nocturne in E-flat major. His fingers found the keys with practiced precision that spoke of thousands of hours, of lessons that started before memory, of parents who considered anything less than excellence a character flaw. The notes flowed like water, technically perfect, each phrase shaped exactly as his teachers had instructed.

But Maxanne was watching Jackie's face, and she saw something his parents missed: he was dying in there. Behind the technical perfection was a boy playing someone else's definition of music, speaking someone else's language with flawless pronunciation but no soul.

The audience nodded appreciatively, already composing the compliments they'd deliver with their champagne. Dr. Marsh sat in the front row, his expression carefully neutral but his posture radiating satisfaction. This was exactly what he'd paid for.

Jackie moved through the program with mechanical excellence. Bach's Goldberg Variations—excerpts only, because even at a private recital, you had to consider your audience's attention span. Debussy's "Clair de Lune," because someone told him it was romantic. Rachmaninoff's Prelude in C-sharp minor, because difficulty impressed people.

Each piece was perfect. Each piece was empty.

Between the Rachmaninoff and the final Mozart, Jackie paused. The pause stretched longer than it should have. Dr. Marsh cleared his throat—a warning about maintaining proper recital pacing. But Jackie wasn't listening to his father. He was listening to something else.

His fingers found different keys. The opening notes of "Bohemian Rhapsody" emerged from that Steinway like escapees from prison.

The room froze. You could hear pearls clutching themselves.

"Is this the real life?" Jackie sang, his classically trained voice hitting notes that would have made Freddie Mercury proud. "Is this just fantasy?"

His hands moved across the keys with all the technical skill he'd brought to Chopin, but now that precision served something real. The classical training became wings instead of walls. His left hand found a bass line that rumbled through the Steinway's frame and into the hardwood floor. His right hand built harmonies that shouldn't have been possible on a solo piano — Mercury's operatic architecture rendered in ivory and wire and eleven years of lessons that had finally found their purpose.

He sang. His voice, trained for Schubert lieder and careful projection, broke open. The champagne glasses on the sideboard vibrated. Dr. Marsh's water glass hummed against its coaster. Four minutes and fifty-five seconds, and Jackie Marsh wasn't performing for anyone in that room.

When he finished, the silence was different from the holy quiet in Nick's garage. This was the silence of scandal, of proper people trying to process impropriety, of a carefully constructed social order realizing it had just been challenged by a seventeen-year-old in a tuxedo.

Dr. Marsh was the first to speak. "That was... unexpected."

"That was rock and roll," Maxanne said, standing up because someone needed to. "That was your son showing you who he really is."

Jackie looked at her then, really looked, and Maxanne saw the same thing she'd seen in Nick Johnson's eyes just days earlier: a desperate need to be heard as himself, not as someone's projection of who he should be.

"You're Maxanne Sartori," he said. "You play real rock and roll."

Maxanne gave Jackie her card — the same one she'd given Nick three days earlier. "Come by the station. Bring whatever music lives in you."

Jackie took the card. His hands were still shaking. He looked at it, then at Maxanne, then at his father's face — which had rearranged itself into something Jackie had never seen before. Not anger. Not pride. Something older and more complicated, like a man watching his son make a choice he recognized from his own youth. Dr. Marsh's right hand moved to his left wrist and pressed there — an unconscious gesture, the place where calluses had once formed on fingers that used to grip a guitar neck, years before economics replaced music and security replaced risk.

"Thank you," Jackie said. "For understanding."

The other guests murmured. Mrs. Marsh moved through the room offering champagne, deflecting questions, doing what she'd been trained to do when the social order cracked.

Maxanne walked to her car thinking she'd found two boys worth saving. She didn't know she was setting them on a collision course.

CHAPTER 3: THE GEOGRAPHY OF SUDBURY

Two lines divided the town. One ran with water, the other with waste.

Hop Brook came down from Ashland, shallow enough in August to count every stone on the bottom, deep enough in April to swallow a kid's boot and not give it back. In spring the water ran fast over granite ledges, dropping into pools where the sound changed pitch—a bright chatter in the riffles, a lower note where the current dug under root systems and carved out pockets of darkness. Stand still long enough in April and you'd hear the brook's whole register: treble over the stones, bass in the bends, a steady white-noise hiss where it narrowed between the glacial boulders the last ice age had dropped like loose change. By July the brook barely whispered. You had to crouch at the bank to hear it, close enough to smell the mud and the iron tang of wet stone, close enough to see water striders dimpling the surface film in their private, purposeless geometry.

The brook gathered what it could and fed itself south into the Sudbury River near Longfellow's Wayside Inn, where Henry Ford's Grist Mill still turned its wheel, grinding corn for tourists who paid a quarter to watch the millstones work. The wheel creaked on its iron axle. The water that powered it smelled like algae and tannin and the particular green rot of a New England river bottom, and it kept moving south toward Concord, indifferent to what Ford had tried to preserve or purchase.

The other line ran east to west. Twenty-three acres of landfill everyone called The Dump. The official name was Sudbury Sanitary Landfill, but nobody used official names for places that smelled like truth.

On Saturday mornings in March the smell hit you two turns before you made the gate off Route 20. Rotting wood first—wet and fungal, the smell of a cellar nobody opened. Then warm metal from the bulldozer blades baking in pale sun. Then something sweeter and worse underneath, the methane-and-decay perfume that made your throat close and your eyes water if the wind was wrong. Seagulls worked the ridgeline in shifting white clouds, fifty miles inland from the nearest ocean, screaming at each other over the rights to a split garbage bag. Their cries echoed off the fill slopes and mixed with the diesel grumble of the dozers idling at the pit's edge, exhaust braiding with the landfill gases until the air itself felt greasy on your skin and in your lungs.

Where water crossed waste, Sudbury split into four quarters.

Stone walls marked the borders between them. Not the decorative walls from garden catalogs—the real ones. Fieldstone hauled by hand two centuries before, laid without mortar by farmers who'd fought at the Old North Bridge and come home to find their fields still needed clearing. The walls ran through the woods between neighborhoods, half-buried under decades of oak leaves, lichen and moss filling the gaps where frost had shifted the granite apart season by season. You could put your palm flat on one of those walls in August and feel the cold that never left the stone, a cold that went all the way back to the glacier that deposited it. The walls divided the town before the roads did.

The northeast quarter bordered Lincoln, a town Sudbury shared a high school with and nothing else. Working families here. Single-family homes on streets named after trees that had been cut down to build them—Maple, Oak, Elm. The houses smelled like coffee by six and motor oil by seven. Fathers worked construction, double shifts at Raytheon, whatever kept the Buick running another winter. Mothers hung laundry on lines strung between aluminum poles, sheets snapping in the wind that came across the conservation land carrying the faint two-stroke whine of somebody's kid on a dirt bike. The kitchens were small and the screen doors banged shut all summer long. Kids here learned guitar because a used Stratocaster cost forty dollars at the pawn shop on Route 9, and forty dollars was a bet a working family could afford to make on a twelve-year-old boy who wouldn't shut up about Aerosmith.

The northwest quarter was where the Revolutionary War never ended, just settled into real estate. Colonial homes sat on three-acre lots behind those same stone walls, white clapboard and black shutters, brass knockers on doors that cost more than a northeast-quarter car payment. The air here smelled like pine mulch and expensive quiet. Even the dogs didn't bark—they'd been trained out of it. These houses had names, not numbers. The kids didn't learn instruments. They took lessons. Violin from a woman in Concord who'd played with the BSO. Piano from a man who'd studied at Berklee. The assumption in every household was that talent was a commodity, same as the Chippendale chairs and the signed lithographs, something you acquired and displayed.

The southwest quarter lived under the protection of the Sudbury Valley Trustees and zoning laws written to preserve both old-growth oak and old-growth wealth. Professors, doctors, a scattering of lawyers who commuted to Boston on the Pike. Colonial homes with original wide-plank floors that creaked differently in every room—a conversation between the house and the earth beneath it that had been going on for two hundred years. The lots were measured in acres, not feet. The mothers ran the PTA the way their husbands ran departments: agenda, minutes, motions carried, dissent noted and overruled. Kids learned piano here because musical literacy was assumed, folded into the same curriculum as Latin roots and the summer reading list. The houses had names. "Goodnow House." "The Noyes Estate." "Wesslen Farm." Names that tasted like permanence when you said them out loud.

The southeast quarter was where the Cavicchio pig farm sprawled against the landfill's southern edge, one hundred acres of Yorkshires and slop troughs and the circle of life running on a Boston accent. Pigs eating garbage, garbage feeding pigs. The smell when the wind turned south could buckle your knees from a quarter mile. It got into everything—your car upholstery, your jacket lining, the pages of your schoolbooks. Giuseppe Cavicchio had refused to sell his water rights to Henry Ford in 1923, blocking Ford's plan for an auto parts factory near the Inn. Ford called it Italian stubbornness. Giuseppe called it understanding what land is worth. By 1979 the third generation ran the operation with the same granite obstinacy, turning down developer buyouts the way other people turned down second helpings—politely, firmly, and without explanation. The pigs didn't care about property values. Neither did the Cavicchios.

There was a girl who grew up on the southeast edge of that farm, close enough that the pig smell lived in her hair and her jacket and the leather of her school bag. She never apologized for it. Other kids wrinkled their noses on the bus and she just looked at them, level and unhurried, like she was waiting for them to say something worth answering. They never did.

Between the quarters ran the railroad lines that proved connection wasn't the same as closeness. The Massachusetts Central east to west, carrying freight that never stopped in Sudbury. The Framingham and Lowell north to south, parallel to Hop Brook, carrying commuters to Boston. Where the lines crossed at South Sudbury station, engineers called it a diamond crossing—four tracks at right angles, each train required to stop and wait. By 1979 both railroads were dying. The diamond crossing still functioned, but the rails had gone the color of dried blood and the signal box hadn't seen a paintbrush since Nixon resigned.

Saturdays belonged to The Dump.

Families from all four quarters lined up at the gate in their cars. Station wagons from the northwest, waxed and quiet. Pickup trucks from the northeast, rust around the wheel wells. Volvos from the professors' streets. The Cavicchios' flatbed that carried twice the load and trailed the smell of pig like a pennant. The kids stayed in back seats while their fathers—always their fathers, this was 1979—backed up to the pit's edge. Glass in one pile. Paper in another. Everything else into the trench that would be sealed with six inches of fill and forgotten until the EPA decided forgetting wasn't allowed.

At The Dump, nobody's garbage was better than anyone else's.

Between the landfill and the pig farm, a network of dirt trails connected to nothing official and everything that mattered. This was where the mini-bikes ruled. Honda 50s and Yamaha 60s with engines that sounded like angry hornets trapped in coffee cans—a high, furious whine that rose when the throttle opened and dropped to an irritable mutter on the downhills. The exhaust hung in blue ribbons between the pine trunks, sweet and acrid, the taste of burnt oil settling on your tongue. Kids from the northeast quarter rode them in hand-me-down helmets, mud spraying off the back tire in a rooster tail that painted their denim jackets and stung the bare skin at their necks. The throttle vibrated up through your palm and into your wrist until your whole arm buzzed and you couldn't tell where your body ended and the machine began. The ruts were axle-deep in spring and hard as concrete by August. Freedom smelled like two-stroke exhaust and tasted like dirt, and it was the best thing these kids had.

The conservation land between the quarters should have been neutral ground. A hundred acres of pine and oak, old growth that remembered when all of Massachusetts was forest. But the woods kept an honest record.

On the northeast side, dirt-bike trails wound through the trees like varicose veins—ruts cut so deep the grass quit trying. Every weekend the air filled with engine noise and blue smoke, kids finding in speed and mud what their parents couldn't afford to buy them any other way. On the northwest side, those same trails were vandalism. Parents in colonial houses wrote letters to the selectmen about noise ordinances and soil erosion. They posted "No Motorized Vehicles" signs that lasted about a week before someone's kid tore them down and used them for jumps. They talked about property values the way other people talked about God—with absolute conviction, and a little bit of fear.

The town center sat like a colonial film set. White churches. A green where sheep once grazed. The old stocks where Puritan justice embarrassed sin in public. By 1979 the stocks were gone but the instinct wasn't.

And through all of it, Hop Brook kept running. Under the stone walls, past the landfill, through the conservation woods where the mini-bikes couldn't reach, down to the Grist Mill where the wheel still turned and the water still fell over the spillway in a sound like quiet, continuous applause—patient and indifferent, the way water always is when people build walls across it.

CHAPTER 4: THE BELT BUCKLES THAT STARTED EVERYTHING

September 1974. The Ephraim Curtis Middle School cafeteria smelled like steam-table Salisbury steak and floor wax. Metal lunch trays clattered against the long folding tables, and someone had propped a transistor radio on the windowsill by the milk cooler. The radio was playing "Rock the Boat" by the Hues Corporation, and half the sixth graders were bobbing their heads like pigeons.

Jackie Marsh sat alone at the corner table closest to the emergency exit. He held a carton of milk that had been sitting out long enough to feel like bathwater against his palm. His brown hair was cut short above the ears—his mother's instructions to the barber, not his—and his polo shirt was tucked in, pressed, creased along the sleeves like someone had ironed it that morning. Someone had. His sneakers were white. Not dirty-white, not scuffed-white. White-white. The kind of white that announced to every kid in the cafeteria that this boy had not yet been allowed to discover the outdoors.

But the belt. Brown leather, and in the center, a gold script buckle that read QUEEN. He'd ordered it from the back of Circus magazine with three weeks of allowance money, and he wore it like a coat of arms.

Across the cafeteria, Nick Johnson came through the double doors carrying his tray at an angle that suggested he'd never carried anything carefully in his life. He was skinny and fast-looking, the kind of kid who ran everywhere even when walking would've been fine. His black hair hung past his collar—long enough that Mr. Fontaine had already threatened him with a note home. His t-shirt had a small hole near the hem, and his fingernails were black with grime from helping his dad frame a garage the previous Saturday. Dan Johnson believed a boy who could swing a hammer by twelve could build a house by twenty, and Nick believed everything his dad told him.

Nick's belt buckle read AEROSMITH in chrome gothic letters. He'd won it off Tommy Kerrigan in a bet involving a dirt bike, a drainage ditch, and a spectacular lack of judgment.

The radio switched to "Kung Fu Fighting" by Carl Douglas, and three kids at the center table started doing karate chops at each other, singing along in voices that cracked on every other word.

Nick was heading toward the far wall when the cafeteria windows threw sunlight across his buckle, and the reflection caught Jackie square in the eyes. Jackie flinched, raised his forearm. When he lowered it, Nick was standing right there, tray in hand, staring at Jackie's belt.

"Queen?" Nick said. "For real?"

Jackie straightened up. "What about it?"

Nick set his tray down uninvited, scraped back a chair, and sat. He pointed at Jackie's buckle with a fork that still had Salisbury steak on it. "I mean, they're okay. But Aerosmith would eat them alive."

"Eat them—" Jackie's voice jumped. "Steven Tyler can't even sing a whole song without screaming."

"That's the point! That's what makes it good!"

"That's what makes it loud. There's a difference."

Nick grinned. He had a gap between his front teeth that made him look like he was always about to say something funny. "Okay, okay. You ever heard 'Dream On'?"

"Yes."

"And?"

Jackie paused. He picked at the corner of his milk carton. "The piano part at the beginning is decent."

"Decent?" Nick almost knocked over his own tray. "Decent. That's like saying the Grand Canyon is a pretty nice hole."

Jackie bit the inside of his cheek, trying not to smile. "Fine. It's good. The piano part is good. But have you ever actually listened to 'Seven Seas of Rhye'? The harmonies? Brian May plays guitar like—"

"Like what?"

"Like it matters."

Nick chewed on that for a second. He stuck a forkful of something gray-green into his mouth—the cafeteria's version of peas—and talked through it. "Okay, but Joe Perry plays guitar like he's angry at it. Like the guitar did something to him personally and he's getting revenge."

Jackie laughed. It came out louder than he meant it to, and two girls at the next table looked over. He ducked his head, but he was still smiling. He couldn't help it. He'd been at Ephraim Curtis for nine days and this was the first conversation he'd had that wasn't about where the bathroom was.

Behind them, "Kung Fu Fighting" was still going. Everybody was kung fu fighting. A kid named Dennis Mahoney was standing on his chair doing crane kicks while his friends pounded the table.

Nick jerked his thumb toward the radio. "Can we at least agree that this is garbage?"

"Oh, this is absolute garbage."

"'Everybody was kung fu fighting,'" Nick sang in a high falsetto, adding karate chops for emphasis. "'Those cats were fast as lightning.' What does that even mean? Cats aren't fast. My cat sleeps nineteen hours a day."

"It's not about actual cats—"

"I know it's not about actual cats, I'm saying it's stupid. The whole thing is stupid. You know what's gonna happen? Five years from now, nobody's gonna remember any of this disco stuff. Nobody. It'll just be gone."

"My dad says it's a fad," Jackie offered.

"Your dad's smart. What's he do?"

"Teaches at BU. Psychiatry."

Nick nodded like this confirmed something. "My dad builds stuff. Houses, mostly. He says disco is music for people who don't actually like music."

"That's..." Jackie tilted his head. "That's actually exactly right."

Nick beamed. He stuck out his hand across the table, between the milk cartons and the Salisbury steak wreckage. His fingernails were still black. "I'm Nick. Nick Johnson."

Jackie took the hand. His own fingers were clean, the nails trimmed straight across. "Jackie Marsh. We just moved here from Ithaca."

"Where's that?"

"Upstate New York. Near Cornell."

Nick looked blank.

"It's a college."

"I know what Cornell is," Nick said, though his face suggested otherwise. "I mean, is there anything to do there?"

"Not really."

"So it sucks."

"It was fine, actually—"

"No, I mean moving sucks. New school, nobody knows you. Is it weird? Being the new kid?"

Jackie looked around the cafeteria—the kids doing karate chops, the Salisbury steak congealing on a hundred trays, the fluorescent lights buzzing their flat two-note hum, Dennis Mahoney now being wrestled off his chair by his friends. "It was weird until about five minutes ago."

Nick leaned back in his chair, balancing on two legs the way teachers were always telling him not to. "So here's what I'm thinking. You like Queen. I like Aerosmith. We're both right, obviously, because we both have incredible taste. But we need to figure out who's more right."

"That's not how being right works."

"It is now. Here's what we do. You bring me your best Queen record. I'll bring my best Aerosmith record. We listen. Then we argue about it."

"Where?"

"My house. I've got a turntable in the basement. My dad hooked up these speakers he pulled out of the Wayland Cinema when they tore it down. They'll rattle your teeth."

"My mom has a good stereo, but she doesn't let me touch the volume."

"Then we're definitely going to my house."

Jackie looked at Nick's black fingernails, his too-long hair, his shirt with the hole in it. Everything about this kid was the opposite of the boys from Ithaca, the ones whose mothers organized play dates and whose fathers played golf on Saturdays. Nick looked like the kind of kid Jackie's mother would describe as "a handful."

"Tomorrow?" Jackie said.

"Tomorrow." Nick picked up his tray and stood, bumping the table with his hip so both their milk cartons wobbled. "Bring 'Seven Seas of Rhye.' I'm gonna make you listen to 'Same Old Song and Dance' so loud your fillings vibrate."

"I don't have fillings."

"You will when I'm done with you."

The bell rang. Chairs scraped across linoleum. Dennis Mahoney was being told to sit properly by Mrs. Whitaker, who looked like she'd already used up her entire supply of patience and it wasn't even one o'clock. Nick and Jackie walked out through the double doors together, their buckles catching the last of the September sun through the windows.

Nick was already explaining how Joe Perry tuned his guitar, using both hands to demonstrate something, nearly hitting a kid with a clarinet case. Jackie was nodding, already planning which album to bring, already thinking about basement speakers salvaged from a demolished movie theater.

Outside, the air was warm and smelled like cut grass from the athletic fields. Somewhere a lawn mower droned. A yellow bus idled at the far curb, its diesel exhaust mixing with the September air. They had math next, in different classrooms, but Nick said he'd meet Jackie by the bike rack after school. He said this like it was already decided, like they'd been doing it for years instead of twenty minutes.

Jackie said okay. He was already thinking about which album to bring first, and whether Nick's dad's movie theater speakers could really rattle your teeth, and whether he could convince his mother that this kid with the dirty fingernails and the hole in his shirt was someone worth knowing.

He could. He would.

CHAPTER 5: BEST FRIENDS (MIDDLE SCHOOL)

It started with cassette tapes.

Jackie would bring Queen albums recorded from his father's stereo system — bootleg copies of A Night at the Opera and News of the World dubbed onto TDK cassettes with handwritten labels in Jackie's careful block print. Nick would bring Aerosmith tapes borrowed from older kids on Dutton Road, the lyrics to "Dream On" and "Sweet Emotion" copied in ballpoint pen on notebook paper during study hall when he was supposed to be learning algebra.

They'd meet in the school library during lunch, one set of headphones split between them, each kid pressing his ear to the foam pad. Mrs. Stephens would shush them every few minutes, but she never kicked them out.

"Listen to this harmony," Jackie would say, rewinding "Bohemian Rhapsody" for the hundredth time. "Freddie Mercury is doing things with his voice that should not be physically possible."

"Maybe," Nick would counter, "but listen to Tyler's scream right here in 'Dream On.' That's not singing. That's a guy bleeding his entire soul into a microphone."

"That's precisely the difference. Mercury controls it. Tyler just — throws himself off a cliff."

"Yeah," Nick said. "And that's why it's better."

Jackie rewound the tape again. Nick let him.

By spring of 1975 they'd found the conservation land that ran behind both their neighborhoods — hundreds of acres of pine and scrub oak stretching from the wrong side of Hop Brook clear through to the good side, the kind of territory that didn't belong to either world. Nick's bike was a hand-me-down Schwinn salvaged from The Dump and spray-painted black, the chain popping off every quarter mile. Jackie's ten-speed Raleigh was shiny and completely wrong for dirt trails — the thin tires slipped on roots and the derailleur caught on every rock. He wiped out twice the first day and came back the next morning anyway.

The trails were narrow, packed dirt threaded between white pines that dropped needles thick as carpet. Their tires hissed through the soft brown mat and kicked up the smell of warm resin and turned earth. On the steep downhills the bikes rattled so hard their handlebars buzzed and the spokes sang a high, tinny note like a fork struck against a glass. In the deeper woods, where the canopy closed overhead, the light came through green and underwater-quiet. Woodpeckers worked the dead oaks in short machine-gun bursts. Chipmunks bolted across the trail a wheel-length ahead. The air smelled different here — not like either of their neighborhoods, not like cut lawns or car exhaust or the cooking grease that drifted from the Friendly's kitchen. It smelled like cold dirt and bark and the particular sweetness of pine sap bleeding from a fresh cut, and it belonged to nobody.

They found swimming holes that weren't on any map. The first one was a quarry cut half a mile past the power lines, where the bedrock dropped straight into black water. The cold hit like voltage — it locked your chest, squeezed your ribs, turned your fingers white. You'd come up gasping with your jaw clamped shut and the sky looked bluer than it had any right to look and every sound was louder, sharper, the way the world gets when your body remembers it's alive. They'd pull themselves onto the granite ledge afterward and sit with their feet dangling, water running off their shorts, watching the surface go still again while the sun baked their shoulders dry. Dragonflies stitched the air above the water in electric-blue streaks.

Some days they didn't even swim. They'd just ride — miles of trail, the bikes ticking beneath them, the woods opening and closing like rooms in a house with no walls. They built a fort out of fallen branches near the old stone wall that marked the property line between the conservation land and the Wayside Inn meadows. It wasn't much — a lean-to with a pine-bough roof that leaked when it rained — but it was theirs.

The first time Jackie came to dinner at the Johnson house, Grace watched him from the kitchen doorway. She'd heard about the Marsh family from the other mothers — the psychiatrist at Boston University, the house on Concord Road, the kind of people who didn't usually sit at tables with mismatched chairs and bills spread on the counter.

But Jackie took the peanut butter sandwich she offered and said "Thank you, Mrs. Johnson," and ate it like it was the best thing he'd had all week. When he finished, he stood up and carried his plate to the sink without being asked. Grace's hand stopped on the dish towel. She knew that move. Not from Sudbury, not from any family on the good side of Hop Brook. That was how she'd been raised — her mother's kitchen, clearing plates before you were told, because helping was what you did when someone fed you. She hadn't expected to see it in a boy whose father drove a new Buick and taught at a university.

Something in Jackie's careful manners — the way he held the plate with both hands, the way he waited until she took it — tugged at a memory Grace hadn't visited in years. Her own mother's dining room table, the good china reserved for Sunday dinners, the way her mother had stood behind her chair and guided her hands: napkin in the lap, elbows off the table, always say please and thank you. Her mother had grown up in a house with a name too, out in Wellesley, before she'd married a man who worked with his hands and moved to a street without sidewalks. Grace had learned her manners in a kitchen that smelled like lavender and lemon polish, and she'd carried them to a kitchen that smelled like dish soap and meatloaf and mortgage worry. She hadn't expected to recognize those same manners in a boy from Concord Road — but she did, and the recognition sat in her chest like a stone.

"He's a good kid," she told Nick later. "Different. But good."

The next Saturday, Dan Johnson came home early from the job site. He pushed through the garage door still in his work boots, concrete dust on his jeans, a socket wrench in his back pocket he'd forgotten to put away. He found Nick and Jackie sitting on overturned milk crates, guitars across their laps.

"This the friend?" Dan said.

"Jackie Marsh," Jackie said, standing up and extending his hand.

Dan looked at the outstretched hand, then shook it. His grip swallowed Jackie's fingers. "You play?"

"Yes, sir. Guitar and piano."

"Piano." Dan nodded, like this confirmed something. "Play something."

Jackie glanced at Nick. Nick shrugged — go ahead.

Jackie played the opening arpeggio of "Bohemian Rhapsody" fingerpicked on the acoustic, the notes climbing in that strange, sad way, each one placed like he was setting stones in a wall.

Dan listened with his arms crossed, head tilted. When Jackie finished the intro, Dan said, "That's real pretty," and walked into the house. Through the screen door they heard him call out: "Grace, the kid can play."

The test came in October. Doug Malone had been watching Jackie for weeks — a big kid who made up for bad grades with his fists and a talent for finding people alone. He caught Jackie between third and fourth period, near the lockers where the fluorescent lights buzzed and the hallway smelled like floor wax and sweat.

"Hey, rich boy." Doug stepped in close. "You lost? Your people hang out on the other side."

Jackie looked up. His hands were damp but his voice was steady. "I'm not lost."

"Sure looks like it. Professor daddy, house on Concord Road. What are you doing over here?"

Nick appeared beside Jackie. He'd come around the corner at a half-jog and slowed to a walk, hands loose at his sides.

"Problem?" Nick said.

Doug looked at him. Nick was smaller, wiry where Doug was thick, but he stood like a kid who'd been hit before and was already past the part where it scared him.

"Nothing to do with you, Johnson. This is between me and the rich kid."

"Funny," Nick said. "Because that rich kid's with me. So now it's got everything to do with me."

The hallway had gone quiet. Other kids stopped walking and pressed themselves against lockers, watching.

Doug looked from Nick to Jackie and back. He was trying to do the math — working-class kid defending a kid from Concord Road — and the numbers didn't add up.

"Whatever." Doug stepped back. "Not worth it."

He walked away. The hallway stayed quiet for another three seconds, and then everybody moved again, like a clock that had paused and restarted.

Jackie and Nick stood there.

"You didn't have to do that," Jackie said.

"Yeah, I did," Nick said. Then, grinning: "Besides, if Malone punched you, you'd probably critique his form. 'That was a C-minus right hook, Douglas, really substandard wrist alignment.'"

Jackie laughed — a real laugh, the kind that unlocked something in his chest. "I would not have said that."

"You absolutely would have."

They walked to class together. Neither of them mentioned it again.

That weekend they were back in the garage, Dan's tools hanging in neat rows on the pegboard behind them — wrenches arranged by size, screwdrivers slotted into a wooden rack he'd built himself, the socket set open on the workbench like a surgical kit. The October light came through the single window in a yellow slant and lit up the dust that floated between them.

Nick strummed the opening to "Sweet Emotion" — that low, dirty, swinging riff, all bass string and attitude. The notes bounced off the concrete floor and the cinder-block walls and came back fatter, meaner. He played it again, letting the groove settle, his right hand loose on the downstroke.

"That's half-decent," Jackie said.

"Half-decent. Okay, Liberace. Show me what you got."

Jackie repositioned his fingers and played the piano intro to "Bohemian Rhapsody" transposed for guitar — the descending chords, Bb to Gm, that cathedral-ceiling sound, each chord ringing into the next like bells falling down a staircase. On the acoustic, without distortion, the harmonics bloomed in the cold garage air. It sounded ancient and enormous, the kind of thing monks might play if monks had Gibsons.

"Holy shit," Nick said. "That's operatic."

"Language, Nicholas."

"That's freaking operatic. Better?"

Jackie was smiling. "Marginally."

Nick looked at his guitar. Then at Jackie. Then back at his guitar. "What if we played them at the same time?"

"They're in completely different keys."

"So we change the key."

Jackie thought about it. He shifted the Bohemian Rhapsody progression, found a voicing that sat in the same neighborhood as Nick's riff. "Try it now. Start the riff."

Nick kicked off "Sweet Emotion" again — that swaggering, sleazy groove. Jackie came in over the top with the Bohemian Rhapsody chords, and for a second it was a car crash, two songs fighting each other in a twelve-by-fifteen-foot box. But then Jackie adjusted his timing, laid his chords across the gaps in Nick's riff instead of on top of them, and the sound locked in. The riff gave the chords a pulse. The chords gave the riff a sky. Nick's guitar was the engine; Jackie's was the wingspan. The low notes rumbled through the milk crates and the high notes rang against the hand tools and the whole garage vibrated like the inside of a drum.

They played it through twice, figuring out where to breathe, where to push, where to let the other one lead. By the third pass, Nick started singing — "Sweet emotion" — in his rough, throaty voice, and Jackie answered with "Is this the real life" in a clear tenor that cut through the distortion and the echo and the dust. The two vocal lines braided together and pulled apart and braided again, and the sound was bigger than two thirteen-year-olds on milk crates in a one-car garage in Sudbury, Massachusetts, had any right to make.

When they stopped, the silence was total. A wrench on the pegboard was still humming.

Nick was breathing hard. Jackie's fingers were shaking.

"We should start a band," Nick said.

"What would we call it?"

"Aero Queen."

Jackie laughed. "That's terrible."

"Queen Smith?"

"Worse."

They sat there grinning at each other in the yellow light, two boys from different sides of a brook that divided everything in their town except this garage, this sound, this one thing they'd built out of a pawn-shop Fender and a birthday Raleigh and two hundred hours of arguing over headphones in a school library.

Outside, the October wind pushed dead leaves across the driveway. Inside, the wrench stopped humming. From the kitchen, through the screen door, they could hear Grace running water and Dan's work boots crossing the floor, and the small ordinary sounds of a house that was still whole.

CHAPTER 6: JACKIE'S WORLD

The thermostat in the Marsh dining room was set to sixty-eight degrees. Always sixty-eight degrees. Jackie had checked once with a thermometer from his chemistry set, expecting a degree or two of drift, some small proof that nature still had a say in things. Sixty-eight point zero. His father's house did not permit variation.

The breakfast table was set the way it was set every morning — forks one inch from the plate edge, knives turned inward, cloth napkins folded into identical rectangles. Jackie knew the distances because he'd measured them on a dare from Elizabeth. She'd bet him a dollar their mother used a ruler. She was right. Not a ruler exactly — habit so deep it had become precision, years of Eileen Marsh setting this table until her hands could find the spacing the way a pianist finds an octave.

Dr. Richard Marsh sat at the head of the table, reading the Boston Globe. He held the paper with both hands, the way he held everything — deliberately, with the posture of a man who had taught himself to take up exactly the right amount of space. He was forty-five, lean from discipline rather than exercise. His hands were what you noticed second, after the posture. Large, strong, with calluses on the left fingertips that had nothing to do with psychiatry. The calluses were old — the ghost of something he'd stopped doing years ago but that his skin hadn't entirely forgotten.

Jackie sat across from him, thirteen and already trained in the architecture of this room. Under the tablecloth, his fingers were moving — silent scales across an invisible keyboard, the notes playing only inside his head.

"Juilliard's pre-college program wrote back," Dr. Marsh said without looking up. "They want to schedule your audition."

Jackie's fingers stopped. "That's — yeah. Great."

"We should discuss your repertoire this weekend. Your Chopin needs another month, but the Bach is ready."

"Sure." Jackie paused. "I've also been working on some other things."

Dr. Marsh turned a page. The sound was precise as a metronome.

Eileen Marsh came in carrying toast cut into triangles — always triangles, because once, years ago, someone had decided that was the shape toast should be in this house, and no one had revisited the decision since. She poured orange juice into crystal glasses, her movements smooth and practiced, and sat down with her napkin across her lap and pearls at her throat at seven-thirty in the morning.

"Different how?" Dr. Marsh asked, still not looking up.

"Queen's arrangements. The way Mercury layers vocals over — "

"Jackie." Just his name. But the way his father said it carried the weight of a full sentence: We've discussed this.

"Rock music is a phase," Dr. Marsh said, folding the newspaper and setting it beside his plate, aligned with the table edge. "You have a genuine gift. I won't watch you waste it."

There was a tightness in his jaw. Jackie had seen it before — not anger, but something underneath anger that looked almost like pain. His father's left hand moved to the table, and the callused fingertips pressed against the wood as if reaching for a fretboard that wasn't there.

"Richard," Eileen said. Quiet. A single word that meant: not at breakfast.

Jackie looked at his mother. She was studying her orange juice with the focus of someone who had learned, over many years, exactly when to speak and when to let silence work. Eileen knew things. She knew about the guitar case in the attic crawl space behind the Christmas decorations, the one his father never mentioned, the one Jackie had found when he was ten and never asked about because even at ten he understood that some doors in this house stayed closed.

"Your father wants what's best," Eileen said, her voice carrying the particular gentleness of a line she'd delivered so many times it had worn smooth.

From upstairs, a violin cut through the silence. Not careful classical scales — this was different. A melody that started with Vivaldi's precision and bent sideways into something warmer, looser. Elizabeth was up there improvising again, finding the cracks between the notes on the page and living in them.

Dr. Marsh glanced at the ceiling but said nothing. Elizabeth's gift was wilder, less controllable, and because it came so effortlessly he seemed to regard it the way you regard weather. You couldn't schedule weather. So he let it be.

"May I be excused?" Jackie asked.

"You have forty minutes before school."

"I want to practice."

His father nodded. Because practice, in this house, was always the right answer.

---

The Steinway grand piano filled a third of Jackie's bedroom. His father had bought it when Jackie was seven, the year his conservatory teacher said he had "remarkable hands." It was tuned every three months by a man who drove up from Boston. The piano was worth more than some of the houses on the other side of Hop Brook.

Jackie closed his door. Locked it — the small rebellion of a turned bolt.

He sat at the bench and let his hands rest on the keys without pressing them. He started with Bach. The Prelude in C Major — his father's favorite, the piece Dr. Marsh sometimes paused outside the door to listen to, his shadow visible through the gap at the floor. Jackie played it perfectly because after six years of training, the notes lived in his tendons. The Bach was beautiful the way this house was beautiful. Measured. Controlled. Every note in its place.

Then he stopped.

He listened. His father's car starting in the driveway. The garage door closing. The house settling into the particular silence it held when Dr. Marsh left — a silence that felt like an exhale, like the walls themselves relaxing by half a degree.

Jackie's hands found different keys.

The opening of "Bohemian Rhapsody" — played soft, so soft the notes barely carried past the bedroom walls. The melody ghosted out of the piano like something trapped inside it, waiting for the house to empty. He kept the sustain pedal barely pressed, dampening the resonance, muting the overtones that would carry through the floor.

He played the ballad section with his eyes closed. His left hand found the bass line — those dark, descending chords that Mercury had borrowed from opera and made into something new. His right hand carried the melody, and he let it ache, let it reach for notes that weren't in any of his conservatory books. He wanted to open the lid all the way, wanted to feel the hammers hit the strings with the full weight of what he was feeling, wanted the sound to fill every room of this sixty-eight-degree house until the walls cracked.

But he played quiet. He'd learned to play quiet the way prisoners learn to speak in code — out of necessity, not choice.

When he reached the operatic section, he sang. Barely above a whisper, Galileo Galileo rising up inside his chest and dying in his throat because volume was the one thing this house could not tolerate. The headboard section — thunderbolts and lightning — he played with his fingers close to the keys, pulling the sound inward, and even restrained it was the most alive thing in the room. More alive than the perfectly made bed. More alive than the alphabetized bookshelf. More alive than the framed conservatory certificates on the wall.

He ended soft, the way Mercury ended it — nothing really matters — and held the last chord until the strings stopped vibrating on their own.

A knock on his door. Jackie flinched, pulled his hands off the keys.

"That was really pretty." Elizabeth's voice, muffled through the door.

He unlocked it. She was leaning against the frame, violin case in one hand, wearing a Fleetwood Mac t-shirt their mother pretended not to see.

"You heard that?"

"The whole thing. You know he's gone, right? You could've played it louder."

"I know."

Elizabeth looked at him like she was reading the back of a book he thought was closed. "You practice Queen like you're hiding a body."

"Liz — "

"I'm just saying. You don't have to whisper when nobody's listening." She shifted her violin case. "I figured out how to get that sliding guitar sound on 'Don't Stop Me Now' with vibrato and an open D. Want to try it together after school?"

"Dad gets home at six."

"So we stop at five-thirty."

She said it like it was the simplest thing in the world. Like scheduling your joy around your father's commute was something every kid did.

Jackie almost told her about the guitar case in the attic, about the calluses on their father's fingers, about the way Dr. Marsh's voice cracked on the word waste this morning like a word he'd said to himself first, years ago, and never recovered from. But Elizabeth was thirteen and already carrying enough of this house's weight.

"Yeah," Jackie said. "After school. Five-thirty."

Elizabeth grinned — too wide, too bright, too much like someone who hadn't yet learned to measure her joy against the expectations of the room. "Cool," she said, and disappeared down the hall.

Jackie closed the door. Put his hands back on the keys and found the opening chord of the Chopin Ballade his father wanted for the audition. His posture straightened. His fingers found their trained positions.

But under the first phrase of the Chopin — just for a measure, just long enough to feel it — his left hand played the bass line from "Bohemian Rhapsody."

Then it was gone. Just the Chopin. Just the practice. Just the sound his father wanted to hear.

That night, the family sat at dinner — same measured forks, same sixty-eight degrees, same crystal throwing chandelier light into scattered prisms on the tablecloth. Eileen had made roast chicken with precisely quartered potatoes. The salt and pepper stood at the exact center of the table, equidistant from all four settings.

"The audition is in six weeks," Dr. Marsh said, cutting his chicken in small, even pieces. "Professor Aldridge at the conservatory will do a mock audition Saturday."

"Okay."

Dr. Marsh set down his knife and fork. He looked at Jackie — really looked — and the mask slipped. What was underneath was not authority or anger but something rawer. Something that looked, in the chandelier light, like grief.

"I had chances," he said. His voice was lower than usual. Eileen stopped chewing. Elizabeth looked up. "When I was your age. Different chances, but chances. And I made the practical choice because that's what you do when you come from where I came from." He paused. "But I want you to have the path I couldn't see. The one that leads somewhere extraordinary. Not just somewhere safe."

The grandfather clock ticked in the hallway. Steady, mechanical, never fast, never slow.

"I hear you, Dad," Jackie said. And he meant it. He loved his father. He loved his father's hands and his father's discipline and even his father's need for everything to be sixty-eight degrees. He just couldn't live there.

Elizabeth reached under the table and squeezed Jackie's hand. Quick, hard, gone — before anyone could see.

"Eileen, this chicken is excellent," Dr. Marsh said, and picked up his fork, and the conversation was over the way conversations in this house were always over — not with resolution but with redirection.

Eileen smiled her careful smile. "Thank you, Richard."

And dinner continued. Sixty-eight degrees. Forks one inch from the plate. Everything in its place.

Later, Jackie sat in the dark. He did not turn on the piano. He listened to the house — his mother downstairs with the radio tuned to the classical station because that was the station this house played. His father's study, the murmur of pages. Elizabeth's violin through the wall, something slow and sad that might have been Barber or might have been her own invention — with Elizabeth you could never tell.

He thought about Nick Johnson. About that garage on Dutton Road where the concrete floor was cracked and the tools hung on pegboards and nobody measured the distance between anything. Where you could play as loud as you wanted and the only rule was that it had to be real.

He thought about the guitar case in the attic. About his father's callused fingers on the newspaper. About what it cost a man to build a life this precise — and whether the cost was the kind you paid once or the kind that kept collecting, interest compounding in silence.

Classical earned his father's approval. Rock earned his breath. He couldn't have both.

Jackie lay back on his perfectly made bed and let that truth sit in his chest like a chord that wouldn't resolve.

Five-thirty, he thought. We stop at five-thirty. And the part of him that was already learning to hide — the part that played Queen at a whisper and loved Freddie Mercury in secret and measured his joy against his father's commute — that part closed its eyes and began to count the hours.

CHAPTER 7: NICK'S WORLD

CHAPTER 9: THE TALENT SHOW

This was the golden moment. This was the high point before the fall. This was the night when Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh stopped being just two kids who played music together and became something that would eventually scare the hell out of everyone who thought they understood how the world was supposed to work.

And that's when the trouble started. That's when expectations began to rise. That's when the golden moment became the weight that would eventually crush them both.

CHAPTER 9A: MAXANNE'S DISCOVERY

Three hours and nineteen minutes.

The talent show story settles over the booth like the last note of a song everyone's afraid to follow. Two thirteen-year-olds in a gymnasium, harmonizing like they'd stumbled onto the frequency the rest of us spend our lives scanning for. Beautiful. Gone.

I take a pull of the Pappy. Let the burn settle.

"You want to know how I got here?" I say into the microphone. "Not the booth. Not WBCN. I mean here — the woman telling you about two kids whose dreams she helped kill. You want to understand how a person goes from rebel to executioner in six years? You've got to go back further than Sudbury. Further than 1979. You've got to go all the way back to a bar in Allston that smelled like spilled beer and ambition, and a twenty-three-year-old who was stupid enough to believe that talent and conviction were the same thing."

I spin the chair toward the album wall. Third row, left side. My hand finds it without looking — Aerosmith's debut, the blue wings on cream, January '73. The vinyl's in pristine condition because I never let anyone else touch it. Some things you protect whether they need protecting or not.

"1973. I was twenty-three years old. And I was about to find the real thing for the first time."

The memory takes me like a riptide.

---

It was a Tuesday in January. The kind of cold that makes your teeth hurt and your breath hang in the air like tiny ghosts. I drove my Dodge Dart down Commonwealth Ave — heater worked when it felt like it, which was almost never — heading to a bar on Brighton Ave called the Brickyard because a sound engineer named Paulie DeLuca had cornered me at the station that afternoon and grabbed my arm.

"Maxanne, there's a band playing the Brickyard tonight. You need to hear them."

"What kind of band?"

"The singer moves like he's trying to shake demons off his skin. The guitar player looks like he'd kill you and not lose sleep over it. They sound like what you'd get if the Stones and the Yardbirds had a kid and raised it behind a dumpster."

Paulie had seen ten thousand bands and been impressed by maybe six. So I went.

The Brickyard was the kind of bar that exists in every city's rock and roll mythology — the place where legends played before they were legends, when they were just kids with cheap equipment and something to prove. It sat between a laundromat and a sub shop, a narrow room with a ceiling so low the tall guys hunched, a stage the size of a dining room table, and a sound system that had been state-of-the-art around the Eisenhower administration. The floor stuck to your shoes. The bathroom door didn't lock. The neon Budweiser sign in the window buzzed like a dying insect, and the whole place smelled like hops and sweat and the particular desperation of musicians who haven't eaten since yesterday.

On a Tuesday night in January, the room held maybe twenty people. Half of them teenagers with fake IDs and nowhere warmer to go. A woman at the bar read a paperback, unbothered by anything. The bartender — a thick-necked guy named Sal — wiped glasses with a rag dirtier than the glasses.

Then five guys walked onto that postage-stamp stage, and the air in the room changed.

I noticed the singer first. You couldn't not notice him. Twenty-four, rail-thin, with lips that belonged on a different species and hair that looked like it had been in a fight with a light socket and lost. He wrapped his hands around the microphone stand like he was hanging onto the only thing between him and the floor. Didn't say hello. Didn't introduce the band. Didn't do any of the usual patter that bar bands use to fill the silence between being nobody and becoming something.

He looked at the guitar player — dark-haired, cheekbones sharp enough to cut glass, eyes that said he'd already seen things he wasn't going to share with you — and nodded.

The guitar player hit the opening riff.

I'm not going to describe what happened in that room without sounding like a tent-revival preacher, and I've spent my whole career trying not to sound like that. So I'll just say this: the sound that came off that stage was alive. Not performed. Not rehearsed into safety. Not polished until the edges couldn't cut you. Alive the way a downed power line is alive — dangerous, electric, throwing sparks into the night and not caring who gets burned.

Steven Tyler sang like a man who'd swallowed fire and was trying to cough up something beautiful before it consumed him. Joe Perry played guitar like each note was a personal argument he intended to win. The bass player laid down grooves that expensive producers would spend thousands trying to replicate in studios. The drummer kept time like a heartbeat in a chest that was running out of reasons to keep going.

They played for forty-five minutes. Four people left during the set — the woman with the book, a couple who'd been fighting since I walked in, and some kid who probably had a curfew. The rest of us stood with our mouths open like we'd forgotten how jaws worked.

I had my portable Panasonic cassette recorder in my bag. I carried one everywhere — that's who I'd decided to be the day I talked my way into WBCN and swore I'd never let a sound I believed in go unrecorded. The tape quality was garbage. Tinny, distorted, the Brickyard's terrible acoustics making everything sound like it was being played inside a coffee can. But underneath the noise, you could hear it. The frequency. The signal underneath the static that said: This is real. This is the real shit. This is what you've been scanning the dial for.

After the set, I walked up to the singer. He leaned against the wall by the emergency exit, smoking a Camel, his T-shirt soaked through with sweat. Up close, he was taller than I'd expected and thinner than should have been legal.

"Maxanne Sartori. WBCN."

He looked at me like I'd told him I was from Neptune. "The radio station?"

"The one where I'm going to play your album tomorrow night."

He stared. Then he smiled — this enormous, disbelieving, full-body grin that transformed his entire face from dangerous to completely, heartbreakingly young. "We have an album. Nobody plays it."

"I'm going to play it."

"You're going to play it."

"On WBCN. Tomorrow. Eleven o'clock. If I can find a copy."

He reached into a duffel bag and handed me a record like it was a holy relic. Which, in a way, it was. "Dream On," he said. "If you're only going to play one, play that."

I took the album and walked out into the January cold, and the air tasted different. Cleaner. Like the frequency had been adjusted and I was hearing the world properly for the first time in my life.

---

The next night I walked into WBCN at ten-fifteen. Charlie Morris, the station manager, was still in his office — unusual for Charlie, who believed the night shift ran itself or it didn't deserve to exist. He had his feet on the desk and a joint between his fingers and looked up at me with the tolerant suspicion of a man who'd built his radio station on the principle that rules were suggestions written by people who didn't understand music.

"You've got that look," he said.

"I found something."

"You always find something."

I put the Aerosmith album on his desk. "Track three."

He studied the cover. Studied me. Dropped the needle.

For four and a half minutes, neither of us breathed. When the piano came in, Charlie closed his eyes. When Steven Tyler's voice hit that open-throated scream — the one that sounds like someone tearing their own heart out and holding it up to the light to see if it's still beating — Charlie opened his eyes and looked at me like I'd just told him where they buried the treasure.

"Where'd you find this?"

"A bar in Allston. Tuesday night. Twenty people in the room."

Charlie picked up the album. Turned it over. Set it down and pointed at me with the hand holding the joint, ash dropping onto the desk. "This is what we DO, Maxanne. Find the real shit. Play it tonight. Eleven. Full volume."

I didn't need to be told twice.

Eleven o'clock, I dropped the needle on "Dream On" without announcing it, without context, without checking with the program director who'd left at six. Just put it on the air and let it breathe. Steven Tyler's voice pouring out of radios across greater Boston — into dorm rooms at BU and Northeastern, into apartments in Cambridge and Somerville, into cars on the Pike, into a city that didn't know yet what it was hearing.

The request line lit up before the song ended. Every light on the board, blinking like a switchboard plugged into something too powerful for its wiring. I played it again at midnight. Twice more before my shift ended at two. By Wednesday afternoon, kids walked into record stores asking for the album by name. By Friday, it had sold more copies in Boston than in the rest of the country combined.

That was discovery. Not a marketing campaign. Not a demographic study. Not a focus group. A woman with a cassette recorder in a half-empty bar, hearing something true and having the nerve to share it with a city that needed it.

---

Three months later, a kid walked into Studio C for his first radio interview. Twenty-three, skinny as a parking meter, wearing a leather jacket that had seen better decades and a bandana that looked like he'd stolen it from a gas station mechanic. He sat down across from the microphone and his hands shook — not nerves, exactly, but the vibration of a body that contains more energy than it knows what to do with.

"You're Bruce Springsteen," I said.

"That's what they tell me."

"From New Jersey."

"Asbury Park. Born to be anywhere else." He grinned. Wild. Unpracticed. The grin of someone who hasn't yet learned to perform happiness.

I leaned into the mic. "WBCN, 104.1. I'm sitting here with Bruce Springsteen, who just released his debut album, 'Greetings from Asbury Park, N.J.' Bruce, welcome to Boston."

And this kid — this unknown, unpolished, absolutely electric kid from the Jersey Shore — leaned into the microphone and said, with the kind of pure, unguarded joy that fame will burn out of you before you ever learn to protect it:

"This is my first time on the radio. My mom lives out in California. HI MOM!"

The control room cracked up. I cracked up. And somewhere out in California, a woman probably pressed her hand to her mouth and understood that her son was going to be all right.

That was 1973. The year I found Aerosmith in a bar in Allston and Springsteen walked in off the street with nothing but a guitar and his mother's love and the kind of hunger that turns ordinary people into legends. The year WBCN was exactly what a radio station is supposed to be — a frequency that finds what's real and broadcasts it at full volume to anyone brave enough to tune in.

---

I open my eyes. 2009. Studio B. The ON AIR sign still pulsing its cardiac red.

The album wall stares back at me. Aerosmith. The Cars. Boston. J. Geils. All discovered within spitting distance of this booth. All given their first breath of airtime by someone who believed that finding real music and putting it on the air mattered more than anything else in the world.

"That was what we were," I say into the mic. My voice sounds old to me now. When did that happen? "WBCN. We didn't market. We discovered. We didn't focus-group a goddamn thing. We listened. We put our ears to the ground and when the ground shook, we told people about it. And the ground shook often enough that we were right more than we were wrong, and the times we were right — the Aerosmith nights, the Springsteen mornings — those were the times that justified everything. Every bad call, every sleepless night, every screaming match with management about playlist freedom. Because we'd found the frequency. We knew what real sounded like."

I pick up the demo tape. Turn it in my hands. "Demo - Nick/Jackie - un-Authorized - March 1979."

"But that was 1973. Six years later, things had changed. I had changed. The woman who walked into a bar in Allston and recorded a band on a Panasonic cassette because she believed the world needed to hear them — that woman was still inside me somewhere. Buried under six years of learning how the business really worked. Six years of watching the frequency get jammed by consultants and demographic reports and suits who thought music was a product you could optimize rather than a wild animal you could only hope to set free."

I set the tape down. Gently.

"In 1973, I found Aerosmith and my first instinct was to share them with the world. In 1979, I found Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh and my first instinct was to figure out how to use them. I told myself I was still discovering. I told myself the Battle of the Bands was about finding talent. But here's what thirty years of insomnia have taught me: you can lie to management, you can lie to your audience, you can even lie to two seventeen-year-old kids who trust you because you're the woman who broke Aerosmith. The one person you can't lie to forever is the woman staring back at you from the dark window of a radio booth at midnight."

The silence stretches. Outside, a bus pulls away from the curb on Boylston, its taillights red as the ON AIR sign.

"Six years. That's how long it took to go from rebel to marketer. Six years from dropping 'Dream On' unauthorized at full volume to designing a Battle of the Bands with corporate sponsors and demographic targets and a tie clause I buried in the fine print because I thought I could engineer reconciliation the way I'd once engineered discovery."

I lean back. The chair creaks like old leather does — tired, holding on.

"But we're getting ahead of ourselves. Before the Battle, before the rules I wrote in the dark, before the meeting where everything turned — first you need to hear what happened to Nick and Jackie after that talent show. How the golden moment tarnished. How America's favorite sport — dividing people who belong together — took two kids with a standing ovation and ground them down to strangers passing each other in high school hallways without a word."

I cue "Dream On." The album cut. The piano intro that still sounds like someone praying in a church they're not sure they believe in.

"Because you can't understand the war until you remember the peace. And the peace between those boys was the most real thing I've encountered in fifty-six years of rock and roll. What happened to it — what we let happen, what I helped happen — is the reason I'm sitting in this booth, burning the last of my borrowed time."

I press play. Steven Tyler's voice fills Studio B, fills the airwaves, fills whatever's left of a city that once believed a sound from a dive bar could change the world.

I close my eyes and let the music say what I can't.

CHAPTER 10: SUMMER CAMP

The bus to Camp Elbanobscot smelled like diesel fuel and adolescent anticipation. Nick Johnson sat next to Jackie Marsh, both of them holding their acoustic guitars in their laps, wondering what the hell they were getting themselves into.

"Think anyone else will know about the talent show?" Nick asked.

Jackie shrugged, but Nick could see the same nervousness in his friend's eyes. "Probably not. It's not like we're famous or anything."

The bus wound through mountain roads that seemed to climb away from everything familiar—away from Sudbury's invisible class lines, away from the expectations that came with last names and addresses, away from the subtle pressures that had begun to shape their friendship into something more complicated than it had been in the garage.

Camp Elbanobscot looked exactly like the brochures had promised: rustic cabins that smelled like pine sap, a dining hall where food fights were inevitable, and a music building where kids who understood that music mattered could find their tribe.

The camp director, a wiry man named Mr. Freedman who looked like he'd been teaching music since before rock and roll was invented, greeted them with enthusiasm.

"Welcome to Cabin 7," he said, leading them toward a wooden structure that looked like it had been built by someone who understood that summer magic required four walls and a roof, but not much else. "Music intensive. You'll be sharing with six other kids who think music is more important than sleep."

The cabin smelled like wood shavings and teenage boy. Nick and Jackie claimed bunks near each other, their guitars resting against the wall.

That first evening, as the sun set over the lake and the sounds of other kids settling into their own cabins drifted through the open windows, Nick found himself talking about things he'd never discussed with anyone outside his family.

"My dad used to say that music was the one thing that didn't care where you came from," he said. "He'd come home from work covered in sawdust and sweat, and he'd pick up his guitar and suddenly he was anywhere he wanted to be."

Jackie was quiet for a moment, and Nick could see him processing this information about Dan Johnson, about the kind of man who could find freedom in six strings and a few chords.

"My dad says music is a luxury," Jackie finally said, and there was something in his voice that suggested he'd been thinking about this for a while. "He says it's fine as a hobby, but real success comes from understanding economics, from knowing how the world actually works."

Nick looked at his friend, really looked at him, and for the first time he began to understand that Jackie's world was different from his own in ways that went beyond the obvious differences in their houses and their parents' jobs.

"What do you think?" Nick asked.

Jackie was quiet for so long that Nick thought he might not answer. Then: "I think your dad was right. I think music doesn't care where you come from. But I think the world does."

The conversation hung in the air between them, and Nick felt something shift in their friendship—not a crack, not yet, but the recognition that there were forces at work in their lives that they were only beginning to understand.

The next morning, they met Patty Lally.

She arrived during breakfast. Fourteen years old, wheat-colored hair, and a voice that made everyone else sound like they were trying too hard.

She was the most magnetic girl at camp. Unlike the girls they knew from Sudbury—who seemed to calculate every word, every gesture—Patty moved through the world like she belonged wherever she happened to be standing.

"Hi," she said, sliding into the seat across from Nick and Jackie. "I'm Patty. You guys are in the music intensive, right?"

Nick nodded. "Yeah. I'm Nick. This is Jackie."

"Piano and vocals," Patty said, not asking, just stating what she did. "You play?"

"Guitar," Nick said.

"Guitar and piano," Jackie added.

Patty smiled. "Good. Most of the kids here just want to show off. You two look like you actually care about the music."

Over the next few days, the three of them became inseparable. They spent their mornings in the music building, working on arrangements that blended Nick's instincts with Jackie's precision and Patty's ability to find where both styles clicked.

"Listen to this," Patty said one afternoon, sitting at the piano and playing a chord progression.

Nick picked up his guitar and found a melody that wove through Patty's chords. Jackie added harmonies that made the whole thing sound like something that had always existed, waiting for the right three people to discover it.

"There it is," Patty said, grinning. "That's what we've been looking for."

They worked on Elton John's "Your Song," Patty's idea to blend Queen's harmonies with Aerosmith's emotion. Nick added the raw power that came from growing up in a house where dreams were luxuries you couldn't afford. Jackie added the technical precision that came from growing up in a house where excellence was not just expected but required. Patty added the heart that came from growing up with a grandmother who believed that beauty was worth investing in.

The result was something that transcended the sum of its parts.

When they performed it at the end-of-camp celebration, the other kids fell silent. This wasn't the kind of performance that made you want to clap politely and move on. This was the kind that made you want to lean forward and listen harder.

Afterward, as they sat by the lake watching the stars appear one by one in the darkening sky, Patty said something that would stick with all three of them.

"This is real, you know," she said. "What we have here. Don't forget it."

Nick and Jackie exchanged glances, and Nick felt something tighten in his chest—the recognition that this moment, this perfect harmony, couldn't last forever.

"What do you mean?" Jackie asked.

Patty was quiet for a moment. "I mean that what we found here—this way of making music together, this way of being friends—it's real. But the world we're going back to, it's not set up for this kind of thing. It's set up to separate people, to put them in categories, to make them compete instead of collaborate."

Nick felt a chill that had nothing to do with the evening air. "So what do we do?"

Patty smiled, but there was something sad in her eyes. "We remember. We remember that this is possible. And maybe, if we're lucky, we find ways to make it possible in the real world too."

The last night of camp, they made promises that felt both sacred and impossible. They would stay in touch, they would form a band together, they would change the world with their music. They exchanged addresses and phone numbers, and for a moment it seemed like nothing could break the bond they'd formed.

But as Nick lay in his bunk that final night, listening to the sounds of other kids packing their bags and saying their goodbyes, he felt that this perfect week had been a gift, but also a preview of something that might never be possible again.

The next morning, as their parents arrived to pick them up, Nick watched Jackie climb into Dr. Marsh's car—a vehicle that spoke of success and stability and a future that was already mapped out. He climbed into Grace's car—a vehicle that spoke of hard work and determination and a future that would have to be earned one day at a time.

As they drove away in different directions, Nick looked back at Camp Elbanobscot. He'd just experienced something beautiful and temporary, something that couldn't survive the return to the real world.

But he also felt something else, something that would sustain him through the difficult years ahead: the memory of what was possible when three people decided to make music together instead of competing with each other.

What they'd found here was real. The question was whether it would be strong enough to survive everything that was coming.

CHAPTER 11: THE FIRST FRACTURE

The first day of high school at Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School felt like stepping into a different universe—one where the invisible rules that had governed their middle school friendship suddenly became visible and unavoidable. Nick and Jackie walked through the front doors together, but by the end of the first week, they might as well have been attending different schools.

The building itself seemed designed to reinforce the social hierarchies that had been building in Sudbury for decades. The main hallway ran through the center of the school, with classrooms branching off on either side, but it was the cafeteria that told the real story of how things worked at Lincoln-Sudbury.

The cafeteria was divided into distinct territories, each one claimed by a different group of kids who had learned, through years of social evolution, exactly where they belonged. The newcomers—freshmen like Nick and Jackie—spent the first few weeks trying to figure out the unspoken rules that governed this space.

The Rampers claimed the elevated section at the back of the cafeteria, a raised platform that looked down on the rest of the room. These were the popular kids—often jocks, usually good-looking, who somehow knew they belonged together and moved with the confidence that comes from never having to worry about whether you fit in. Money helped, but it wasn't a prerequisite; it was more about the way they carried themselves.

The Rats congregated in the glass corridor that connected the cafeteria to the main building—a space that was technically part of the school but felt separate, like a buffer zone between the institution and the outside world. These were the kids who smoked cigarettes between classes, who talked about weekend parties and who seemed to understand that the rules were made for other people.

The Regs occupied the middle tables, the safe space between the elevated platform and the glass corridor. These were the kids who just wanted to fit in, who didn't want any trouble and who understood that the best way to get through high school was to keep your head down and follow the established patterns.

The Rollers claimed the tables near the auto shop entrance, a space that smelled like motor oil and possibility. These were the kids who lived and breathed anything with an engine and wheels, who spent their free periods working on cars and who understood that sometimes the best way to fix a problem was to get your hands dirty. They ate quickly and couldn't wait to get back to their projects, treating the cafeteria as a necessary stop rather than a social destination.

Nick and Jackie didn't fit into any of these categories, at least not at first. They were freshmen, still figuring out who they were and where they belonged. But the social forces that had been building in Sudbury for decades were already beginning to work on them, pulling them in different directions.

The first sign came during their second week, when Nick found himself gravitating toward the Rollers' tables during lunch. It wasn't a conscious decision—he just found himself sitting with kids who talked about cars and motorcycles and who seemed to understand the kind of practical problems that Nick's family faced every day.

"Your dad work construction?" asked a kid named Carl Marquis, who had grease under his fingernails and the kind of confidence that came from knowing how to fix things.

"Used to," Nick said, and he felt something tighten in his chest at the past tense. "He's a carpenter now, works construction when he can get it."

Carl was quiet for a moment, and Nick could see him processing this information. "Sorry to hear that. My old man's a mechanic. Says construction's dangerous work."

Nick nodded, and he felt something click into place—the recognition that he was sitting with kids who understood what it meant to work with your hands, to come home tired and dirty, to worry about whether the car would start in the morning.

Meanwhile, Jackie found himself being drawn toward the Rampers' elevated platform, not because he was trying to be popular, but because the kids up there seemed to understand the kind of future that Dr. Marsh had been mapping out for his son since birth.

"Your dad teach at BU?" asked a kid named David Bellenghi, who wore neat, preppy clothes and who seemed to understand that success was something you planned for rather than hoped for.

Jackie nodded. "Psychiatry."

David smiled, and Jackie felt something click into place—the recognition that he was sitting with kids who understood what it meant to have parents with advanced degrees, to grow up in houses where books mattered, to assume that college was not just possible but expected.

The first real fracture came during their third week, when Nick and Jackie found themselves sitting at different tables during lunch. It wasn't a dramatic moment—no argument, no falling out, no conscious decision to drift apart. It was just the natural result of two kids being pulled in different directions by forces they were only beginning to understand.

Nick looked across the cafeteria and saw Jackie sitting with the Rampers, laughing at something David Bellenghi had said. Jackie looked across the cafeteria and saw Nick sitting with the Rollers, listening to Carl Marquis explain something about carburetors.

They made eye contact, and for a moment it seemed like they might wave to each other, might cross the invisible divide that had opened up between them. But then Nick looked away, and Jackie looked away, and the moment passed.

That afternoon, Jackie came to Nick's house after school, carrying his guitar and the kind of nervous energy that comes from trying to maintain a friendship that's beginning to feel strained.

"Want to play?" he asked, and Nick could hear the uncertainty in his voice.

They went to the garage, and for a while it was like old times. Nick's raw talent blended with Jackie's technical skill, and they found the harmony that had always come naturally to them. But something had changed—not in the music, but in the space between the music.

"You hanging out with those car kids?" Jackie asked during a break between songs.

Nick felt something tighten in his chest. "They're all right. What about you and those college kids"?

Jackie was quiet for a moment. "They're all right too."

The conversation hung in the air between them, and Nick felt something shift in their friendship—not a crack, not yet, but the recognition that they were being pulled in different directions by forces they couldn't control.

That evening, Nick received a letter from Patty. She wrote about her first few weeks at Lincoln-Sudbury High School, about the kids she'd met and the music she'd been making. She asked if Nick and Jackie were still playing together, if they'd found ways to maintain the harmony they'd discovered at camp.

Nick wrote back, but he found himself lying by omission. He didn't mention the growing distance between him and Jackie, didn't mention the way they were being pulled in different directions. Instead, he wrote about the music they were still making, about the friendship that was still strong.

But as he sealed the envelope, Nick felt that he was trying to hold onto something that was already beginning to slip away.

The next day, Jackie received his own letter from Patty. He wrote back, and he found himself lying by omission too. He didn't mention the way he was being drawn toward the Rampers, didn't mention the growing sense that he and Nick were living in different worlds. Instead, he wrote about the music they were still making, about the friendship that was still strong.

But as he sealed his envelope, Jackie felt the same—trying to hold onto something that was already beginning to slip away.

The weeks passed, and the distance between them grew. Not dramatically, not all at once, but gradually. They still saw each other in the hallways, still waved when they passed, still maintained the outward appearance of friendship. But the easy intimacy they'd shared in the garage, the perfect harmony they'd discovered at camp, was beginning to feel like something that belonged to a different time and place.

The first real test came in November, when Nick's mother's car broke down and she couldn't afford to fix it. It wasn't a dramatic moment—no scene, no confrontation, no obvious crisis. It was just the kind of thing that happened to working-class families, the kind of thing that made Nick feel like he was living in a different world from the kids who sat on the elevated platform in the cafeteria.

Nick didn't tell Jackie about it. Not because he was ashamed, but because he didn't know how to explain something that Jackie's family would never have to worry about. How do you explain to someone who's never had to worry about money that your family is one broken-down car away from everything falling apart?

Jackie didn't tell Nick about the pressure he was feeling from his parents to focus on academics, to start thinking about college applications, to understand that his future was already being mapped out in ways that didn't include the kind of practical concerns that Nick's family faced every day.

They were still friends, but the cracks were forming. The social forces that had been building in Sudbury for decades were working on them, pulling them in different directions, teaching them that the world was divided into categories and that they belonged in different ones.

What they'd found at camp—that connection, that harmony—was still there. But it was beginning to feel like a memory of something that had once been possible, rather than something they could hold onto in the real world.

And then, in November of 1977, everything would shatter.

CHAPTER 12: DAN'S DEATH

The morning of November 14th, 1977, began like any other Monday in the Johnson household. Grace woke at five-thirty, made coffee, and packed Dan's lunch—a tuna sandwich on white bread, an apple, and two cookies she'd baked the night before. She wrapped it all in wax paper and aluminum foil, the way Dan liked it, and set it on the kitchen counter next to his thermos.

Dan came downstairs at six-fifteen, already dressed in his work clothes—heavy boots, jeans, and a flannel shirt that smelled like sawdust. He kissed Grace goodbye, told her he'd be home by six, and picked up his lunch the way he did every morning.

Nick was still sleeping upstairs, a seventeen-year-old boy who'd stayed up too late practicing guitar in the garage, trying to perfect the solo from "Dream On" that his father had taught him the week before. Dan had been proud of how quickly Nick picked up the technical parts, how naturally he understood the relationship between melody and rhythm.

"Let him sleep," Dan told Grace as he headed for the door. "He's got calculus first period. Kid needs his rest."

Grace watched him go, not knowing that this would be the last time she'd see her husband alive, the last time she'd hear his voice calling "Love you" as he walked down the driveway toward his truck.

Dan Johnson died that morning, working the Prudential Tower project in Boston's Back Bay. A cable snapped without warning, sending four steel I-beams crashing down onto the construction trailer where Dan had been checking blueprints, eating the tuna sandwich Grace had made that morning, probably thinking about Nick's calculus grades or getting home by six like he'd promised.

They said he died instantly, which is what they always tell the families because instant death is supposed to be a mercy, as if the speed of dying somehow makes up for the fact of it. Grace never believed them. She knew Dan well enough to know he would have fought it, would have tried to dive under the desk or behind the file cabinet, would have spent his last seconds thinking about getting home to his wife and seventeen-year-old son who was learning guitar in the garage Dan had built with his own hands.

The call came to Friendly's at 11:30 AM, during the lunch rush. Grace was serving table seven, refilling coffee cups and taking orders for burgers and fries, when Tom Andrews, the manager, appeared at her side with a look on his face that made her stomach drop.

"Grace," he said quietly, "there's a phone call for you. It's important."

She followed him to the office, her hands shaking as she picked up the receiver. The voice on the other end belonged to a man she'd never met, someone from the construction company, someone whose job it was to deliver the worst news a person could hear.

"Mrs. Johnson? This is Frank Morrison from Boston Construction. I'm calling about your husband, Dan. There's been an accident at the Prudential site. I'm afraid I have some very bad news."

Grace sat down in Tom's chair, the phone still pressed to her ear, and listened as the man explained what had happened. The words seemed to come from very far away, as if she were hearing them through water. Accident. Crane. Steel beams. Instant death. I'm so sorry for your loss.

When she hung up the phone, she looked at Tom Andrews and said, "I need to go to the hospital. My husband's been hurt."

Tom's face told her everything she needed to know. "Grace, honey, let me drive you. You shouldn't be driving right now."

At Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School, Nick was sitting in calculus class, trying to concentrate on derivatives while his mind wandered to the guitar solo he'd been working on. He was thinking about Dan's advice—"Don't rush the notes, let them breathe"—when the classroom door opened and Mrs. Patterson, the principal's secretary, stepped inside.

"Nick Johnson?" she said, her voice carrying that careful tone adults use when something's wrong. "Could you come with me, please?"

Nick gathered his books, confused and slightly worried. "What did I do?" he asked, but Mrs. Patterson just shook her head and led him down the hallway toward the principal's office.

"Your mother's on her way to pick you up," she said. "She'll explain everything when she gets here."

Grace arrived twenty minutes later, still wearing her Friendly's uniform, her face pale and her eyes red from crying. When Nick saw her, he knew immediately that something was very wrong.

"Mom?" he said, and his voice sounded small and uncertain, like he was five years old again.

Grace took his hand and led him to a chair. "Nick, honey, there's been an accident. At Daddy's work site. I'm afraid... I'm afraid he didn't make it."

Nick stared at her, trying to process what she was saying. "What do you mean, he didn't make it?"

"He's gone, Nick. Daddy's gone."

The words hung in the air between them, and Nick felt something break inside his chest, something he knew would never be whole again.

At the hospital, they wouldn't let Nick see the body. Too much trauma, the doctor explained. Closed casket would be the recommendation. Grace signed papers, her hands shaking as she filled out forms that asked for Dan's social security number, his date of birth, his next of kin. Nick sat in a plastic chair in the waiting room, numb and unable to process that his father was gone, that he would never hear Dan's voice again, never see him walk through the door after work, never feel his hand on his shoulder as they worked on a song together in the garage.

The next day, Jackie heard about Dan's death from kids at school. The news spread quickly through the hallways, carried by teenagers who understood that death was something that happened to other people's families, not their own. Jackie felt a chill run down his spine when he heard the news, and he knew immediately that he needed to go to Nick, that he needed to be there for his friend in whatever way he could.

But he hesitated. The class divide that had been growing between them over the past year suddenly felt like a chasm. What could he say to someone whose father had just died? How could he relate to that kind of loss when his own father was alive and well, teaching economics at Boston University?

Finally, after school, Jackie drove to Nick's house and knocked on the door. Grace answered, her eyes red and swollen, wearing a bathrobe over her clothes.

"Jackie," she said, and her voice was hoarse from crying. "It's good to see you."

"I'm sorry about Mr. Johnson," Jackie said, and the words felt inadequate, like trying to hold back the ocean with a teaspoon. "I'm really sorry."

Grace nodded. "He liked you. Said you were good for Nick. Said you understood music the way he did."

Nick appeared at the door behind his mother, and for a moment the two boys looked at each other across the threshold. Nick's face was pale and drawn, his eyes hollow with grief. Jackie felt something tighten in his chest, the recognition that his friend was carrying a weight that he couldn't begin to understand.

"I'm here if you need anything," Jackie said, and he meant it. "Anything at all."

Nick nodded, but he couldn't speak. The words seemed to stick in his throat, and he realized that he didn't know how to talk to Jackie anymore, didn't know how to explain what it felt like to lose your father, to become the man of the house at seventeen, to understand that the world was a dangerous place where people you loved could be taken away without warning.

"Thanks," Nick finally managed, and the word felt like a stone in his mouth.

The funeral was held three days later at St. Mary's Church in Sudbury. The working-class neighborhood turned out in force—union brothers from the construction site, neighbors who'd known Dan for years, people who understood what it meant to lose someone who worked with his hands, who provided for his family, who built things that mattered.

Grace wore black, stoic and composed, accepting condolences with the kind of dignity that comes from having no choice but to keep going. Nick wore his only suit, a hand-me-down from Dan that didn't fit quite right, the sleeves too short and the pants too long. He stood next to his mother, feeling like an imposter in clothes that belonged to a man he would never become.

Jackie and his parents attended the funeral, and the class divide was visible even in grief. Dr. Marsh wore a dark suit that had been tailored to fit him perfectly, his shoes polished to a shine that reflected the overhead lights. Mrs. Marsh wore a black dress that looked expensive without trying too hard, her hair styled in a way that suggested she'd had time to prepare for this moment.

Nick watched them from across the church, and he felt something harden in his chest. Even at his father's funeral, even in the face of death, the Marsh family looked perfect. Their clothes fit right. Their grief looked composed. They belonged to a different world—a world where you had time to mourn, where you didn't have to go back to work the next day just to keep the lights on.

After the funeral, Grace returned to work at Friendly's. She couldn't afford to take time off, not with the mortgage payment due and Nick's school expenses and all the other bills that didn't stop coming just because Dan was gone. She worked double shifts, serving burgers and fries to people who had no idea that the woman refilling their coffee cups was a widow, that she was holding herself together with sheer willpower and the knowledge that her son needed her to be strong.

Nick felt the weight of responsibility settle on his shoulders like a physical thing. At seventeen, he was now the man of the house, expected to help with the bills, to take care of his mother, to understand things about the world that he wasn't ready to understand. He stopped playing guitar in the garage because every note reminded him of Dan, every chord progression brought back memories of the man who had taught him that music was the one thing that didn't care where you came from.

The garage became a place of ghosts instead of music. Dan's tools sat on the workbench where he'd left them, waiting for hands that would never come back to use them. The guitar Nick had been learning on sat in its case, silent and accusing, a reminder of happier times that felt like they belonged to someone else's life.

Jackie tried to reach out, calling Nick's house, stopping by after school, offering to help with homework or just to talk. But Nick found himself building walls, pushing his friend away.

"I don't want to talk about it," Nick would say when Jackie asked how he was doing, and the words came out harsher than he intended.

Jackie would nod and say he understood, but he didn't. How could he? His father was alive and well, his family was intact, his future was mapped out in ways that didn't include the kind of practical concerns that Nick's family faced every day. The chasm between them grew wider with each passing week, until it felt like they were living in different worlds, speaking different languages, carrying different kinds of pain.

By the time summer arrived, Nick and Jackie were still friends in name only. They would see each other in the hallways at school, nod in recognition, maybe exchange a few words about homework or the weather. But the easy intimacy they'd shared in the garage, the perfect harmony they'd discovered at camp—all of that was gone, replaced by a distance that felt both inevitable and heartbreaking.

What they'd once had was still there, somewhere. But it was buried under layers of grief and responsibility, under the weight of a world that had proven itself dangerous and unpredictable.

Nick had learned that lesson the hard way, and it was a lesson that would shape everything that came after.

CHAPTER 14: THE BRIDGE WHERE FRIENDSHIP DIED
**Third Person - Fall 1978**
**Word Count Target:** 2,000-2,500 words

---

Fall 1978, nearly a year after Dan Johnson died, and the friendship between Nick and Jackie had become a casualty they both refused to acknowledge. They nodded in hallways. They didn't speak. The easy intimacy that once let them finish each other's sentences had calcified into the kind of distance that feels permanent.

Nick had learned to carry his grief alone. Jackie had learned that some people don't want to be saved.

And then came the day that made it final.

Saturday afternoon in October, the kind of New England fall day that gets photographed for tourist brochures—leaves bleeding gold and red, air sharp enough to sting your lungs. The conservation land that separated the two neighborhoods and ran adjacent to Hop Brook was alive with the sound of dirt bikes tearing through trails that were never meant for engines.

Nick led the pack on a Honda CR125 he'd rebuilt from scrap parts, the two-stroke engine screaming defiance through the woods. Behind him, Stephen Murphy and Mark Pianka—his bandmates from Lincoln—followed on their own bikes, engines idling with the confidence of kids who knew how to fix what they broke.

Jackie was in the woods too, but on foot. He'd been walking these paths since childhood, knew every stone wall marking real American history in a forgotten town. He went there to think, to escape his father's house, to be nobody for a while. To be himself.

They met where the main trail crossed Hop Brook.

The wooden bridge was older than either family's time in Sudbury, planks gray with age and slick with moss. Jackie stood dead center, looking down at the water like it might offer answers. Nick killed his engine but didn't dismount, front tire inches from Jackie's boots.

Neither one moved.

"You need to move," Nick said.

"It's a walking bridge," Jackie replied, not looking up. "Always has been."

Behind Nick, the other bikes idled. Their riders sensed this was about more than right of way.

"Lot of things used to be different," Nick said. "Doesn't mean they can't change."

Jackie finally looked up. For a moment—just a flash—they almost smiled at each other. Almost remembered being thirteen and fearless, making music in that garage before the world taught them they were supposed to be enemies.

Then a voice called from the woods. "Everything okay here?"

Dr. Richard Marsh emerged from the trail in pressed khakis and a Boston University windbreaker, dressed for his afternoon walk. He took in the scene—his son facing down four kids on dirt bikes—and his face set into an expression that said his worst assumptions were being confirmed.

"These boys bothering you, son?"

And there it was. *These boys.* Not classmates, not neighbors, not even kids. *These boys.* The pronoun that built walls.

"No, Dad," Jackie said, and something in his voice changed. The almost-smile vanished, replaced by the mask he wore at recitals. "I was just explaining about the walking bridge."

Dr. Marsh looked at Nick the way you'd look at a stray dog in your yard—assessment without recognition, threat calculation without seeing a person. In that look lived every letter to the town council, every property value concern, every fear that the wrong element was getting too close.

"Perhaps you boys could find another trail," he said. "One more appropriate for your vehicles."

Nick's hands tightened on the handlebars. Behind him, someone revved their engine—the meaning unclear.

"It's a public trail," Nick said. "We got as much right—"

"Rights," Dr. Marsh interrupted, "come with responsibilities. Something you might understand when you're older."

The casual cruelty of it—the way adults could weaponize class difference with such pettiness, turning teenagers into combatants with a single condescending sentence.

What happened next set everything in motion.

Jackie moved. Stepped aside, gave ground, chose peace over principle. The gesture was small—just a few steps to the right—but in the economy of teenage pride, it was surrender.

Nick could have just ridden past. Could have taken the victory and moved on. But something in the way Dr. Marsh stood there—protective and possessive and so goddamn sure of his righteousness—made that impossible.

Nick revved his engine.  And revved it again for extra measure and rode across that bridge at full throttle.

The old wood groaned like something dying. The sound echoed across the water, a scream turned into splinters. His friends followed, four bikes turning the walking bridge into a statement about whose freedom mattered more, whose world had the right to take up space.

When they were gone, the silence felt like aftermath. Like something had been killed that couldn't be brought back.

Dr. Marsh put his hand on Jackie's shoulder. Not comforting—claiming.

"Those boys," he said again, the pronoun carrying even more weight now. "They don't understand boundaries. That's why they'll never amount to anything."

Jackie opened his mouth, but the words died in his throat like fish pulled from water. What could he say? That one of those boys had been his best friend? That they'd made music together that felt like truth? That the boundary wasn't about bridges or trails but about something neither of them had chosen and both were trapped by?

"Come on," Dr. Marsh said. "Your mother's expecting us for dinner."

Jackie looked back once. His eyes met Nick's across the distance that had opened up between them—a distance measured in more than feet, more than the width of Hop Brook, more than the space between their separate quadrants of Sudbury. For a moment, it seemed like he might crash the code of silence, that he mighy yell out, scream out at the walls that had been built around him. Might try to bridge what was breaking.

But Dr. Marsh's hand tightened on his shoulder, and Jackie turned away. He followed his father down the trail toward a future already mapped out, toward a world where he belonged and Nick didn't, toward a life that would never include the friendship they'd once shared.

Nick watched them go, understanding that this was it. The friendship was over. Not dying—dead. Done. Whatever they'd had in that garage, whatever harmony they'd found at camp, whatever made them believe music could bridge the gap between their worlds—it was gone.

He circled back, stopped and sat on his bike for a long time, listening to water flow under the bridge. Thinking about the man, Dan Johnson. About that garage full of unused tools. About the music that had once connected them and now just reminded him of everything he'd lost.

Some walls last longer than the people who build them. The stone walls in these woods had outlived the farmers who stacked them, still dividing land nobody owned anymore. Some divisions, once made, become permanent. Some friendships, once broken also become permanent.

He kick-started the engine and rode away, leaving the bridge behind. Leaving Jackie behind. Leaving whatever they'd been to each other behind.  

That was it. Four bikes, one pronoun, and a friendship dissolved into the water below.

CHAPTER 15: THE POSTER

April 2nd, 1979. Monday morning, 7:30 AM.

The Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School music room occupied a small corner of the building where acoustics went to die. Cinder block walls painted institution green, fluorescent lights buzzing overhead, instrument lockers that hadn't been properly maintained in years. Music had been dying at L-S for a while now—budget cuts, declining enrollment in electives, kids choosing computer lab over band practice. But on the bulletin board between the jazz band schedule and a faded Boston Symphony poster, someone had mounted something new. Something irresistible.

**BATTLE OF THE BANDS**
**March 15, 1979**
**Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School**
**Grand Prize: $500 and professional recording session**
**Sponsored by WBCN "The Rock of Boston"**

Five hundred dollars in 1979 was real money. Three months of Grace Johnson's grocery budget. A used car. A semester at community college. For Nick, it meant his mother wouldn't have to pick up that extra Friday shift at Friendly's. For Jackie, it meant proving something his father couldn't buy for him.

Nick entered from the west hallway, heading to his first period class. Jackie came through the east entrance, dropping off sheet music before homeroom. They both saw the poster at the same moment and moved toward it, reading from opposite sides of the bulletin board. Close enough that their shoulders almost touched. Close enough to remember what it felt like to stand next to each other without everything else in the way.

Bob Wentworth watched from his desk. He'd given up symphony halls for steady paychecks twenty years ago, but he could still hear potential in the noise teenagers made. And he could recognize a dangerous moment when he saw one.

"Gentlemen," he said. "I see you've noticed the announcement."

Neither boy moved. They kept staring at the poster like it might offer a way back to something they'd lost.

"Band names due Friday," Wentworth continued. "Performance slots are limited to eight bands."

That's when Nick looked at Jackie. Really looked at him for the first time since the bridge.

Six months had changed them both. Jackie stood straighter now, like he'd finally grown into the posture his parents had been correcting since childhood. His eyes were careful, measured. Nick's jaw was set in a way that hadn't been there before, his hands still even when they used to fidget. They'd both learned how to hide.

"I suppose you'll both be entering," Wentworth said into the silence.

"Queen," Jackie said. Not a suggestion—a statement of fact.

"Aerosmith," Nick said.

Wentworth sighed. "The competition is meant to celebrate musical diversity—"

"It's about the music," Nick interrupted, still looking at Jackie. Not at Wentworth.

"Exactly," Jackie said. "The music. Not the story around it."

"Right. Just the sound."

"Just what you can actually do."

Wentworth looked from one boy to the other, understanding that every word they were saying meant something else entirely. They weren't talking about music at all. They were talking about everything that had broken between them, everything they'd lost, everything they couldn't say directly because saying it out loud would make it too real.

He wanted to stop this. He'd seen it before—friendships that turned into competitions, competitions that turned into something uglier. But he also knew that some moments couldn't be stopped, only witnessed.

"Wait," Wentworth said carefully, pulling open his desk drawer. "I attended the '75 Curtis Middle School Talent Show. You two stood on that stage together." He found what he was looking for—a photograph, slightly faded. "You made something real."

He held up the photograph. Two thirteen-year-old boys, arms around each other, holding guitars. Smiling like the future was something they'd figured out together.

"This is what you were capable of," Wentworth said.

Nick and Jackie stared at the photograph. For a moment, something shifted in both their faces—recognition, maybe, or grief for who they used to be. The room was so quiet Wentworth could hear the clock ticking on the wall.

But Nick looked away first. Jackie followed.

"That was middle school," Nick said, his voice flat. "This is different."

"We're different," Jackie added.

"Yeah," Nick said. "We are."

The bell rang. Students would flood the hallways in thirty seconds.

"May the best band win," Nick said, because he didn't know what else to say.

"You can count on it," Jackie replied. 

They walked out through different doors without looking back. Wentworth sat at his desk, still holding the photograph of two boys who used to be best friends, and felt something break in his chest that he couldn't quite name. He'd seen students lose interest in music, lose passion for their instruments, lose faith in their own talent. But he'd never watched two kids lose each other quite like this—slowly, inevitably, like watching a landslide in slow motion and being unable to stop it.

By lunch, the entire school knew.

The posters multiplied. By second period, they hung in the library. By third, one appeared in the cafeteria near the lunch line. By fourth, there was one taped to the entrance of the gymnasium, another in the infamous Glass Corridors where kids smoked between classes and pretended the teachers couldn't see through the purple-white haze.

Music had been dying at L-S for years. But this—a real competition, a real prize, WBCN sponsorship—woke something up. The school came alive like someone had kicked over a hornet's nest.

It wasn't just Nick Johnson versus Jackie Marsh. It was Aerosmith versus Queen. Garage rock versus private lessons. The kind of competition that made everyone choose without anyone asking them to.

And something strange happened.

Lincoln-Sudbury had always been divided. You were a Ramper—the cool kids, the ones with letter jackets and party invitations. Or you were a Rat—the rebels who smoked in the Glass Corridors and showed up late. Or you were a Reg—regular, invisible, just trying to get through without being noticed. Or you were a Roller—the kids who hung around the auto shop, fixing cars, riding anything with an engine.  Rampers, Rats, Regs and Rollers.

Those lines didn't blur. They didn't cross. That was just how it worked.

But the Battle of the Bands did something nobody expected. It created a new division that cut straight through the old ones. Rampers who liked Aerosmith sat with Rats at lunch. Regs who'd never spoken to Rollers argued about Queen's vocal harmonies on the school bus. Kids who couldn't tell the difference between a power chord and a car chord were picking sides anyway, the way you pick a team in a game you don't understand but everyone else is playing.

The four-way split that had defined the school for generations suddenly collapsed into two camps. And for the first time in years—maybe ever—kids from different worlds were talking to each other.

Over the next two weeks, the cafeteria split down the middle. The Lincoln kids naturally gravitated toward Nick's table—three of their own were in his band. The Sudbury kids divided themselves—some toward Jackie, some toward Nick—following instincts they simply couldn't name, because nobody asked. Geography older than any of them, playing out in lunch table arrangements.

Kids who'd sat together for years found themselves on opposite sides of an aisle they'd never noticed before. New friendships formed, at least temporarily.

Even the teachers felt it, though they'd never admit it. Mr. Ogden found himself thinking about harmonic structures during calculus class. Mrs. Williams caught herself humming "Dream On" while demonstrating how to fold pie dough in the home ec room. The janitor kept his head down and fixed the cooling ducts, having seen this kind of thing before and knowing it never ended well.

Few people remembered what Wentworth remembered—that in 1975, these two boys had won the middle school talent show as a duo. Most of their classmates had been there, but four years is a long time when you're seventeen. Memories fade. Friendships end. People forget that things were ever different. That at thirteen, Nick and Jackie—best friends then—had stood on a gymnasium stage and made something that felt honest, pure, completely free of everything that would come later.

Five posters hung proudly throughout the school for six weeks. Every morning, students stopped to look at them. Some added notes in the margins—band names, practice schedules, crude drawings of guitars. The paper became worn at the edges from being touched so many times, from being read and reread like they might reveal something new.

Lincoln-Sudbury was gearing up for a battle.

And in the middle of it all, two boys who'd once been best friends practiced separately. Nick stayed in his garage playing "Dream On" and other Aerosmith classics until well past midnight, fingers cramping but refusing to stop. Jackie rehearsed "Somebody to Love" alone in the music room, his throat raw from pushing his voice to match Freddie Mercury's range.

Neither one would be the first to give in.

The Battle of the Bands was six weeks away, and Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School had chosen sides.

Because sometimes the only way to prove you've survived losing something is to compete against what you lost.

And sometimes that's the saddest thing of all.

CHAPTER 16: SENIOR YEAR - NICK'S WORLD
**Third Person - April 1979**
**Word Count Target:** 1,200-1,500 words

---

The cafeteria at Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School smelled like industrial food service and teenage sweat. Nick Johnson sat at his usual table with Stephen Murphy and Mark Pianka, eating the lunch Grace had packed before her morning shift at Friendly's. Three weeks since the poster appeared, and the cafeteria had reorganized itself into two camps without anyone officially drawing the line.

Across the room, Jackie Marsh sat with the college-bound crowd, kids who talked about SAT scores and summer programs. Different worlds, same building.

That's when Jackie stood up.

"Somebody to Love," he announced to the cafeteria.

The room went quiet. Students turned in their seats.

Jackie began to sing. His classically trained voice filled the space—operatic, controlled, technically flawless. His band members joined in, moving with precision. For four and a half minutes, Jackie Marsh owned that cafeteria. The performance was impressive. Undeniably impressive.

When he finished, scattered applause from his side of the room.

Then Nick stood up.

"Dream On," he said.

Nick began to sing a cappella. His voice was rougher than Jackie's, but it carried something else—something that came from knowing what it meant to lose. Stephen Murphy joined in with harmonies. Mark Pianka played air piano, fingers moving through invisible chords. The three of them created something that didn't sound polished, didn't sound trained, but sounded honest.

"Dream until your dreams come true," Nick sang, the final note hanging in the cafeteria air.

Silence.

Both performances were brilliant in completely different ways.

Mr. Wentworth appeared from the faculty table. "Save it for May 15th. Both of you, come with me."

Nick and Jackie walked to the principal's office without looking at each other.

Principal Brad Sargent sat across from them, looking tired. He'd been doing this job for two decades. He'd seen plenty of rivalries, but this one felt different.

"I'm hearing reports," he said. "Students skipping classes to practice. Arguments in hallways. Now cafeteria performances that stop lunch service."

"We're preparing for the competition, sir," Jackie said.

"Music is supposed to be passionate," Nick added.

"Music is supposed to bring people together," Sargent said. "You two are tearing this school apart." He leaned back in his chair. "I should shut the whole thing down. But we've got other schools entering, WBCN's involved, the fire marshal's already approved the capacity..."

Nick and Jackie both tensed. Neither wanted it shut down.

"Here's what's going to happen," Sargent continued. "Keep your musical differences off school property until May 15th. No more cafeteria performances. No more hallway arguments. Understood?"

"Yes, sir," they both said.

"Good. Now get out."

They walked down the hallway in silence. Three years of not speaking, three years of separate paths, all leading to this.

"It's on," Nick said finally.

"How's the set list coming?" Jackie asked, his voice carefully neutral.

"Good. Yours?"

"Fine."

The politeness felt like armor. Neither one willing to show weakness, neither one willing to admit they cared what the other thought.

The words hung between them like a door slamming shut.

That evening, Nick sat in the garage Dan had built. The Battle of the Bands poster was taped above the workbench, next to the old Aerosmith calendar from 1975. The garage still smelled like sawdust and motor oil, even though Dan's tools hadn't moved in two years.

Grace appeared in the doorway, still in her Friendly's uniform. Her hands were red from dish water.

"You okay?" she asked.

Nick looked up. "Yeah. Just thinking."

Grace sat on the workbench, movements careful. "Your father would've liked this. You competing."

"I know."

"He always said you had something. Not just talent. Something else."

Nick felt his throat tighten. "I miss him."

"I know." Grace's hand found his shoulder. "But he's here. In this garage. In that guitar. In you."

They sat quietly, listening to the neighborhood settle into evening—distant traffic, someone's TV through an open window, a dog barking three houses down.

Nick picked up his guitar. Started playing the opening chords to "Sweet Emotion," the ones Dan had taught him four years ago. His fingers found the patterns like muscle memory, like Dan's hands were still guiding his.

Grace closed her eyes and listened.

The Battle of the Bands was six weeks away. Nick practiced until Grace finally went inside and turned off the porch light as a hint. He kept playing in the dark for another hour.

Some things you don't do for the audience. Some things you do for the people who aren't there to hear them.

CHAPTER 17: JACKIE'S WORLD
**Third Person - April 1979**
**Word Count Target:** 1,200-1,500 words

---

Dinner at the Marsh house was served at six-thirty. Always six-thirty. The dining room table could seat twelve but usually held four—Dr. Richard Marsh at the head, Eileen Marsh at the foot, Jackie and his sister Elizabeth on opposite sides like chess pieces arranged for a game that had been decided before it started.  

The meal was chicken piccata. The conversation was equally measured.

"Jackie received his acceptance letter from Juilliard today," Eileen said, her voice carrying the practiced enthusiasm of someone announcing good news that had been expected all along. "Early admission. Full scholarship."

"Congratulations," Elizabeth said, looking at her brother across the table.

Jackie cut his chicken into precise pieces. "Thank you."

"We're very proud," Dr. Marsh said. "Though I assume you'll continue with Mr. Holloway's lessons through graduation. No point in letting technique slip before fall semester."

"Of course."

"And the spring recital at Jordan Hall is April twentieth. The program coordinator called—they'd like you to perform the Chopin Nocturne in E-flat. The one you played at Tanglewood last summer."

Jackie's fork paused halfway to his mouth. April twentieth was three weeks before the Battle of the Bands. Three weeks of intensive practice for a classical recital, then shift gears completely for Queen covers in a high school auditorium.

"I can do both," Jackie said.

Dr. Marsh set down his wine glass. The crystal made a precise sound against the mahogany. "Jackie, we've discussed this. The Jordan Hall recital will have reviewers from the Globe, faculty from the Conservatory. Real opportunities. This high school competition—"

"Is also real."

The words came out before Jackie could stop them. Too sharp. Too direct. Not the way conversations worked at this table.

Eileen's smile tightened slightly. "Of course it's real, sweetheart. Your father just means it's not the same caliber—"

"It's sponsored by WBCN," Jackie said. "It's regional. Five schools, eight bands total. The prize includes a professional recording session."

"Which you don't need," Dr. Marsh said. His voice was still calm, still measured, but something underneath had shifted. "You'll have access to professional studios at Juilliard. You'll work with musicians who've played Carnegie Hall. This is a local radio station promoting local bands. It's beneath your level."

Jackie looked down at his plate. The chicken had gone cold.

Elizabeth spoke into the silence. "Can I be excused? I have calc homework."

"Finish your vegetables first," Eileen said automatically.

Elizabeth ate three more green beans, each one a small act of compliance, then left. Jackie heard her footsteps on the stairs, the sound of her door closing with just slightly more force than necessary.

"Jackie," Dr. Marsh said. "Help me understand why this high school competition matters so much to you."

Because I need to know if I can make something that feels real instead of just correct. Because every note I play in this house is someone else's idea of perfect. Because Nick will be there and I need to see—

"I want to do it," Jackie said instead.

"That's not an answer."

"It's my answer."

The silence that followed had weight to it. Dr. Marsh and Eileen exchanged a look—the kind of wordless communication that happens between people who've been married long enough to develop their own language.

"We'll discuss this later," Dr. Marsh said finally. "When you've had time to think clearly."

Jackie was excused twenty minutes later. He climbed the stairs to his room, each step feeling like lifting weight. His bedroom was exactly what you'd expect—organized, clean, full of the markers of a successful childhood. Trophies from piano competitions. Certificates from music camps. A bookshelf with theory textbooks arranged by difficulty.

A knock on his door. "It's me," Elizabeth said.

"Come in."

She was thirteen, small for her age, with their mother's dark hair and their father's analytical eyes. She sat on his bed without being invited, the way she'd been doing since she was old enough to walk.

"You're going to do the Battle of the Bands thing anyway," she said. Not a question.

"Yeah."

"Even though Dad will lose his mind."

"Probably."

Elizabeth picked at a loose thread on his bedspread. "Is it because of Nick?"

Jackie looked at his sister. Sometimes she saw too much.

"It's complicated," he said. 

"You guys used to be best friends."

"Yeah."

"What actually happened? I always wanted to know but I was afraid to ask."

Jackie sat down next to her. Through the window, he could see the houses on Maple Street—perfect lawns, perfect spacing, perfect lives arranged in perfect rows. Everything in its proper place.

"We grew up," Jackie said. "People change."

"That's what adults say when they don't want to explain things."

Jackie almost smiled. "Yeah. It is."

Elizabeth was quiet for a moment. Then: "I heard you practicing last night. After Mom and Dad went to bed. You were playing that Queen song. The one from the competition."

"'Somebody to Love.'"

"You sounded different. Not like when you play Chopin for Dad. Like you actually cared about the notes instead of just hitting them right."

Jackie felt something crack in his chest. Elizabeth was thirteen years old and she'd just articulated something he'd been trying to understand for three years.

"Don't tell Dad," Jackie said.

"Obviously."

They sat there together, not talking, just being in the same space without having to perform anything. Outside, the streetlights came on. Perfect timing, like everything else in this neighborhood.

"Jackie," Elizabeth said finally. "When you do the Battle thing—"

"What?"

"I hope you win. Not because of Juilliard or whatever. Just because I want to see you do something that makes you look the way you looked last night when you were practicing. Like you actually wanted to be there."

After she left, Jackie sat alone in his room. On his desk, next to the acceptance letter from Juilliard, was a photograph from the Curtis Middle School talent show, May 1975. Two thirteen-year-old boys on stage, arms around each other's shoulders, holding their trophy between them. Grinning like they'd figured out the secret to everything.

He picked up the photograph. Looked at it for a long time.

Then he put it face-down in his desk drawer and went back to practicing Chopin. The way he'd been taught. The way that was expected.

Because some performances you give for other people.

And some prisons you build yourself, one perfect note at a time.

CHAPTER 18: THE TOWN WEIGHS IN
**Third Person - Late April 1979**
**Word Count Target:** 1,500-2,000 words

---

By late April, Sudbury had chosen sides.

Grace Johnson noticed it first at the grocery store. Women she'd known for years now stopped to ask about Nick's "big competition" with a particular tone in their voices—some encouraging, some pitying, all of them making it clear they'd formed opinions about how this would end. The cashier at Star Market asked if Nick was nervous. The pharmacist wanted to know if he was "ready for that kind of pressure." Even Mrs. Warren at the dry cleaners had thoughts about whether garage bands could compete with "proper training."

Grace kept her responses short. "He's working hard." "He's focused." "We'll see what happens."

What she didn't say: *Please stop making my son's high school talent show sound like the rest of his life depends on it.*

---

The Sudbury Diner on Route 20 had become neutral territory, which meant it had become a battlefield. Grace sat in her usual booth Tuesday morning, her one day off from Friendly's, nursing coffee and trying to read the Globe. But the conversations around her made that impossible.

"—real talent versus just making noise—"

"—Marsh kid's been trained since he was five—"

"—but the Johnson boy's got heart, you can hear it—"

"—heart doesn't win competitions—"

Grace kept her eyes on her newspaper. The Red Sox had lost to the Yankees again. Nothing new there.

"Grace?" Mary Murphy slid into the booth across from her without being invited. Her son Stephen played bass in Nick's band. "How's Nick holding up?"

"He's fine."

"Stephen says they're practicing six hours a day. Is that true?"

"Something like that."

Mary leaned forward, voice dropping. "Grace, people are betting on this. Frank at the barber shop has a pool going. My husband put ten dollars on Nick winning."

Grace looked up from her newspaper. "People are betting on high school kids?"

"It's Sudbury," Mary said, as if that explained everything. Maybe it did.

The waitress came by to refill Grace's coffee. She was wearing a button that said "Rock the Vote" with a small Aerosmith logo underneath. Grace hadn't voted for anything. Wasn't planning to.

"The whole town's talking about it," Mary continued. "The Weekly ran another article yesterday. Front page. They interviewed both families. Did you see it?"

Grace had seen it. The reporter had called three times before she'd finally agreed to answer questions. They'd photographed Nick in the garage, surrounded by Dan's tools, looking like exactly what he was—a working-class kid trying to make something out of nothing. The headline: "Battle of the Bands: Two Students, Two Worlds, One Stage."

"I saw it," Grace said.

"Dr. Marsh wasn't happy about that article," Mary said. "My neighbor works at the school. She said he called the principal, wanted to know why they were 'turning a musical competition into class warfare.'" Mary made air quotes with her fingers.

Grace said nothing. Took another sip of coffee.

"There's a PTA meeting Thursday night," Mary said. "Emergency session. About the 'tension' in the school." More air quotes. "Are you going?"

"I work Thursday nights."

"Grace, you should be there. Dr. Marsh is going to be there. He's going to talk about how this whole thing has gotten 'out of hand' and maybe they should cancel the competition to 'restore harmony in the school community.'"

Grace set down her coffee cup carefully. "He wants to cancel it?"

"That's what I heard."

"Two weeks before the Battle?"

"He's going to say it's for the kids' own good. That the competition is causing too much stress, dividing the school, creating an unhealthy environment." Mary leaned back, watching Grace's face. "Someone needs to tell him that's bullshit."

Grace looked at Mary Murphy. They'd known each other for fifteen years. Their sons had played Little League together, gone to the same elementary school, learned to ride bikes in the same neighborhood. Mary's husband worked at the post office. They lived three streets over in a house that looked exactly like Grace's—small, worn, paid for one month at a time.

"I'll be there," Grace said.

---

Thursday night, Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School's library was packed. The PTA had scheduled the meeting for seven, but by six-forty-five every seat was taken. Parents stood along the walls, crowded in the doorway. Principal Sargent sat at the front table looking like he regretted every decision that had led to this moment.

Grace arrived at six-fifty, still wearing her Friendly's uniform. She stood in the back, next to the water fountain.

Dr. Richard Marsh sat in the front row, surrounded by other parents from the northwest part of town. He wore a blazer and khakis. His wife sat beside him, perfectly composed. They looked like people who attended a lot of meetings and knew how to run them.

Principal Sargent called the meeting to order at seven-oh-five. "Thank you all for coming. I know emotions are running high about the upcoming Battle of the Bands competition. The purpose of tonight's meeting is to address concerns about the impact this event is having on our school community."

Dr. Marsh raised his hand. Was recognized immediately.

"Thank you, Brad," he said, using the principal's first name in a way that suggested they'd talked before this meeting. "I think we all want what's best for our children. And I think we can all agree that what started as a simple musical competition has become something else entirely. Students are skipping classes to practice. Arguments are breaking out in hallways. The school newspaper ran a poll asking students to choose sides—as if this were a political election rather than a talent show."

He paused, letting that sink in.

"What concerns me most," Dr. Marsh continued, "is that this competition has become a referendum on class. Working-class students versus privileged students. Garage bands versus classical training. This is not what education should be about. We're supposed to be teaching our children to work together, to respect different approaches, to celebrate diversity. Instead, we're teaching them to compete, to divide, to see each other as adversaries."

Several parents nodded. Someone said, "Hear, hear."

"I propose," Dr. Marsh said, "that we cancel the competition. Or at the very least, postpone it until next fall, when emotions have cooled and we can approach it with the proper educational framework."

More nodding. More murmurs of agreement.

Grace felt something shift in her chest. That old familiar anger—the kind that came from being told by people with money and education what was best for people without either.

"Excuse me." Her voice carried across the library. Heads turned.

Principal Sargent looked relieved to see her. "Grace. Please, come up front."

Grace walked down the center aisle. She was aware of her Friendly's uniform, her worn sneakers, the fact that she smelled like fryer oil no matter how many times she washed. She was aware of Dr. Marsh watching her approach, his expression carefully neutral.

She stood in front of the room. Didn't sit.

"I'm Grace Johnson," she said. "Nick's mother. I work at Friendly's. I couldn't afford to take tonight off, but I'm here anyway because Mary Murphy told me there was talk about canceling the competition my son has been working toward for six weeks."

She looked directly at Dr. Marsh.

"Dr. Marsh is right about one thing. This competition has become about class. But not because the kids made it that way. Because the adults did. Because we decided that some kinds of music matter more than others. That some kinds of training are more legitimate than others. That some kids deserve opportunities and other kids should just be grateful for what they get."

The room was silent.

"My son practices in a garage his father built before he died. He plays a guitar that cost twenty-five dollars at a pawn shop. He's been working weekends at an auto shop to help pay for new strings. This competition—this five-hundred-dollar prize, this recording session—it means something to him. It means his music matters. It means talent comes from more than just expensive lessons and the right neighborhood."

She paused. Took a breath.

"Dr. Marsh wants to cancel the competition because his son might lose. He's dressing it up as concern about education and school harmony, but what he really means is that he can't imagine his son competing on equal ground with someone like mine. Because if Jackie Marsh—with all his training, all his advantages, all his opportunities—if he loses to a kid from a garage in Sudbury, what does that say about the world he believes in, where success goes to the people who can afford it?"

Dr. Marsh stood up. "That's not fair—"

"You're right," Grace said. "It's not fair. None of this is fair. But canceling the competition won't fix that. It'll just teach our kids that when things get hard, when the outcome isn't guaranteed, we give up. We find excuses. We protect the kids who were always going to be fine and tell the others that their dreams are just hobbies."

She looked around the room. At the parents watching her. At Principal Sargent, who was trying not to smile. At Mary Murphy in the back, who gave her a small nod.

"Let them compete," Grace said. "Let them play their music. Let them find out what happens when you work hard and take a chance and put yourself out there. Win or lose, that's worth more than any lesson we could teach them by canceling it."

She walked back down the aisle. Out the door. Into the parking lot.

Her hands were shaking. She sat in her car for a long time before driving home.

---

The PTA voted forty-two to eleven to keep the competition as scheduled.

Grace heard about it later from Mary, who called at ten-thirty that night.

"You should have seen Marsh's face," Mary said. "He tried to respond, tried to say you'd misunderstood him, but nobody wanted to hear it. Principal Sargent called for a vote five minutes after you left. It wasn't even close."

"Good," Grace said.

"Grace, that was the bravest thing I've ever seen."

Grace didn't feel brave. She felt tired. She had to be at Friendly's at six the next morning.

"Get some sleep, Mary," she said, and hung up.

The Battle of the Bands was nine days away.

The whole town was watching.

CHAPTER 18A: THE STATION MEETING

Two hours and forty-one minutes.

Grace Johnson just left a PTA meeting in her Friendly's uniform and told a room full of professionals what they didn't want to hear. Forty-two to eleven. Not even close. I told you that woman had steel in her spine.

I pour another inch of Pappy. Just an inch. I need to be sharp enough to finish this story and drunk enough to tell the truth.

"You know what the difference is between a rebel and a sellout?" I say into the microphone. "About one meeting. One conference room. One moment when you trade what you believe for what they'll approve. That's all it takes. You walk in pure and walk out with a company line, and it happens so smooth you don't feel the cut until you're bleeding all over someone else's floor."

I lean back and the chair protests, and I think: I know how it feels.

"April 1979. Three weeks before the Battle of the Bands. Two months before the last gasp of everything WBCN was supposed to be. Let me tell you about the meeting that changed everything."

---

WBCN's management office sat on the third floor above the studios — a room that had once been somebody's idea of cool and had aged into somebody's idea of a health code violation. The carpet was the color of a bruise, stained with decades of spilled coffee and dropped cigarette ash. The walls held framed gold records from bands the station had broken, hung at angles that suggested whoever mounted them had been in a hurry or half in the bag. Aerosmith. J. Geils. Boston. The Cars. Each frame a trophy case for a war we'd already won and were about to surrender.

The conference table was a slab of pressboard that buckled in the middle, covered in ring stains from mugs that had been set down and picked up by ten years of people debating what rock and roll owed to its audience. I'd sat at this table when we decided to give Aerosmith their first heavy rotation. I'd sat here when we broke The Cars and the phones rang for three days straight. I'd argued at this table, cursed at this table, once threw a promotional ashtray at Tom Andrews across this table because he wanted to put a jingle between Led Zeppelin and Black Sabbath.

This table was where the real work happened. And now it was where the real work was going to die.

The meeting started at ten AM. Charlie Morris — station manager, joint-smoker, believer in the power of letting DJs run free — sat at the head, which told me nothing was wrong. But the man sitting to Charlie's right told me everything was wrong.

His name was Gerald Kowalski. New Program Director, hired six weeks earlier from a station in New York City. Late thirties, sharp jaw, hair parted with the mathematical precision of someone who'd never in his life played an instrument or felt the floor shake under a Marshall stack. He wore a suit in a building where nobody wore suits. He carried a leather portfolio the color of money. His shoes were Italian and polished to a shine that reflected the overhead fluorescents like a funhouse mirror.

I hated him on sight, which I understand isn't fair. But I've spent my life trusting my instincts about people the same way I trust them about music, and Gerald Kowalski's frequency was static. Pure, manufactured, corporate static.

Tom Andrews sat across from me. Tom — who'd been at WBCN since '71, who'd broken J. Geils when Peter Wolf was still his overnight DJ roommate, who'd once stayed on the air for twenty-seven straight hours during a blizzard because he believed the city needed rock and roll more than it needed sleep. Tom caught my eye and gave me the smallest nod that said: I know. Hang on.

"Let's talk about the future," Kowalski said.

He opened his portfolio and spread papers across the buckled table like a general deploying troops. Charts. Graphs. Demographic breakdowns printed in the kind of typeface that makes everything look scientific and nothing feel human. I could see columns of numbers, age ranges, household income brackets, pie charts sliced into segments labeled 18-24, 25-34, Male, Female, College-Educated, Blue-Collar.

"WBCN's core audience is aging," he said. "Our 25-34 demo is up seven percent, but our 18-24 — your growth market, your future — is down twelve percent in the last eighteen months. The advertisers who matter — automotive, audio equipment, lifestyle brands — they're buying 18-34. That's the sweet spot. That's where the dollars live."

He tapped a chart like a teacher correcting a student.

"We need to grow the 18-34 demo, or we lose the automotive sponsors. Lose the automotive sponsors, we lose the revenue that keeps this station independent. Lose the independence—" He spread his hands and smiled the kind of smile that knows it's winning. "Well. You all know what's happening to independent radio."

We did. Clear Channel was swallowing FM stations like a python digesting rabbits. One by one, independent voices replaced by automated playlists generated by consultants in offices that had never been within a thousand miles of the bands they programmed. Boston had lost three stations in two years. We all knew the python was circling our building.

Charlie tapped his pen on the table. "Gerald has a proposal."

Kowalski pulled out a single sheet. Clean. Typed. The heading read: WBCN COMMUNITY ENGAGEMENT INITIATIVE — BATTLE OF THE BANDS.

"Regional high school music competition," he said. "WBCN sponsors. We brand it. We market it to schools within a thirty-mile radius of Boston. Kids enter their bands, we provide judges from our DJ roster, winner gets a recording session and airtime exposure. We sell sponsorship packages — Coca-Cola, local car dealerships, Jordan's Furniture — and position WBCN as the station that discovers the next generation of rock talent."

He paused. Looked around the table with the confidence of a man who knows he's selling something people are going to buy.

"It's community engagement that drives 18-34. It's authentic — local kids, local music, local pride. It's sponsorable — brands want to be associated with youth and discovery. And it's repeatable — we build it as an annual franchise that generates revenue every spring."

The silence in the room lasted about four seconds. The time it takes to cross a line you can't uncross.

"Annual franchise," Tom Andrews said. Not a question. A repetition, the way you repeat a diagnosis you're not ready to accept.

"The data supports it." Kowalski tapped his charts. "Stations that run community engagement programming see a fourteen percent lift in target demos within two rating periods. WJLB in Detroit. KROQ in LA. It's proven."

I looked at Tom. Tom looked at me. And in that look was everything we both understood: this was the moment. This was where the road forked. This was where WBCN either stayed what it was — a station that found real music and played it at full volume because we believed in it — or became what everything else was becoming. A brand.

I opened my mouth. And what came out surprised me.

"What if we use it to actually find talent?"

The table turned to me.

"Not as a brand exercise," I said. "Not as a demographic play. As what WBCN does. Discovery. We go into these schools and we listen. We send DJs who know the difference between a kid who can play and a kid who can make you feel something. We don't look for the next marketable act — we look for the next real thing. The next Aerosmith playing a bar in Allston that nobody knows about yet."

Tom leaned forward. "Maxanne's right. If we do this — if we put our name on it — it has to be authentic. WBCN discovers talent. That's the brand, Gerald. That's what we do. If this turns into a glorified corporate talent show, we lose the one thing that makes us worth sponsoring in the first place."

Kowalski watched us the way a chess player watches an opponent move exactly where he wants them. "I agree completely," he said.

He agreed. That should have been my first warning. When the man in the suit agrees with the rebels, it means the rebels just walked into the cage and closed the door behind themselves.

"Discovery. Authenticity. That's exactly the narrative we sell to sponsors. 'WBCN — Where Legends Begin.' We find talent, we give them a platform, sponsors associate their brands with the discovery process. Everybody wins."

Charlie tapped his pen. "So we're doing this?"

The Battle was eighteen hours away.

CHAPTER 23: MAXANNE - THE EVE OF BATTLE

---

Three hours into the broadcast. One hour left.

Studio B smells like fifty years of cigarette smoke that never quite left the walls, even after they banned smoking in 1995. The ON AIR light glows red above the door. Outside the booth, the station is empty—everyone evacuated hours ago, desks cleared, computers dark, fifty-six years of rock radio reduced to boxes in a hallway waiting for someone to decide what gets saved and what gets thrown away.

My hands rest on the console. Steady. That's the thing about guilt—you carry it long enough, it stops shaking you. It just sits there, heavy and permanent, like scar tissue around your heart.

I cue "Bohemian Rhapsody." The full six minutes, no radio edit. Because tonight we have time. Tomorrow we have nothing.

---

The Battle of the Bands was my idea.

Not mine alone—Tom Andrews and I pitched it together in the fall of 1978. Regional high school competition, WBCN sponsorship, prize money and a recording session for the winners. We sold it to management as a marketing campaign: discover the next Aerosmith before they need us, drive listeners in the 15-25 demographic, attract sponsors who wanted access to that audience.

It made sense. Corporate consolidation was already starting—you could feel it coming like weather, the way playlists were tightening, the way management kept talking about demographics instead of just playing music that mattered. The station needed money to stay independent. I thought I could game the system. Use capitalism to fund rebellion.

I'd discovered Aerosmith in 1973. Broke The Cars when they were still playing dive bars in Cambridge. Gave Boston their first airtime when everyone else thought they were too polished. I had the track record. I had the ear. I thought I could make stars, control outcomes, find lightning and bottle it.

The Battle of the Bands was supposed to prove that. Marketing genius wrapped in artistic legitimacy. Find talent, give them a platform, turn a high school talent show into something that mattered.

I thought I was the architect of success.

Turns out I was the architect of tragedy.

---

March 15, 1979. Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School auditorium, 985 seats packed with people who'd chosen sides before the first note was played. I sat in the judge's panel between two other WBCN DJs, clipboard in hand, trying to look professional while watching something die in real time.

Nick Johnson's band—Walking the Line—four kids from the northeast neighborhoods with duct-taped equipment and more hunger than technical skill. They played "Walk This Way" like their lives depended on it, because maybe they did. I could see Grace Johnson in the third row, still wearing her Friendly's uniform, hands gripped together in her lap like she was praying.

Jackie Marsh's band—Crown Jewels—four kids with professional equipment and the kind of technical precision that comes from expensive lessons and parents who can afford to care about things beyond making rent. They played "Bohemian Rhapsody" flawlessly. Every note perfect. Every harmony arranged. Empty as a mansion with no one home.

The crowd wasn't divided the way I'd expected. That was the strange part. Kids from all four quadrants—Rampers, Rats, Regs, Rollers—mixed together for the first time, choosing Aerosmith or Queen instead of zip codes. But they were still choosing sides. Still making it a war. Just different battle lines than the ones Sudbury had drawn for them.

Nick and Jackie didn't look at each other. Not once. Not during setup, not during performances, not when it was over and the damage was done.

I watched their friendship continue to drown in front of 985 witnesses. Public execution disguised as competition. And I'd sold the tickets.

---

Here's what I tell myself: I didn't create their division.

High school did that. Hop Brook geography did that. Dan Johnson's death in November 1977 did that—grief builds walls faster than friendship can climb them. Dr. Marsh's pressure on Jackie did that. Class warfare did that. America did that.

Two boys who once harmonized so perfectly it made their eighth-grade music teacher stop mid-sentence, stand there with her baton frozen, and realize she'd been teaching notes when these kids were making something that couldn't be taught—they'd been drifting apart for years. The labyrinth was killing them slowly. Private tragedy, the kind that happens to friendships every day. They might have found their way back to each other eventually, or they might not have. That's how innocence dies—quietly, gradually, in the spaces between conversations that never happen.

But I took that slow, private death and turned it into public spectacle. I saw two boys drifting apart and said, "Let's make them fight on stage. For sponsors. For content. For money."

I didn't kill their friendship. But I gave class warfare a microphone. I sold tickets to watch innocence die. I turned tragedy into entertainment.

And then I called it opportunity.

---

I left WBCN in 1980. One year after the Battle. Exactly one year.

The official reason was career advancement. Danny Goldberg's management company offered me a job doing national tour promotion. More money, more influence, more power to shape careers from the industry side instead of the radio side. Record label A&R work, artist management, the kind of position that meant I'd made it.

I told everyone it was the logical next step. Ten years breaking bands on-air, now I'd help them from behind the scenes. Still in music, just a different angle. People congratulated me. Tom Andrews threw me a going-away party.

But here's what I knew, sitting in this booth on my last night in 1980, watching the sun come up over Boston:

I was fleeing.

Not from failure—from success. The Battle of the Bands had been successful. Great turnout, ratings up, coverage in the Globe. Strong sponsorship, management wanted to make it annual. I'd proven I could turn content into money, tragedy into product.

And I couldn't face what I'd become.

Corporate consolidation was accelerating. I could see the future: playlists, safe choices, rebellion as branding. WBCN would survive by compromising, gradually, until the rebel station became just another frequency selling nostalgia for what it used to be.

I left before I had to compromise. I thought that was the smart choice. I thought leaving meant I'd preserved something pure.

Forty-four years later, I can see the truth: Leaving was the compromise. I traded my voice for survival. I went corporate. I became what I fought. Just quietly. Just gradually. Just the same as if I'd stayed.

---

The song reaches the opera section. Freddie Mercury's voice fills Studio B. He died in 1991. AIDS, thirty years of medicine too late. Another casualty of a world that punishes people for being what they are.

The clock on the wall says 11:47 PM. Thirteen minutes left.

Forty-four years in corporate music. A&R, promotion, artist management. I helped bands get signed—the safe ones, the ones that fit the formula, the ones that wouldn't challenge anything. I survived by becoming the machinery. 

I told myself I was still in music. Still helping artists. Still connected to what mattered.

But I wasn't on-air. I wasn't speaking. I'd gone silent.

The rebel DJ who turned the volume to full and didn't give a damn what anyone thought became a corporate suit who measured risk and calculated demographics and helped turn art into product.

I survived. Nick and Jackie didn't—their potential erased, their friendship killed, their music silenced. WBCN survived until 2024, compromising gradually until corporate consolidation killed it anyway. 


Three casualties of the same war. Three different strategies. Same outcome.

Nick stayed real. The system crushed him.

Jackie compromised. The system crushed him.

I fled. The system followed me.

The labyrinth has no exit. Fight, compromise, or run—you die regardless. Just different timelines. Just different flavors of the same defeat. 

---

"Bohemian Rhapsody" builds toward the rock section. "So you think you can stone me and spit in my eye..."

I know why the station brought me back. Not for nostalgia week. Not to "celebrate" fifty-six years of rock radio before corporate overlords flip the switch.

They brought me back because I'm the mirror. The cautionary tale. The one who left in 1980 and went corporate and survived by becoming complicit.

Look what you became, the empty station says. You left. We stayed. We all died anyway. Now tell that story on your way out. Confession disguised as celebration.

I could have said no. I'd been silent for forty-four years. Why come back?

Because the station's death gives me permission. You can't fire someone from a corpse. You can't blacklist someone when the industry's already dead. This is my last chance to break forty-four years of silence. To tell the truth about what I did. What we all did.

Not redemption. I can't undo May 9th, 1979. I can't bring back Nick and Jackie's friendship. I can't restore WBCN's rebel soul. I can't un-become what I became.

Just confession. Just witness. Just: This is what I did. This is what my ambition cost. This is what we lose when we turn children into content, friendship into competition, art into product.

The labyrinth wins. Always. That's not a question. 

But someone has to say it out loud. Someone has to document what we kill. Someone has to refuse to let the machinery erase even the memory of what existed before we destroyed it.

That's all I have. It's not redemption. It's not hope. It's not even resistance. 

It's just: I saw it. I participated. And before the lights go out, I'm telling you what we did.

---

The song ends. Six minutes of Freddie Mercury's genius, fading to silence in an empty station.

I lean into the microphone. The ON AIR light burns steady above the door.

"May 9th, 1979," I say. "Twenty-four hours before the Battle of the Bands. My Battle. My idea. My destruction."

My voice is steady. Forty-four years of practice at not shaking.

"Two boys in different rooms. Nick Johnson in his garage on Dutton Road, surrounded by his dead father's tools. Jackie Marsh in his basement studio, surrounded by his father's expectations. Both carrying more weight than any seventeen-year-old should carry. Both making choices they didn't understand yet. Both heading toward a stage I built for them."

I reach for the next cart. "Dream On." Aerosmith. The band I discovered in 1973.

"Neither of them knowing what I'd created. What I'd done to their friendship. What the labyrinth does to innocence. Every time. Forever."

I press play.

"This is 'Dream On.' The band that proved I had the ear. The band that made me think I could control outcomes."

The opening piano fills the booth. Haunting and pure.

"Twenty-four hours before I learned I couldn't save anyone."

I watch the clock tick toward midnight.

"Including myself."

CHAPTER 23A: PATTY'S DECISION

---

The rooster didn't crow at the Lally place. The pigs handled that — two hundred and sixty Yorkshires grunting their way to consciousness at five-fifteen every morning, a sound like the earth clearing its throat.

Patty Lally pulled on her boots at the back door and stepped into April air thick with the smell of hog waste and wet hay. The southeast corner of Sudbury didn't wake up pretty. It woke up honest.

She'd done morning feed since she was twelve. Five-gallon buckets of grain mixed with kitchen scraps, distributed across eight pens while her father checked water lines and her mother tallied receipts at the kitchen table. The pigs pressed against the gates when they saw her coming — not affection, just hunger wearing a familiar face. Patty didn't mind. There was something clean about a relationship that never pretended to be more than it was.

The Cavicchio farm sprawled three fields east, and when the wind came from that direction the combined stench of two pig operations could stop traffic on Landham Road. People on the west side of Hop Brook called it the smell of Southeast. They meant it as an insult. Patty wore it like a badge.

By seven-forty she'd showered twice, braided her hair still damp, and caught the bus at the corner of Morse and Landham. The shower never fully killed the smell. Soap could mask pig but not erase it. She'd learned this in seventh grade when Cheryl Mancini wrinkled her nose in homeroom and said, loud enough for everyone, "Something smells like bacon." The class laughed. Patty didn't. She looked at Cheryl until the laughter died, and said, "Bacon pays our mortgage." Nobody brought it up again.

Lincoln-Sudbury in April smelled like chalk dust and floor wax and the low-grade panic of seniors counting down to graduation. Patty walked the east hallway toward first period, past lockers tagged with band names and Aerosmith vs. Queen graffiti that the janitor scrubbed off every Friday and that reappeared every Monday, the argument as permanent as the cinder blocks underneath.

She stopped at Mr. Wentworth's music room to drop off a permission slip. The room occupied a corner of the building where acoustics went to die — cinder block walls painted institution green, fluorescent lights buzzing overhead, instrument lockers that smelled like valve oil and teenage spit. But on the corkboard between a yellowed Boston Symphony schedule and a jazz band sign-up sheet that nobody had signed, someone had pinned something new.

Block letters on goldenrod paper.

BATTLE OF THE BANDS
MAY 15, 1979
LINCOLN-SUDBURY REGIONAL HIGH SCHOOL
GRAND PRIZE: $500 AND PROFESSIONAL RECORDING SESSION
SPONSORED BY WBCN "THE ROCK OF BOSTON"

Patty read it twice. Not the prize money — five hundred dollars wouldn't change what she already knew about her music. She read the date. Six weeks away. Six weeks to decide whether she wanted to walk into a war zone carrying a guitar instead of a weapon.

---

She'd been watching them for months.

The cafeteria at Lincoln-Sudbury split like a cell dividing — Nick Johnson's table near the windows where the Rollers and working-class kids ate fast and loud, Jackie Marsh's table by the doors where Rampers debated college applications over sandwiches their mothers had wrapped in wax paper. Between them, thirty feet of linoleum that might as well have been the Mekong Delta.

Patty sat with Kristy Wesslen and Robin Bosshard at a table near the milk coolers — neutral ground, neither camp, claimed by nobody. From there she could see both boys without turning her head. Nick hunched over a tray, jaw working, not talking much, eating the way a man fuels machinery. Jackie sat with posture like a conductor waiting for the downbeat, every gesture measured, every word selected before it left his mouth.

Two boys performing different versions of who they thought they had to be.

The strange thing — the thing only Patty seemed to notice — was the way they never looked at each other. Not avoiding. Avoiding would require acknowledgment. This was something worse. They'd erased each other from the room. Nick's eyes swept the cafeteria and skipped Jackie's table like a scratch on a record, the needle jumping over the part it couldn't play. Jackie's gaze moved through the space where Nick sat as if that corner of the cafeteria held nothing but empty chairs.

Patty saw it because she remembered when they couldn't stop looking at each other.

---

Camp Elbanobscot. Summer of 1975. A music camp tucked into thirty acres of pine and scrub oak off Route 117, where Sudbury's conservation land met the river. Fifteen kids, three cabins, a piano that needed tuning, and a counselor named Dave who played Joni Mitchell so well that even the boys shut up and listened.

Patty had arrived knowing no one. Thirteen years old, already tall, her wheat-colored hair hanging straight to her waist, smelling faintly of something the other kids couldn't name but she could. Home. She'd packed extra soap and a bottle of her mother's perfume, just in case.

She didn't need them.

Nick Johnson sat on the dock the first morning, bare feet in the water, playing an acoustic guitar like he was arguing with it. No finesse — his fingers hit the strings hard enough to make them buzz against the frets, and the sound that came out was raw and specific and entirely his. He played "Dream On" like a boy talking to someone who'd left the room and wouldn't come back.

Jackie Marsh sat at the camp piano in the music cabin, running scales that melted into something from Queen when he thought nobody was listening. The shift happened in his hands — rigid precision loosening into something riskier, wilder, alive. When he played what he wanted instead of what he'd been taught, his shoulders dropped three inches.

On the third day, Dave put all three of them together for a group exercise. "Find harmony," he said. "Not unison. Harmony. Three different notes that need each other."

Nick started singing "Your Song." Elton John — not his usual territory, but the melody was simple enough. His voice cracked on the high notes and he didn't hide it. Jackie came in underneath with piano chords that steadied the song without smothering it. And Patty — Patty found the space between them.

That was the only way she could describe it later, to anyone who asked, though nobody ever did. Nick's voice ran hot. Jackie's ran cool. And there was a temperature in the gap between them, a frequency where neither boy lived alone. She stepped into it with her voice and the gap closed.

Dave stopped the exercise. Just stood there with his hands at his sides and his mouth half-open, because what he was hearing shouldn't have come from three kids who'd met three days ago.

"Do that again," he said.

They did it all week. By the lake at sunset. In the music cabin during free period. Walking the trails between sessions, Patty humming the melody while Nick and Jackie found their parts without thinking, three voices braiding until you couldn't hear where one ended and the others began. The pine trees listened. The lake held still. And for five days, nothing in the world mattered except the sound they made when they stopped trying to make it.

That week she learned what harmony actually meant — not agreement, but three different truths finding the exact place where they didn't have to choose.

Now she sat by the milk coolers in a cafeteria that smelled like industrial ketchup and watched two boys who couldn't even look at each other, and the distance between what they'd been and what they'd become sat in her chest like a stone she'd swallowed years ago and never passed.

---

She brought it up at rehearsal.

St. Michael's Church on a Tuesday evening, the four of them sprawled across pews that smelled like old hymnals and lemon oil. Robin Bosshard sat cross-legged on the altar steps, bass across his lap, his faint Swiss accent making every sentence land a half-beat slower than the words around it. Kristy Wesslen picked at her acoustic, blonde hair curtaining her face. Steve Bankuti tapped a paradiddle on the back of a pew with his sticks — a habit so constant that Patty sometimes heard it in her sleep, ghost rhythms in the dark.

"Battle of the Bands," Patty said. "WBCN's sponsoring it. May fifteenth. Eight slots."

Robin looked up from his bass. "At Lincoln-Sudbury?"

"We'd have to register by Friday."

Kristy stopped picking. "That's the Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh show. The whole school's chosen sides."

"I know."

Steve's paradiddle stopped. In the sudden silence the church creaked around them — old wood settling, a radiator ticking somewhere in the walls.

"What are you saying?" Steve asked.

"I'm saying we enter."

Robin set his bass down. He did that when he wanted to think, as if the instrument's weight cluttered his head. "Why?"

Patty looked at the stained glass above the altar. St. Michael with his sword, standing over something beaten. Evening light through colored glass threw patches of red and blue across the wooden floor like bruises on the church's skin.

"Because somebody has to play music that isn't a weapon."

Kristy exchanged a look with Steve. "You want to walk into their war?"

"I want to play our songs. 'Dreams.' 'Landslide.' 'Silver Springs.' 'Go Your Own Way.' The way we play them — not louder, not angrier, not more perfect. Just honest."

"And what does that do?" Robin asked. Not skeptical — genuinely curious. Robin was always genuinely curious. It was his most useful quality and his most infuriating one.

"I don't know," Patty said. "Maybe nothing. Maybe you can't fix something by playing music at it. But I heard what those two sound like in the same room, and right now they've forgotten. Somebody has to remind them."

Robin picked his bass back up, turned it in his hands. "So we're entering a war to play peace songs?"

Patty almost smiled. "Something like that."

Steve resumed his paradiddle — faster now, a double stroke roll that rattled the hymnal rack. "Nick's band plays like they're kicking down a door. Jackie's band sounds like the Boston Pops with better taste in rock. We'll get swallowed."

"I don't care about winning."

"Then what's the point?"

She thought about the lake at Elbanobscot. Three voices and the pine trees and the last light on the water. The way Dave had stood there, frozen, because what he was hearing had no business coming from kids who barely knew each other's last names.

"The point is showing up. The point is playing music that remembers what music is for."

Kristy started picking — the opening notes of "Dreams," slow and sure. Robin found the bass line without being asked. Steve joined with brushes on the pew back, soft as rain on a window.

Nobody said yes. Nobody had to.

---

She walked home after rehearsal because the bus didn't run past seven and her mother was buried in feed invoices. Two miles through Southeast Sudbury in the April dark, past the Cavicchio property where the security light threw long shadows across empty pens, past the conservation land where mini-bike tracks scarred the trailheads like old wounds, past mailboxes that leaned toward the road like tired men waiting for news that never came.

The smell found her at the property line. Thick, animal, alive. Home.

Other girls at Lincoln-Sudbury fought for their freedom with clothes and attitudes and the careful deployment of cruelty. Patty fought for hers with feed buckets and guitar calluses and the stubborn refusal to be ashamed of where the wind blew from.

She wasn't the kid who got invited to parties on the west side of Hop Brook. She wasn't the kid whose mother drove a Volvo to PTA meetings or debated summer programs over white wine in kitchens with granite countertops. She was the kid who could tell the difference between a gilt and a sow by sound alone and didn't apologize for any of it. If that made her strange, fine. Strange was just another word for free, and freedom was the one thing she'd never let anyone take.

The house sat dark except for the kitchen light — a small colonial with vinyl siding that needed paint, a barn that leaned slightly east, and a yard where chickens had beaten the grass into mud. Her mother sat at the table, calculator and ledger open, reading glasses pushed up on her forehead. The radio played something country, turned low enough to be company without being conversation.

"Rehearsal okay?"

"Yeah. We're entering the Battle of the Bands."

Her mother looked up. "At the high school? The one with the radio station?"

"WBCN. Yeah."

A pause. Calculator set down. Glasses removed. The look a mother gives when she knows her daughter is about to walk into something complicated and has decided to let her do it anyway.

"That's the Johnson boy and the Marsh boy's thing. The whole town's been talking about it."

"I know."

"You sure you want to get in the middle of that?"

Patty opened the refrigerator, found last night's meatloaf, and ate it cold over the sink. The meat was dry. She didn't care.

"I'm not getting in the middle of anything. I'm just playing music."

Her mother watched her eat. The expression on her face said she'd raised a daughter who made her own decisions and she wasn't about to start second-guessing now — but she reserved the right to worry.

"Okay," she said. And went back to her ledger.

---

Friday. Last period. 2:45 PM.

The hallway outside Wentworth's room had cleared. Most kids were already gone — sprinting for buses, idling in parking lots, scattering into the warm afternoon like seeds from a dandelion head.

Patty walked into the music room alone. The fluorescent lights hummed their one flat note. Wentworth sat behind his desk grading papers with the patience of a man who'd traded symphony halls for steady paychecks twenty years ago and had mostly stopped regretting it.

The sign-up sheet for the Battle of the Bands sat at the corner of his desk. Seven names filled the seven lines above the last blank space.

"Mr. Wentworth."

He looked up. Pen still in hand.

"I'd like to register a band."

Something shifted in his face. Not surprise. Recognition. He'd heard Patty sing in music class — had stopped mid-sentence once, the way Dave had stopped at camp, because the voice coming out of this tall girl from the pig farms wasn't the voice of a girl who should have been sitting in a high school music class filling out worksheets about time signatures.

"Band name?"

"Rumours."

He wrote it down. Careful block letters.

"Members?"

"Robin Bosshard on bass and guitar. Kristy Wesslen, harmonies and acoustic. Steve Bankuti, drums. Me on lead vocals and guitar."

"Genre?"

"Fleetwood Mac."

Wentworth set his pen down. Leaned back. Studied her — this girl with damp-braided hair and fingers callused from steel strings and the faintest trace of something soap couldn't conquer. The pig farm kid who sat by the milk coolers and watched the cafeteria divide itself along lines she refused to recognize.

"You know what you're walking into," he said.

It wasn't a question.

"I know what I'm walking into."

He held her gaze for a moment longer, then slid the registration form across the desk. "Eight bands. You're the last slot."

Patty signed her name. The pen scratched against the paper, loud in the empty room.

She took the carbon copy, folded it into her back pocket, and walked out into the hallway. The fluorescent lights buzzed overhead like trapped insects. The linoleum gleamed under her boots. Somewhere down the corridor, a locker slammed — the last student, the last echo, the building settling into the hollow quiet of a Friday afternoon.

Six weeks. That was how long she had to stand between two boys determined to destroy the best thing either of them had ever made.

She hummed "Go Your Own Way" all the way to the bus stop. Not as a statement. Just something she did.

CHAPTER 23B: PATTY'S PREPARATION

---

St. Michael's smelled like dust and devotion and whatever they put in the wood polish that made the pews glow amber under the overhead lights.

Patty had arranged the rehearsal for Tuesday — five o'clock, after the parish secretary locked up and left the key under the mat because Father DiNardo trusted musicians the way he trusted anyone who brought their own instrument to a house of God. The church was theirs from five to eight, and by five-fifteen they'd moved hymnals off the front pews, run an extension cord from the sacristy outlet, and turned the altar into a stage.

Steve set up his kit on the altar rug — snare, hi-hat, kick drum, a ride cymbal he'd bought at a yard sale in Maynard for three dollars. Robin plugged his bass into a small Fender amp wedged between the pulpit and the baptismal font. Kristy perched on the communion rail, acoustic guitar across her knees, tuning by ear because the electronic tuner Steve had borrowed from his older brother ate batteries faster than it found notes.

Patty stood at the center, where the priest stood on Sundays. Guitar strap over her shoulder. No setlist written down — she carried it in her head, each song in sequence, each transition mapped to the emotional architecture of what she wanted the audience to feel. Not think. Feel.

"Dreams" first. Open with longing. Let them settle.

"Landslide" second. Slow it down. Make them listen.

"Silver Springs" third. Turn the heat up. Let the ache build.

"Go Your Own Way" to close. Give them back their feet.

She'd chosen every song the way a surgeon chooses instruments — not for spectacle, but for function. Each one carried a specific message, and threaded together they formed an argument she couldn't make with words because words had a way of starting fights and she wasn't here to fight.

"Dreams" said: You don't own me, but I remember you.

"Landslide" said: Time passes. You can't stop it. What matters is what survives.

"Silver Springs" said: I know what we lost and I won't pretend it didn't matter.

"Go Your Own Way" said: You can leave. I'll still be here. And the door's still open.

Nick wouldn't hear it that way. Jackie wouldn't either. That was fine. Patty wasn't playing for their conscious minds. She was playing for the part of them that still remembered a lake, a sunset, three voices braiding in the pine-scented air before the world taught them to be afraid of harmony.

---

"From the top," she said. "Dreams."

Kristy started the intro — that floating C-major arpeggio that Lindsey Buckingham had made sound like falling water. Robin found the bass note underneath, a slow pulse. Steve came in with brushes, barely touching the snare, a whisper rhythm that said: listen.

Patty closed her eyes and sang.

"Now here you go again, you say you want your freedom..."

Her voice rose through the church. Past the pews and the hymnals and the offering boxes. Past the Stations of the Cross and the stained-glass St. Michael with his sword and the plaster saints in their wall niches who'd heard every kind of prayer but this. Up into the vaulted ceiling where the old beams held the weight of a hundred years of worship and the sound bounced back down warm and strange, the church itself becoming an instrument.

By the second verse, Robin had locked in. His bass hummed through the stone floor — you could feel it in your feet, a low vibration that connected the music to the bones of the building. Kristy leaned into the harmony on the chorus, her voice winding around Patty's like ivy around a trellis, different but inseparable. Steve's brushes hissed against the snare with the patience of someone who understood that rhythm wasn't about speed. It was about trust.

They ran "Dreams" three times. The first was loose — finding the tempo, remembering where the dynamics shifted. The second was tighter, the four of them listening to each other with the concentration of people defusing a bomb. The third was the one that mattered. On the third pass, the song stopped being four people playing and became one sound breathing.

"Landslide" came next. Patty alone at first — just voice and acoustic, the pick pattern Stevie Nicks had turned into a kind of lullaby for grown-ups. The church went still. Even Steve put his sticks down. There was no percussion in "Landslide," and Steve was smart enough to know that silence was a drummer's most powerful tool.

"I took my love, I took it down..."

Her voice cracked on the second line. Not a mistake — the crack was the song. Patty didn't smooth it over or pretend it hadn't happened. She leaned into it the way a good carpenter leans into the grain of the wood, letting the flaw become the feature. The congregation of empty pews received it. The saints in their niches received it. The dust motes floating in the evening light through stained glass received it and kept moving, indifferent and beautiful.

They worked for two hours. "Silver Springs" needed the most attention — the build from quiet verse to soaring chorus was the hardest transition, the place where a lesser band would either rush the crescendo or let the quiet parts die of neglect. Patty made them run the bridge eleven times until Robin's bass walk ascended in perfect parallel with her vocal line, two rivers meeting.

"Go Your Own Way" was the easiest. They'd played it a hundred times — at birthday parties, in Kristy's basement, at the Concord River cleanup where the only audience was a dozen volunteers in rubber boots. The song was in their muscles. All they had to do was let it out.

At eight o'clock, they packed up. Steve broke down his kit with the efficient motions of a kid who'd been setting up and tearing down drum sets since he was twelve. Robin coiled cables. Kristy stacked hymnals back into the pew racks.

"That felt different tonight," Robin said, zipping his bass into its case.

"Good different?" Patty asked.

Robin paused. Held the case in both hands. "Intentional different. Like you know something we don't."

Patty turned off the Fender amp. The hum died, and the church filled with the kind of silence that only exists in places built for it.

"I know what I want the music to do," she said. "That's all."

Robin looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and walked out into the parking lot where his mother's Buick idled under the security light.

Wednesday. 11:15 AM. The hallway outside the biology lab.

The between-class crush at Lincoln-Sudbury moved like cattle through a chute — four hundred kids in six minutes, shoulder to shoulder, sneakers squeaking on linoleum, the air thick with body spray and bubble gum and the low roar of voices saying nothing important at maximum volume.

Patty pressed against the lockers to let a group of Rampers pass. She had biology in three minutes and the textbook weighed six pounds and her locker combination had been sticking since February.

Nick Johnson came from the other direction. Walking fast, guitar case slung over his shoulder like a rifleman carrying his weapon, head down, the set of his jaw saying don't talk to me and the tension in his shoulders saying I dare you to try. He'd lost weight since she'd last really looked at him — cheekbones sharper, eyes darker, the softness of the boy she'd known at Elbanobscot burned away by two years of grief and a house where the mortgage was always a month behind.

He passed within arm's reach.

Patty hummed. Not deliberately — it was something she did, the way other people cracked their knuckles or tapped their fingers. A melody lived in her throat at all times, and the one that surfaced now was "Go Your Own Way." Five notes. The opening phrase. A reflex, not a message.

Nick stopped.

One step past her. Frozen. His shoulders tightened, then loosened, then tightened again — the micro-movements of a body fighting a memory it didn't want to acknowledge. He turned his head, not all the way, just enough to show his profile against the fluorescent light. Something in the melody had caught him. A hook snagged on a memory buried so deep he couldn't name it — a lake, pine trees, a girl's voice finding the space between his and Jackie's, a week when music hadn't been a war.

He looked at Patty. She looked back.

For one second the hallway noise fell away and they stood in the gap between past and present, two people who knew what the other sounded like at thirteen, what their voice did when it wasn't trying to prove anything. The recognition was there — faint, underwater, a shape seen through clouded glass.

Then Nick blinked. Shook his head, a half-motion, like shaking off a chill. He walked on. Guitar case bouncing against his hip. Gone around the corner before Patty could exhale.

She watched him go. The hallway refilled with noise. Lockers slammed. Somebody laughed too loud about something that wasn't funny.

Patty leaned against the cool metal of her locker and let the melody die in her throat. Five notes. She hadn't planned them, hadn't aimed them. But they'd landed. Somewhere behind Nick's eyes, in the part of his brain that remembered things his body had decided to forget, those five notes had made contact.

Whether it mattered was another question. Whether anything she did in six weeks could undo four years of silence and bitterness — that was a question she didn't have the right to answer.

She hefted her biology textbook and walked to class.

Thursday. 4:30 PM. Wentworth's music room.

Patty had come back for the guitar picks she'd left in the instrument cupboard during last period. The hallway was empty — after-school clubs had migrated to the gym, and the music room existed in the deep quiet that settled over unused classrooms like dust on a shelf.

She opened the door and heard the piano.

Jackie Marsh sat at the upright in the corner. Not the Steinway in the band room — the scarred practice piano that Wentworth kept for students who needed to work through something the Steinway was too pristine to hold. Jackie's back was to the door. His blazer was draped over the piano bench beside him. His sleeves were rolled to his elbows, and his hands moved over the keys with the kind of precision that costs ten thousand dollars in lessons — but something else was happening in the music. Something the lessons hadn't taught.

He was playing "Somebody to Love."

Not performing it. Not rehearsing it. Playing it the way a person writes in a journal — private, unpolished, honest. The runs weren't clean. He let notes ring too long or cut them too short, feeling his way through the song instead of executing it. His left hand found the bass line by instinct, not by chart, and the chord voicings were his own — darker than the original, more complex, stacked with dissonance that resolved into something aching and true.

"Can anybody find me somebody to love?"

He sang it quiet. Barely above the piano's volume. Not Freddie Mercury's operatic howl — Jackie's voice, stripped of the performance polish he wore like armor, raw and reaching, the voice of a boy who meant every word and wished he didn't.

Patty stood in the doorway. She didn't move. Didn't breathe loud enough to break the room's agreement with Jackie's playing — the old walls and the fluorescent lights and the instrument lockers all holding still, offering the boy what the rest of his life refused to give him: a space where he didn't have to be perfect.

The song ended. The last chord hung in the room for three seconds, then two, then one, then gone.

Silence.

"That was real," Patty said.

Jackie spun on the bench. His hands came off the keys like they'd been burned. His eyes went wide — not fear, exactly, but the wild exposure of someone caught without their costume, standing in their own skin for the first time in front of someone who could see them.

"How long have you been standing there?"

"Long enough."

He stared at her. His mouth opened, closed, opened again. She watched the armor rebuilding in real time — shoulders squaring, chin lifting, the careful construction of a boy who'd been taught that vulnerability was a weakness and musicianship was measured in precision, not feeling.

But underneath it, in the second before the walls went up, she saw something she recognized. The same thing she'd seen at Elbanobscot when Jackie played Queen in the music cabin and thought nobody was listening. The same dropping of the shoulders. The same breath.

Freedom.

Patty turned to leave.

"Patty."

She stopped. Looked back over her shoulder.

Jackie sat on the piano bench with his hands in his lap. The armor was up, but it had a crack in it — a thin line running from his throat to his chest where the song still lived.

"What are you doing here?" he asked. The question meant more than the words.

"Getting my picks," she said. "From the cupboard."

He nodded. Not satisfied, but unable to ask what he really wanted to ask.

She crossed the room, opened the instrument cupboard, found her picks in the small cardboard box where she kept them — three medium tortoiseshell Fenders, worn smooth at the tips. She put them in her jacket pocket.

At the door, she stopped again. Not for drama. Because something in her wouldn't let her leave without saying it.

"You don't play like that when anyone's watching."

Jackie said nothing.

"You should."

She walked out. The door closed behind her with a soft click. In the hallway, her boots sounded like a slow heartbeat on the linoleum.

The night before the Battle.

St. Michael's Church. 10 PM. Everyone gone.

Patty sat barefoot on the altar rug. The wool was rough under her feet — decades of knees and spilled communion wine and the slow wear of people kneeling to ask for things they already had. Her guitar sat in her lap. A Martin D-28 that her father had bought used from a music shop in Framingham when she was fourteen, the spruce top darkened with age, the neck worn smooth where her thumb rode the back during chord changes.

The church was dark except for the votive candles flickering in their red glass holders near the side altar. Thirty small flames making thirty small shadows that moved on the walls like the ghosts of prayers that hadn't been answered yet.

She didn't play. Not at first. She sat with the guitar and listened to the church breathe — the radiators ticking their cooling rhythm, the old wood popping in its joints, the wind pressing against the stained glass from outside, St. Michael's sword catching the last light from the parking lot in a thin line of amber.

Tomorrow she'd stand on a stage in front of nine hundred people. Nick would be there with his grief and his duct-taped guitar and the fury that passed for music when you couldn't say what you actually felt. Jackie would be there with his precision and his blazer and the voice he only used when he thought no one could hear. Eight bands. A war dressed up as entertainment. A town watching its children fight each other because adults couldn't figure out how to stop drawing lines.

She couldn't save them. She knew that. She'd known it since camp, since the last morning at Elbanobscot when they drove away in different cars toward different lives and she stood on the gravel path with her duffel bag and understood — at thirteen, with the certainty that only children possess — that the world would not make room for what they'd found.

But she could play.

She could stand on that stage and play music that didn't ask for anything — not victory, not vindication, not proof that one kind of life was worth more than another. She could play "Dreams" and mean it. She could play "Landslide" and let her voice crack on the second line because the crack was real and real was the whole point. She could play "Silver Springs" and let the chorus swell until the walls pushed back. She could close with "Go Your Own Way" and let the room decide what that meant — separation or invitation, goodbye or open door.

She couldn't fix two boys who'd been broken by a town that taught them friendship was a luxury they couldn't afford.

But she could remind them what they sounded like before they learned to be afraid of each other.

She placed her fingers on the fretboard. C-major to G. The opening of "Go Your Own Way." The pick caught the low E string and the note filled the church, one clean tone expanding outward through the empty pews and the votive shadows and the stone walls that had heard a century of prayers and confessions and knew the difference between performance and truth.

Patty played it through. Once. The whole song. Alone in St. Michael's with her bare feet on the altar rug and the candles burning down and the wind outside pressing against the colored glass like it wanted to come in and listen.

When the last chord faded, she didn't move. The silence that followed wasn't empty. It was the silence of a room that had received something and was holding it, the way a church holds everything — the grief and the gratitude and the stubborn human insistence that some things are worth singing even when nobody's listening.

She wasn't nervous. She wasn't hopeful. She was ready.

Tomorrow, she'd walk onto a stage and into a war she hadn't started, carrying nothing but a guitar and four songs and the memory of three voices that once found each other by a lake in the summer when they were young enough to believe that finding was the same as keeping.

She packed her guitar. Slid on her boots. Blew out the votives one by one — thirty small flames dying between her fingers, thirty small trails of smoke rising toward the ceiling in the dark.

She locked the church door behind her and walked into the May night.

The stars were out. The air smelled like cut grass and lilac and, faintly, from the southeast, the honest smell of home.

She hummed "Go Your Own Way" all the way to her mother's car.

CHAPTER 24: PATTY RETURNS

---

May 9th, 1979. St. Michael's Church, Sudbury.

The stained glass threw colored light across empty pews. Patty Lally sat alone in the sanctuary, listening to the silence that came after her bandmates packed up their gear and left. Four years since Camp Elbanobscot. Four years since she'd written seven letters to two boys who did write back—separately, telling different stories about the same events.

The letters sat in her camp notebook, folded and refolded until the creases threatened to tear. Nick's handwriting cramped and urgent, talking about his father's death like it was something he could resurrect if he played hard enough. Jackie's handwriting precise and measured, talking about classical competitions and college prep like music was a subject to be mastered, not a language to be spoken.

They'd both invited her to lunch over the years. Her house was a ten-minute bike ride from the school, and she'd gone. Not because she thought she could fix them, but because she was curious. What happened to the boys who used to make music like they were discovering something the world had forgotten?

Nick's lunches were at Friendly's. He'd order a tuna melt and talk about his guitar like it was the only thing that mattered. "I'm getting better," he'd say, fingers drumming on the table. "Mom says I'm obsessed, but she doesn't understand."

The guitar had become his father's ghost. Every note was a prayer to a man who'd never answer. Patty watched him eat, watched the way his hands moved even when they weren't holding strings, and thought: This isn't music anymore. This is grief wearing a guitar strap.

Jackie's lunches were at a café near his house. He'd order tea and talk about his father's expectations like they were weather—unavoidable, but temporary. "He wants me to focus on classical," Jackie would say, stirring sugar into his cup. "But when I play Queen, when I really play it, I can breathe. Does that make sense?"

The piano had become his prison. Every note was a performance for an audience of one—a father who'd never be satisfied. Patty watched him drink his tea, watched the way he measured every word before speaking, and thought: This isn't music anymore. This is seeking approval that will never come.

They were both changing for different reasons, but the result was the same. They were losing something. Something that used to be pure.

Patty leaned back in the pew, stared at the cross above the altar. Tomorrow night, she'd walk onto that stage at Lincoln-Sudbury and enter a war she hadn't started. Not to fix them. Not to make them friends again. Just to show them what music looked like when you weren't trying to prove anything.

Nothing to prove. Have some fun. See some friends. Good experience.

That's what she'd told her bandmates when they asked why she was entering the Battle of the Bands. But sitting alone in the church, with the letters in her lap and the silence pressing against her ears, she knew it was more than that.

She was free and she knew it, she had always been free with her music. At Camp Elbanobscot, when they'd harmonized by the lake, she'd felt it—the way three voices could become one thing, something that didn't belong to any of them individually but existed in the space between their breath. That's what music was supposed to be. Not a weapon. Not a prayer. Not a performance.

Just truth.

Tomorrow night, Nick would be playing for his father's memory, trying to resurrect a ghost with six strings and rough fingers. Jackie would be playing for his father's approval, trying to earn love with technical perfection and classical training. Both of them trapped in different prisons, both of them suffocating in the roles they'd chosen.

And she'd be there, alive.

The church was quiet except for the sound of her breathing. Outside, Sudbury slept under a sky that didn't care about battles or bands or boys who'd forgotten how to play music instead of proving points.

She couldn't stop the loss of innocence. No one could. It happened to everyone, eventually. The world taught you that music had to serve something—memory, approval, competition, success. It taught you that harmony was just preparation for war.

But she could refuse to be sucked into the vortex.

Tomorrow night, she'd walk onto that stage and play music the way it was meant to be played—not to prove anything, not to win anything, just because it made her alive. She'd remind everyone what air tasted like before it became something you had to earn.

Not to save them. They were already lost.

Just to show them what they were missing.

May 14, 1979. The night before Lincoln-Sudbury’s first Battle of the Bands, and across Sudbury's quadrants—northeast, southwest, southeast, and wherever hope was hiding—the nerves and neon anticipation ran hotter than muffler pipes. Three bands, three sanctuaries, three different rules about who got to walk away proud.

### NICK & WALKING THE LINE

Somewhere behind the gym and the dumpsters, auto shop bay three stank of hot metal, rubber, and sweat. Nick Johnson had grease on his jeans and a chip on his shoulder. His band—Stephen Murphy stringing the bass like a guy wringing out dishwater, David Cassel tight on the snare, Mark Pianka coaxing chords from a sticky Rhodes—was the last crew in town anybody bet on. Joe Pacenka watched from the door, arms folded, face carved from old wars and afterschool shifts, the sort of grownup who saw music as a way to keep the loud shadows away.

Their run-through was more debrief than rehearsal. No sheet music, no tuning forks; just ears and memory, fixing what could be fixed and ignoring what couldn’t.

"Walk This Way" came out like a dare—the riff deliberately imperfect, more message than melody.  
"Dream On" was all ache and ragged edges, the tempo breathing with their nerves, a song for every kid stuck in neutral.  
"Sweet Emotion" nearly ran off the rails before Nick threw it back on, more about heat than finesse.  
"Train Kept A-Rollin’" took the band past the edge and halfway toward the next county, a wall of sound fueled by sheer need.

Nothing was neat, but everything was vital. Nick kept them drilling sections till arms ached, sweat pooling on concrete, never quite saying aloud what he wanted: not applause, but proof they could still matter. The details they sweated were practical—tape on cables, a backup pick, making sure nobody’s amp shorted from a stray drip in the old bay roof. A band forged on shop time and stubbornness, their preparation was all instinct—no one wrote anything down, but everyone knew what was ride-or-die.

When the band filtered out—Stephen slapping him on the back, David already half-laughing at tomorrow’s catastrophe, Mark half-hoping for a miracle—Nick lingered, playing the solo Dan taught him. He was supposed to be doing what mattered one last time. But music, he couldn’t help feeling, sometimes just wasn’t enough. Not to bring Dan back, not to unbreak what living had broken. Still, the chords hung around his shoulders like an old winter coat—scratching, familiar, maybe the only thing left that felt like home.

### JACKIE & CROWN JEWELS

Up in the music wing, the Lincoln-Sudbury band room was bright as an operating theater, polished and antiseptic. Jackie Marsh presided at the Steinway, posture perfect even when nobody watched. Elizabeth tuned her violin by ear, Ace Callahan checked stick grips like a surgeon prepping tools, Max Wesslen noodled on bass, focused but loose. Their setup was pristine—fresh reeds, new strings, everything catalogued and counted. Here, mistakes were diplomatic incidents.

Their rehearsal unfolded like an audition for something bigger than Sudbury—every passage twice, then a third time, then everything again at half and full tempo.

"Bohemian Rhapsody" didn’t just get played; it was dissected, mapped, each tempo change corrected with the kind of meticulousness that said losing was not an option.  
"Somebody to Love" went under the microscope, harmonies tuned with a piano tuner’s exactness, gospel stripped clean until even the messy bits shone.  
"We Will Rock You" saw Ace and Max trading glances, pulse locked not just for rhythm but for the symmetry of applause.  
"We Are the Champions" sounded like a declaration written in gold leaf, but under it all, Jackie winced at a single flubbed note that nobody else heard.

Their preparation bordered on compulsive. Acoustics? Checked from every seat. Cables run and re-run—engineer’s tape marking precisely where microphones would be tomorrow. Jackie had lists and backup lists: who would start each song, how applause should be handled, the posture for the final bow. It looked effortless, but wore each of them ragged by the end. Perfection wasn’t just a standard, it was armor, and with each take, Jackie patched another seam in the suit he wore to keep his father’s disappointment from seeping in.

When the others left, Jackie stayed behind, pressing the Steinway’s keys with something close to panic. In the silence, the echoes boasted and mocked. Tomorrow, the stakes weren’t just the trophy, but finally answering what sort of music counted: the kind that soothed a town or the kind that might—once—let him breathe.

### PATTY & RUMOURS

Across the river, St. Michael’s Church hung thick with incense, rain through stained glass, and joy snagged in the rafters. Patty Lally sprawled barefoot across the worn altar rug, Robin Bosshard on bass, Kristy Wesslen flicking bright chords on an acoustic, Steve Bankuti drumming with borrowed brushes. No charts, no roadies, no plan, just four kids and faith that their sound—woven, unfussy—could fill the space between what was broken and what could still be healed.

Their prep wasn’t about precision but feeling—humming through each number until the chords disappeared under muscle memory.

"Dreams" floated up like a prayer, Patty bending melody until even the old saints would have smiled.  
"Go Your Own Way" never sounded angry—just free, even when off-beat.  
"Rhiannon" barely made sense, notes glimmering under pews that had seen every kind of confession.

They rehearsed in half-light, scrawling setlists on church bulletins, testing tempos with feet rapping against altar tile. Attention to detail came in different flavors: Kristy checked harmonies in the echo, Steve padded his kit with a torn hymn, Patty focused on eye contact, learning to trust the wave of a friend’s voice. If something broke, it was patched with duct tape and stubbornness. Their biggest anxiety? Whether the crowd would listen through a song’s quiet and not just wait for the next loud part.

When the others fizzled out into the parking lot, Patty played on, thumb cramping, still hunting the moment when music rose up and closed the wide gap between hope and history. She couldn’t save anyone, but she could at least remind them what was worth playing for.

### THE EVE

So went the night: three bands, three rooms, each chasing excellence by their own definition. For Nick’s crew, preparation was practical, almost superstitious, every run-through a fight against fate. Jackie and the Jewels measured their odds with engineering precision, every bar counted out, every mistake sealed away under polish. Patty’s Rumours built something looser, built of grace and gut, their greatest weapon the undimmed faith that music mattered even when nothing else did.

Outside, night pressed up against the school walls, washing the windows and shop doors with the kind of forgiving dark that hides as much as it reveals. Inside, the kids worked their knuckles sore, sweat finding every worry in their bones, each careful detail or reckless improv meaning more than any chord change on the page.

Because in Sudbury, tomorrow wasn’t just a concert—it was a reckoning. Those hours of practice, the attention to every knob and tremor and trailing note, said something about class and hope and what got handed down, broken, or polished until it shone. 

Tomorrow, spotlights would amplify every crack and every triumph. Maybe the music would heal old wounds. More likely, it would just hold them open long enough to hear the echo. But tonight—just tonight—three bands across three rooms burned for a little glory and a shot at rewriting the ending, hungry to believe that sound alone could bend the future.

And for a while, under yellow light and linoleum, that hope felt real enough to sing.

# CHAPTER 26: THE BATTLE BEGINS

May 15th, 1979. Seven o'clock on a Tuesday night, and the Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School auditorium was filling up with kids who'd been told this mattered.

Grace Johnson had changed at home before leaving, putting on her best dress—the one she'd worn to church and Dan's funeral and the middle school talent show when Nick won. She'd taken off early from her shift at Friendly's to see her son play tonight.

She drove the ten minutes to the high school, the parking lot already full. Grace found a spot in the back and walked toward the auditorium, smoothing down the fabric like it mattered how she looked.

Inside, the auditorium filled with eight hundred people—high school kids, parents, friends, siblings who'd come to see their brothers or sisters play. The Rampers claimed the front rows, their usual territory. The Rats took up positions along the back and aisles, boots up on seats. The Regs settled into the middle sections, trying to stay invisible. The Rollers were scattered throughout, like they'd forgotten there was supposed to be a system.

On stage, the judges' table sat front and center. Maxanne Sartori from WBCN, late-night voice who'd championed Aerosmith years before they made it big. Carter Alan, baby-faced, quick-eared. Bob Walker from Strawberries Records and Tapes, whose thick arms rested atop a clipboard like he was selecting the draft. And Chuck Morrison, WBCN's morning show MC, tall and frayed at the edges, exactly the sort of local star who could turn a town's ordinary into something worth missing your curfew for.

"Good evening, Lincoln-Sudbury!" Chuck boomed into the battered mic, sending feedback twining around the rafters. "Welcome to the first ever WBCN Battle of the Bands! Sponsored by WBCN, the Rock of Boston, and Strawberries Records and Tapes, the place to get your albums, cassettes, eight-tracks—and if you're lucky, immortality."

There was a laugh in his voice, and more than a few out near the fire doors. Eight bands tonight. Five hundred bucks. Real recording time at WBCN's studio in Boston. And one shot to show this town had more to offer than anyone expected.

Chuck worked his list: Maxanne, Carter, Bob—all given their due by the house, but when Bob Walker's name landed, a good portion of the crowd rose to their feet, clapping harder than anyone else. Studio time was golden. Plugging a local kid on the city's late-night radio? That was something to carry into adulthood.

"Here's your lineup, Sudbury," Chuck announced. "Let's see who's brave, who's foolish, and who—if anyone—can wake up this sleepy town."

The Jesters—freshmen, too new to the world to be self-conscious, all bright hope and Cars covers. Detroit Rock City—Acton-Boxborough's KISS disciples, face paint and fire, more comedy than chaos. The Dead Heads—Wayland's jam faithful, playing Grateful Dead by way of summer camp. Talking Heads Talking—Boston Latin's new wave vanguard: sharp suits, sharper nerves. Walking the Line—Nick Johnson's crew, Aerosmith loud and full of challenge. Crown Jewels—Jackie Marsh's outfit, Queen covers built from both sweat and inheritance. Rumours—Patty Lally's southeast Sudbury dreamers, conjuring Fleetwood harmonies. The Regulators—Framingham's Zep-worshippers, promising to break the windows with volume.

The Jesters opened the night with "Just What I Needed." It was careful, earnest, almost sweet—a reminder of late summer swings and Friday night sleepovers, but nothing to rearrange your understanding of the universe. Detroit Rock City stomped in next, everything sprayed silver, platform heels wobbling, blood packets spent by the second verse. There were mishaps: fire-breathing that mostly steamed, cake makeup drooping into the eyes. Still the crowd cheered, partly for the spectacle, partly in hope their own embarrassing attempts at cool weren't quite so obvious.

The Dead Heads rolled slow and warm through "Truckin'," crowd hypnotized at first, then gradually fidgeting as minutes stretched into what felt like months. It was only after seven minutes of "Dark Star" and a couple restless sighs from the judges' table that Chuck, mercy in his eyes, dimmed the lights: the old "wrap it up" signal. The applause that followed was brisk and forgiving.

After a break, Talking Heads Talking brought their own clinical weirdness—matching suits, nervous glances, "Psycho Killer" played within an inch of its life. A few older siblings in the crowd grinned knowingly while most kids waited for something they recognized, and the room's temperature settled somewhere between cautious and kind.

But by the time the fourth band had left the stage, you could feel it—the crowd getting restless. A few kids drifted toward the exits. Others checked their watches. The opening acts were done; if they didn't get the better bands on stage soon, people would start leaving. The mood wasn't electric—it was impatient.

Grace found her seat in the third row, on the left. She'd be able to see Nick clearly from there. Her purse sat in her lap, her hands gripping it the way she did when she was nervous.

Behind the stage curtain, Nick Johnson stood in the narrow space, watching the crowd through the gap where the velvet didn't quite meet the floor. His guitar hung from a strap his father had bought him three years earlier. The strap was worn smooth where Nick's thumb rested when he wasn't playing. A piece of leather gets soft and dark after years of being touched.

A random song came out of the PA speakers between acts—something Nick didn't recognize. He closed his eyes and heard his father's voice saying *Don't rush the notes, let them breathe*. That was eighteen months ago, before the cable snapped and four steel I-beams came down. Before Nick stopped being a kid who played guitar for fun and started being a kid who played guitar because it was the only thing that made the silence bearable.

The song ended. Nick opened his eyes. One more band before Walking the Line went on.

Through the curtain gap, he saw his mother in the third row. Grace looked small and tired and proud all at once, like she'd been carrying something heavy for a long time and tonight was the first night she might get to put it down. 

Dr. Richard Marsh sat three rows behind her. Jackie's father. He taught psychology at Boston University, which meant he spent his days studying human behavior and his nights applying those same principles to his own son. He'd published papers on adolescent development in academic journals that nobody read outside of university libraries. He understood motivation, expectation, performance anxiety. He understood everything about why people did what they did, except when it came to his own son.

He'd never written a paper about his own son, but Jackie suspected he'd thought about it. 

Across the backstage area, separated by extension cords and amplifiers, Jackie Marsh stood at the school's Steinway piano, running his fingers across the keys without making any sound. The Crown Jewels would go on sixth, after Walking the Line. Jackie had time to run through the parts that still felt uncertain. Not the notes themselves—those were perfect. He'd played "Bohemian Rhapsody" so many times he could do it with his eyes closed. The uncertainty was in the space between the notes, the places where he was supposed to feel something and instead felt nothing at all. 

Elizabeth tuned her violin, her fingers moving without thought. Ace Callahan spun his drumsticks, the wood clicking against itself. Max Wesslen adjusted the bass amp, testing levels that nobody would notice except the kid who'd been taught that every detail mattered. The Crown Jewels. Four kids who'd been practicing since they were old enough to hold instruments.

"Jackie."

Dr. Marsh stood in the doorway. His arms were folded the way they were during lectures.

"Fine," Jackie said. "Just nervous."

"Good. Nerves mean you're taking it seriously." Dr. Marsh adjusted his tie. "Remember, this isn't just about music. It's about showing people who you are."

Jackie nodded. That was the problem. He wasn't sure who he was when he wasn't trying to be who everyone else needed him to be. 

The guitar had been Nick's thing. The piano was supposed to be Jackie's thing. But somewhere along the way, the piano had stopped being about what Jackie wanted and started being about what Jackie thought everyone else wanted. Classical earned his father's approval. Rock earned his breath. He couldn't have both.

"You'll do fine," Dr. Marsh said. "You always do."

Jackie watched his father walk back toward the auditorium. The Crown Jewels would play Queen songs, and everyone would see exactly who Jackie Marsh was supposed to be—the kid with perfect technique, the kid who made difficult things look easy.

But the person he was supposed to be wasn't the person he was, and that was the problem. That had always been the problem.

Backstage, Nick heard the crowd react to whatever band was playing now. One more act before Walking the Line. In a few minutes, he'd be standing on that stage with Dan's solo running through his head. The solo his father had taught him one Sunday afternoon in the garage, both of them hunched over the guitar, Dan's rough hands guiding his until he got it right.

The guitar had become an obsession after Dan died. Nick practiced in the garage late into the evening, sometimes past midnight, until his mother came out in her bathrobe and asked him to stop. The neighbors might complain. Grace was tired. But Nick kept playing because it was the only thing that made the silence bearable, the only thing that made him feel connected to his father in a way that words couldn't touch.

"Walking the Line!" someone called from the stage. "You're up!"

Nick looked at Stephen, at Mark, at David. They were just kids. But they were his kids, his band, and for the next fifteen minutes, they were all he had.

He took a deep breath and walked toward the curtain, toward the stage, toward whatever happened next.

Behind him, Jackie watched Nick disappear through the curtain. The Crown Jewels would go on after Walking the Line. Jackie would play perfectly, because that's what he did. He'd make his father proud and his mother smile and everyone in the crowd would see exactly who Jackie Marsh was supposed to be.

But the person he was supposed to be wasn't the person he was, and that was the problem. That had always been the problem.

The crowd roared as Walking the Line took the stage. Jackie pressed his fingers against the piano keys and tried to remember what music had felt like when it was just about the music, when it wasn't about expectations or fathers or proving anything to anyone.

But that had been a long time ago. Before high school. Before everything got complicated.

Before he'd needed Nick for all the wrong reasons.

# CHAPTER 27: JACKIE'S PERFORMANCE

Jackie Marsh sat backstage at 8:15 PM, his hands shaking as he adjusted the tuning pegs on his Martin D-28. His fingers knew where every string should be, knew the exact pressure it took to make each note ring clear and true. Eleven years of classical training had done that. Eleven years of being told he was gifted, special, chosen.

Behind him, Crown Jewels was finishing their warm-up. Thirteen-year-old Elizabeth Marsh stood with her violin tucked under her chin, playing scales without looking at her fingers. Her face was serious, the way it got when she was concentrating on something that mattered. She was better than Jackie at this, had been since they were kids. Elizabeth played for herself. Jackie played to be enough.

Ace Callahan sat behind his drum kit, tapping out nervous rhythms against the snare. "You ready, Jack?" he asked, his voice too loud in the small backstage space.

Jackie didn't answer. He was running through chord progressions in his head, trying to remember what the music was supposed to feel like. But all he could think about was Nick, standing somewhere backstage waiting for Walking the Line's turn, probably not nervous at all, probably just excited to finally prove that working-class authenticity was better than purchased excellence.

The posters. The town choosing sides. The phone calls and the  teachers fanning flames and the way everything had been building toward this moment for months. This was what it had all been about—Nick and Jackie competing, the community watching, everyone waiting to see which  version of excellence would win.

But Jackie wasn't sure he wanted to win. He wasn't even sure what winning meant anymore.

"Five minutes," someone called from the wings.

Jackie stood up, his legs feeling foreign beneath him. He walked to the edge of the stage, peered through the curtain, looked out at the 800 people who'd come to see eight bands compete for a prize that didn't matter and a title that meant less. He saw his father in the front row, Dr. Richard Marsh sitting with perfect posture, his face a study in controlled pride. Jackie would either prove tonight that his father's investment had been worth it, or he would finally expose the doubt that had been eating him alive for months.

A question he'd been carrying since  Dan Johnson died two years ago, since his best friend had become his rival, since rock and roll stopped being something he did for fun and became something he had to win: Are you good enough? Are you really good enough, or am I just a parent who can't see your limitations?*

The thought made his stomach tighten. He'd spent his entire life trying to be enough. Enough for his father. Enough for his teachers. Enough for the system that had given him every advantage except the permission to fail.

Tonight, he would either prove he deserved those advantages—or finally expose the doubt that had been eating him alive.

"Three minutes." The voice from the wings was getting impatient now.

Jackie looked over at Nick, who stood on the opposite side of the stage, not looking at him, just staring at the floor with that expression that meant he was running through chord progressions in his head. Nick didn't have to worry about limitations. Nick just had to play. Nick's music was supposed to be raw and emotional because that's what working-class authenticity demanded. Jackie's music was supposed to be technically perfect because that's what privilege required.

But what if both expectations were wrong? What if the whole thing was wrong?

Jackie had spent four years of high school playing the part he was supposed to play. Classical training on Monday, Wednesday, Friday afternoons. Rock band on Tuesday and Thursday evenings. Academic excellence during the day. Rock and roll when the adults weren't watching. He'd learned to exist in both worlds, to be enough for both audiences, to prove that he could have it all.

But what if having it all meant having nothing?

What if Jackie wasn't supposed to be proving anything about privilege or technical perfection or whether eleven years of lessons had been worth it? What if he was just supposed to play Queen because he fucking loved Queen, because Freddie Mercury wrote songs that made him feel less alone when he sat at his bedroom Steinway after everyone was asleep, because "Somebody to Love" was the most honest song anyone had ever written about needing connection and Jackie had been singing it to himself for three years?

The thought hit him like a physical blow. He wasn't competing against Nick. He wasn't proving anything to his father. He wasn't representing privilege or technical perfection or purchased excellence. He was just a seventeen-year-old kid who loved Queen, who'd learned to play piano because his father wanted him to, but who'd fallen in love with rock and roll because he chose to.

And maybe that was enough.

"Two minutes."

Jackie went back to his band. Elizabeth was still practicing, her bow moving across the strings in patterns that looked automatic. Max was checking his bass, fingers pressing down on frets to test tuning. Ace was spinning his drumsticks, waiting.

"Hey," Jackie said, and they all stopped what they were doing and looked at him.

"We're not here to prove anything," he said, and his voice carried something it hadn't carried before. Certainty. Not confidence—certainty. "We're here to play Queen because we fucking love Queen. That's it. That's enough."

Ace smiled. Max nodded. Elizabeth lowered her violin and looked at her brother like she was seeing him for the first time.

"What if we don't win?" Ace asked, because Ace always asked the question everyone was thinking.

Jackie shrugged. "Then we don't win. But we'll have played Queen."

Ace's smile got wider.

"One minute." The voice from the wings was final now.

Jackie grabbed his guitar, walked to the center of the stage where the Yamaha grand piano sat waiting—the same piano from the locked music room, the same piano his father had helped persuade the school to purchase. But tonight, it wasn't his father's piano. It was his. Tonight, he wasn't playing for anyone's approval except his own.

The curtain went up. Eight hundred people. Eight hundred faces watching, waiting, expecting perfection.

Jackie sat at the piano, looked at Elizabeth and gave her a nod. She raised her violin. Ace sat behind his drums. Max picked up his bass.

And Jackie began to play "Killer Queen."

The opening notes filled the auditorium like Mozart on cocaine, like classical training meeting rock and roll soul, like eleven years of lessons becoming something Jackie had chosen to make his own. Elizabeth's violin soared above the piano in harmonies that Freddie Mercury would have loved, classical technique serving rock and roll instead of replacing it.

The southwest section erupted. This was what they'd come for—technical excellence, musical sophistication, proof that advantages could create something beautiful. Kids who'd grown up in the same neighborhood as Jackie, who'd had the same advantages, who understood that this was their validation too.

Dr. Marsh leaned forward in his seat, a smile breaking his controlled expression. *This is it. This is what eleven years of lessons produced. This is why investment matters. This is what I bought for him.*

But Jackie wasn't looking at his father. He wasn't looking at the southwest section or the judges' table or even at Nick in the wings. He was looking at his hands, at the keys, at the way his fingers moved without him telling them where to go. Eleven years of training, and finally he was using it for something he'd chosen instead of something he'd been assigned.

When "Killer Queen" ended, the applause was thunderous. But Jackie wasn't done. He wasn't playing for applause. He was playing for himself.

He switched to the guitar, strummed once to check the tuning, then began the opening chords of "Somebody to Love."

This was the song. This was the one he'd been practicing in his bedroom for months, the one he'd fallen in love with because it was honest in a way nothing else in his life was honest. Freddie Mercury singing about loneliness that Jackie understood, about needing somebody to love him, about the specific desperation that came from having everything except connection.

But now, on this stage, surrounded by 800 people who expected him to prove something, Jackie understood that he wasn't singing about needing somebody else to love him. He was singing about needing to love himself, about needing to stop choosing between classical approval and rock breath, about needing to be enough for himself instead of for anyone else.

"Somebody to love," he sang, and his voice carried the specific vulnerability that comes from being honest about what you need. Not technical precision. Not privileged protection. Just honesty.

The southwest section—his natural constituency, the kids who'd come to see proof that their advantages could create something better than Nick's authenticity—they were quiet now. This wasn't what they'd come for. They'd come for technical perfection, for purchased excellence, for proof that privilege could win. They'd come for Jackie to be better than Nick.

But Jackie wasn't trying to be better than Nick. He was trying to be honest with himself.

Nick's face changed. He'd been watching from the wings, and Jackie could see him through the stage lights, see the way his expression shifted from defensive to curious to something that looked like recognition. Like he was seeing something in Jackie he hadn't seen in four years.

The verse about loneliness, about needing someone to help him find the love he couldn't find alone—Jackie sang it like he meant it. Like he understood that loneliness wasn't about being alone. It was about being trapped in the wrong version of yourself, about choosing classical training because it earned approval when what you wanted was rock and roll breath.

*Classical earned his father's approval. Rock earned his breath. He couldn't have both.*

But he'd just proved he could. He was playing Queen with eleven years of classical training. He was technically perfect AND emotionally honest. He didn't have to choose. He could have both.

The bridge. The part where Freddie Mercury's voice breaks and all the desperation comes through. Jackie hit that note, and it wasn't perfect. It cracked. It showed. And that was the point. He wasn't hiding behind precision anymore. He was showing who he was.

The crowd responded to the crack in his voice. Not with confusion. With recognition. They knew what he was feeling—the desperation, the loneliness, the need for connection that had nothing to do with technical perfection.

When "Somebody to Love" ended, the auditorium was silent. Not the silence of confusion or disappointment. The silence of people who'd just witnessed something they hadn't expected to see. Not technical perfection. Not emotional manipulation. Just honesty. A seventeen-year-old kid on stage playing Queen because he fucking loved Queen, because the music mattered to him more than winning, because he'd figured out what he was fighting for.

Then the applause started. Not thunderous, not revolutionary, but real. Appreciation for someone who'd stopped trying to prove something and started being something.

Dr. Marsh was clapping, but his expression had changed. The controlled pride was gone. In its place was something Jackie had never seen on his father's face before. Not confusion. Not disappointment. Something like recognition. Like his father was seeing his son for the first time.

Jackie stood up, his legs shaky with adrenaline, and looked at his bandmates. Elizabeth was smiling. Ace was nodding. Max gave him a thumbs up.

Together, they walked off stage.

Backstage, the crew was setting up for the next band. Someone called Walking the Line was on next. Nick's band. Jackie should have been nervous about that, should have been worried about how Nick would compare. But he wasn't. He didn't care anymore.

Max clapped him on the shoulder. "That was amazing, man."

"Thanks," Jackie said, but he wasn't really listening. His mind was somewhere else, replaying what had just happened.

He hadn't played to prove he was better than Nick. He hadn't played to earn his father's approval. He'd played because he loved Queen, because the music mattered to him more than winning, because he'd finally figured out that he could be enough for himself.

In the wings, Nick was watching him. Not with jealousy or resentment or triumph. With something that might have been respect.

Jackie nodded once, then kept walking. He wasn't competing against Nick anymore. He'd found what he was fighting for. He'd found himself.

And that was enough.

## CHAPTER 28: NICK'S PERFORMANCE

### **Walking the Line (9:00 PM)**

And then it was time for Walking the Line.

Nick Johnson walked onto the stage carrying his pawn shop guitar with hands that showed calluses from more than just playing music. He wore his best jeans—which meant his least-torn jeans—and a black t-shirt that said nothing because it didn't need to. No costume, no pretense, no attempt to be anything other than exactly what he was: a seventeen-year-old boy from the wrong side of Hop Brook who'd been chosen to prove that talent didn't require a trust fund.

Behind him, Stephen Murphy took his position at the drums, his face serious with concentration. Tommy Morrison picked up his bass guitar, and Danny Kowalski settled behind the keyboard. They were Walking the Line, and they were about to prove that authenticity could be just as powerful as technical excellence.

Nick looked out at the audience, at the northeast section where his natural constituency sat, at the southwest section where Jackie's people applauded polish over passion. He saw Grace Johnson in the front row, her face a study in controlled pride, watching her son transform disadvantage into art.

And then he began to play.

### **"Walk This Way"**

The opening riff of "Walk This Way" filled the auditorium like a declaration of war. Nick's guitar was raw, unpolished, authentic in a way that made you believe every note was being played for the first time. This wasn't just an Aerosmith cover—this was Aerosmith distilled, purified, made real without losing its rock and roll soul.

The northeast section erupted. This was what they'd come to see—authentic rebellion, musical truth, proof that disadvantage could create something beautiful. Grace Johnson sat in the front row, her face a study in controlled pride, watching her son transform struggle into art.

But Nick was looking at Jackie, who stood in the wings, watching with an expression that was impossible to read. Was it admiration? Resentment? Recognition of something he'd lost?

### **"Dream On"**

For the second song, Nick chose "Dream On," and this was where the cracks began to show. The song was emotionally powerful—every note raw, every harmony honest, every moment authentic to the bone. But something was missing.

The polish.

The technical excellence that made Jackie's performance so impressive. Nick was singing about dreaming on, but he sounded like someone who'd never had the luxury of dreaming, never had the security to take risks, never had the advantages that made ambition possible.

Jackie's face changed. He wasn't resentful anymore—he was sad. He was watching someone he'd once called his best friend perform a song about dreams while carrying burdens that made dreaming feel like a luxury he couldn't afford.

### **"Hop Brook Blues"**

And then Nick did something nobody expected. He stopped playing Aerosmith covers and began to play something original.

"I wrote this last night," he said into the microphone, his voice carrying the particular vulnerability that comes from admitting you've been working on something personal. "It's called 'Hop Brook Blues.'"

The title was a reference to the stream that divided Lincoln-Sudbury, the stream that had become a metaphor for the divisions that separated the town. Nick's song was about those divisions, about how they'd destroyed his friendship with Jackie, about how they'd turned music into a weapon instead of a bridge.

*"Hop Brook Blues, running through my town*
*Hop Brook Blues, dividing us all down*
*Hop Brook Blues, separating friends*
*Hop Brook Blues, where the music ends"*

The song was raw, honest, vulnerable in a way that Nick had never been on stage before. He was singing about the weight of disadvantage, about the loneliness of being chosen to represent something larger than himself, about the pressure of proving that talent didn't require a trust fund.

*"I want to be real, I want to be true*
*I want to be someone who matters to you*
*Hop Brook Blues, running through my town*
*But the blues are crushing everything I do"*

When he finished, the auditorium was silent. Not the silence of confusion, but the silence of recognition. Everyone—Jackie included—understood that they'd just heard something real, something honest, something that transcended the divisions that had separated them.

The applause was thunderous, but Nick wasn't celebrating. He was looking at Jackie, and Jackie was looking back at him, and in that moment, they both understood that they'd just witnessed something important.

They'd both been wrong about what the other represented.

### **The Transformation**

As Walking the Line left the stage, something began to happen in the auditorium. The social boundaries that had defined Lincoln-Sudbury for years began to dissolve. Kids from the northeast section started talking to kids from the southwest section. The divisions that had separated them—quarters and comics, advantages and disadvantages, privilege and struggle—began to feel artificial, temporary, like costumes they'd been wearing without realizing it.

Grace Johnson stood up and walked over to Dr. Marsh, who was sitting in the front row with Elizabeth and Ace. They'd never spoken before, but now they were talking about their sons, about the music they'd just heard, about the possibility that maybe the divisions weren't as real as they'd thought.

Sarah Morrison, who'd been sitting in the northeast section with the other kids from the wrong side of Hop Brook, stood up and walked over to Tommy Morrison, who'd been sitting in the southwest section with the other kids from the right side of Hop Brook. They were cousins, but they'd never talked at school because they'd been assigned to different sections, different worlds, different versions of reality.

Lisa Henderson, who'd been sitting in the northwest section with the other kids who didn't know which side they were supposed to be on, stood up and walked over to Danny Kowalski, who'd been sitting in the southeast section with the other kids who were trying to figure out where they belonged. They'd never met before, but now they were talking about music, about the possibility that maybe the divisions weren't as real as they'd thought.

The auditorium was transforming. The Battle of the Bands was becoming something else entirely—a reminder of what was possible when people stopped competing and started connecting.

## CHAPTER 29: PATTY'S PERFORMANCE

### **Rumours (8:40 PM)**

There are nights when one song can shift the fault lines of a whole town—and then there are nights like this one, when it takes a girl with a secondhand guitar and a voice pitched somewhere between a force of nature and a gentle plea.

Patty Lally hadn’t grown up in Sudbury, but as she took the stage, barefoot on the dark riser, it didn’t matter where she called home. Her hair was loose, honey in the high school lights. She looked smaller than the others who’d come before—until she set her guitar’s shoulder strap and found the microphone. Then, all at once, she was the axis of the room.

No one expected strategy from Patty. No one expected revolution. They expected Fleetwood Mac covers and a nice voice, maybe something to fill the time until the next big act. Even Nick and Jackie, cooling their nerves in the wings, figured they knew how this set would go.

But Patty had always seen the big picture. Harmony, goodwill, connection—those weren’t abstractions to her. They were as real as splinters under her fingers or the ache in her throat after too many late nights practicing. She’d never chased technical perfection or dazzling arrangements. She just played what mattered, and let the music do the rest.

She strummed a simple, rolling C chord and smiled, brief and genuine, then stepped gently into “Dreams.”

---

### **"Dreams"**

If there was such a thing as communal breath, the auditorium held it.

Patty sang “Now here you go again, you say you want your freedom,” and it wasn’t Stevie Nicks’s smoky ache on the radio. It was something softer and more defiant—kid-sister to heartbreak, the memory of summer nights with windows cracked open and nothing to lose but sleep. She let the melody float, unfussy, as if the song were made for patched-together hearts. With every line, she reminded the crowd that freedom wasn’t about leaving—it was about letting others in.

By the second verse, even the chaperones in the aisles had gone still. There was no showmanship, no reach for applause. Just Patty, voice shimmering at the edges, bringing out something in everyone—the wish for things to be made right, or at least understood.

---

### **"Landslide"**

She didn’t speak between songs, just let her guitar flow into the next arpeggio. The first notes of “Landslide” fell over the audience like the first snow of winter—thin, sparkling, inevitable.

“I took my love, I took it down…” Patty nearly whispered, letting the words land. Some in the audience blinked a little harder, recognizing themselves. Every kid in the room who’d ever faced change—the ones about to leave for college, or lose friends to the slow drift of high school drama—heard that tremble in her voice and felt seen.

Her delivery was delicate but never fragile; it was sturdy with hope, the way children continue to believe despite what the world teaches. She didn’t play for points, or posture for the judges. Patty played as if each chord might mend a rift in the bleachers or heal some old argument in the wings.

---

### **"Silver Springs"**

From the soft hush of “Landslide,” Patty took a single deep breath, her knuckles pale around the guitar’s neck, and moved into “Silver Springs.” Something in her carriage shifted—still gentle, but now fiercely clear, the way boundary lines in small towns refuse to stay put.

She turned the refrain into a lament and a promise, her voice swelling with each chorus: “You’ll never get away from the sound of the woman that loves you.” Patty didn’t try to outdo the original; she didn’t need to. She let the pain and forgiveness in the lyrics shine, bright as a bell in the rafters. By her second time through, it was as if she were singing not only for herself, or for Nick and Jackie—whose attention hung on her every word—but for the whole room. For anyone who’d ever loved and lost, or fought to keep hope when bridges burned.

It was here that the revolution started, quietly, in the auditorium. In that moment, competition seemed so small; everyone was part of something larger, each voice invited into the refrain.

---

### **"Go Your Own Way"**

Last, her band joined her—Robin on bass, Kristy with harmonies, Steve patting out the heartbeat on snare. “Go Your Own Way” was the song everyone knew by heart. Patty let it roll loose, a little rough, all feeling. It was a lightning flash of forgiveness, defiance, and affection bundled up in three unruly minutes. Her friends’ voices met hers at the choruses, not perfectly, but with the kind of honest, cracked joy that only happens when no one’s worried about being impressive anymore.

By the end, even the foot-tappers in the back row were on their feet, not sure when they’d stood up. Maybe they’d been lifted—carried by something old and necessary in those melodies, and by the kind of open-hearted hope that Patty Lally always seemed to carry like a lantern.

When the final chord faded, the hush wasn’t disappointment—it was something closer to awe. Then: the applause. Not polite, but tidal. Chairs scraping, kids cheering, parents admitting something had happened here they’d remember all their lives. Patty blinked, honestly stunned, as if she’d expected a quiet ripple and been met by a rising sea.

For years people would remember that set. Later they might argue who was the best, whose chops were sharper or whose solos sparkled brightest. But they’d all agree on this: Patty Lally gave them the night’s truest gift, raising the music from contest to communion, turning rivalry into something hopeful and whole.

CHAPTER 30
THE ANNOUNCEMENT

The judges' room smelled like burned coffee and indecision.

Four folding chairs around a cafeteria table. Fluorescent lights that turned everyone the color of old newspaper. Maxanne Sartori sat with her back to the door, her fingers wrapped around a Styrofoam cup she hadn't drunk from in twenty minutes. Across from her, Carter Alan leaned forward on his elbows, jaw working like he was chewing something that wouldn't break down. Chuck Morrison had his legal pad covered in notes he kept crossing out. Bob Walker stood at the window, watching the parking lot fill with headlights as families arrived for the announcement.

"We've been at this forty minutes," Carter said.

"I'm aware." Maxanne set down the cup. A ring of coffee bloomed on the table beneath it.

"Walking the Line played the tighter set," Chuck said. He tapped his pen against the pad. "Johnson's guitar work in 'Dream On' — that wasn't a cover. That was a conversation with the original. The kid played like he had something to prove and nothing to lose."

"Crown Jewels had the superior arrangement," Bob said from the window, not turning around. "The Marsh kid's piano in 'Somebody to Love' — I've heard session players with less control. And his sister on violin? That wasn't rock. That was architecture."

Carter looked at Maxanne. Everyone always looked at Maxanne.

She'd been quiet through most of the deliberation. Listening. Cataloging. Running the same calculation she'd been running since both bands walked off stage — a calculation she already knew the answer to because she'd written the equation herself, six months ago, in a clause buried on page four of a contract no seventeen-year-old would read twice.

"How do you choose between authenticity and excellence?" she said.

The room went still.

Chuck stopped tapping. Bob turned from the window. Carter's jaw went slack for half a second before he caught it.

"Maybe you don't," Maxanne said.

She pulled the contract from her bag. Four pages, photocopied on the Sudbury High office machine, the toner already fading at the margins. She opened to page four and laid it flat on the table, her index finger landing on Section 7, Subsection C.

"'In the event of a tied decision among the judging panel, prize money shall be divided equally between tied parties. Studio recording time, as specified in Section 3, shall be designated as shared time, to be used collaboratively or forfeited in full.'"

Carter read it twice. "You wrote this."

"I wrote the whole contract."

"When?"

"October."

"Before you heard either band play."

Maxanne picked up her cup and drank the cold coffee. It tasted like pennies. "I heard them both play in 1977. At a middle school talent show. They did a mashup — Aerosmith and Queen. Two kids who had no business sounding that good together. I wrote the tie clause because I thought there was a chance — a small one — that they'd both be too good to choose between."

"That's not judging," Chuck said. "That's engineering."

"Yes," Maxanne said. "It is."

Bob Walker sat down. The chair squeaked against the linoleum. "So we're calling it a tie."

"I'm not calling anything. I'm asking you three a question: can you honestly pick one?"

Silence filled the room the way water fills a glass — completely, with nowhere for air to go.

Carter shook his head first. Then Chuck. Bob Walker looked at his hands and said, "No."

"Then it's a tie."

---

The auditorium held six hundred people and sounded like it held twice that. Nick Johnson stood stage left with Stephen Murphy, David Cassel, and Mark Pianka behind him. His guitar hung from his shoulder, the strap digging into a sunburn he'd gotten helping his mother reshingle the garage roof last weekend. Jackie Marsh stood stage right, Elizabeth beside him with her violin case clutched like a life preserver. Ace Callahan and Max Wesslen flanked them, Ace drumming his thighs with his palms.

Between the two bands: twelve feet of empty stage and a single microphone on a stand.

Maxanne walked to the microphone. The auditorium noise dropped to a murmur, then to breathing.

"This has been one of the finest competitions I've witnessed in my career," she said. Her voice carried the FM warmth that had made her famous — the voice that had introduced Aerosmith to Boston in 1973, that had turned unknowns into legends with nothing but a turntable and conviction. "Both bands performed at a level that would be remarkable for professionals. For high school musicians, it borders on the extraordinary."

She paused. Let the silence build. She knew how to use dead air the way a drummer knew how to use the space between beats.

"The judges' decision is unanimous. The 1979 Sudbury Battle of the Bands ends in a tie."

The auditorium inhaled.

Then the noise came — not cheering, not booing, but the confused, electric sound of six hundred people trying to process something that wasn't in the script. Murmuring swelled, broke into scattered applause, died back to murmuring. A few shouts from the back rows. Someone whistled, high and piercing.

"Prize money of five hundred dollars will be split equally," Maxanne continued. "Two hundred and fifty dollars to Walking the Line. Two hundred and fifty dollars to Crown Jewels."

She held up the contract. The paper caught the stage lights and glowed.

"One hundred hours of recording time at WBCN Studio C has been awarded as shared time. Per the competition contract, this time must be used collaboratively by both bands — or it is forfeited entirely."

Now the murmuring stopped. Now the auditorium understood.

Nick's head turned toward Jackie. Jackie's head turned toward Nick. Twelve feet of empty stage between them, and in that space, the ghost of every conversation they hadn't had in two years.

Maxanne stepped back from the microphone. "Gentlemen, would you come forward to sign the award acknowledgment?"

They walked toward center stage from opposite directions, meeting at the microphone stand like two tides converging. Maxanne held the contract between them. Nick took the left edge. Jackie took the right. They stood shoulder to shoulder, holding the same piece of paper, and neither one looked at the other.

Nick stared at the contract. The words swam. Two hundred and fifty dollars. His mother could use that. His mother could always use that. The studio time — a hundred hours in a professional recording studio — that was the real prize, and it came with a chain attached. Shared. Collaborative. With Jackie Marsh, who hadn't spoken to him since the funeral.

Jackie stared at the contract. Shared time. His father would want him to forfeit. Walk away clean. Take the money and the moral victory and pack for Juilliard. But the contract said collaborative, and some part of Jackie — the part that still heard Nick's guitar when he closed his eyes at night — knew what that word meant.

They signed. Separate pens. Same line.

In the fifth row, Grace Johnson stood.

She didn't applaud. She didn't shout. She stood the way she'd stood at Dan's funeral — straight-backed, dry-eyed, holding herself together with the same iron discipline that got her through double shifts and mortgage payments and PTA meetings where people looked at her with pity she hadn't asked for. Her Friendly's uniform was visible under her open coat, the brown and orange stripes faded from industrial washing. She'd come straight from work. She always came straight from work.

She watched her son on that stage, holding a contract with the Marsh boy, and she thought about Dan. Dan, who would have been in the front row. Dan, who would have been shouting loud enough to embarrass Nick. Dan, who would have grabbed both boys in a bear hug and said something like "That's how you do it" and meant every syllable.

Two hundred and fifty dollars. She could hear what Dan would say about that too. "Take it. We need it." No pride. Dan never had pride about money, only about work.

Grace sat down. She folded her hands in her lap and waited.

Three rows behind her and across the aisle, Dr. Richard Marsh unclenched his hands. He hadn't realized he'd been gripping the armrests. The wood had left red grooves across his palms. He stared at the marks, a psychiatrist cataloging his own stress response with clinical detachment, and then he looked up at his son.

Jackie stood in the stage lights with someone else's contract in his hands, and for one flashing, unbearable moment, Dr. Marsh saw himself. Twenty-two years old. A guitar in a case he was about to close for the last time. The rational choice. The correct choice. The choice that still woke him at 3 AM with his fingers curled around a fretboard that wasn't there.

He closed his hands again. Gently this time. The marks would fade.

---

In the back row, Patty Lally sat alone.

She hadn't won. She hadn't officially placed. Her name wasn't on the contract, wouldn't appear in the Sudbury Town Crier's write-up, wouldn't be mentioned when people talked about the Battle of the Bands at the diner tomorrow morning.

But she'd played "Go Your Own Way" on that stage with her eyes closed and her voice wide open, and for four minutes and thirty-eight seconds, the auditorium had belonged to her. Not to Nick's grief or Jackie's precision or the rivalry the whole town had been feeding on for months. To her. To a girl from a pig farm who sang Fleetwood Mac because Stevie Nicks understood what it meant to love something that was already leaving.

She hummed it now. Quiet. Just for herself. Go your own way.

People were filing out. The parking lot would be chaos — Suburbans and pickup trucks and Volvos all trying to exit through the same two lanes. Families clumping on the sidewalk, talking in the particular animated way that meant everyone had an opinion and no one was sure what had happened.

Patty watched them go. She didn't move. She'd wait until the auditorium was empty, then walk home. Three miles on Route 20, past the dairy farm and the reservoir, humming the whole way.

She hadn't won anything. She hadn't lost anything either.

---

Outside, the March air was sharp enough to sting. Nick and Jackie emerged from separate doors, the contract between them like a custody agreement for something that hadn't been born yet. The town spread below the high school's hill — lights in kitchen windows, television glow through curtains, the distant hiss of Route 9 traffic.

Somewhere in that town, two hundred and fifty dollars would pay a car insurance bill. Somewhere else, two hundred and fifty dollars would sit in a savings account already thick with Juilliard money, barely noticeable.

A hundred hours of studio time hung in the air between them, unclaimed.

The town held its breath. The antenna on the WBCN tower blinked red against the clouds, slow and steady, like a heart that hadn't decided yet whether to keep beating.

What happens now.

CHAPTER 31
THE GARAGE — MIDNIGHT

Stephen Murphy's truck smelled like wet dog and transmission fluid. Nick sat in the passenger seat with his window cracked two inches, letting the March cold knife across his face. The parking lot had mostly emptied — a few cars idled near the gymnasium entrance, parents loading instrument cases into trunks, the last knots of conversation unwinding under the sodium lights.

Stephen had gone inside twenty minutes ago to help David load the drum kit. Nick said he'd wait. He didn't want to walk back through the auditorium. Didn't want the handshakes, the "great job" from people who meant it and the "great job" from people who didn't. He wanted to sit in a truck that smelled like Stephen's golden retriever and stare at the windshield until the anger burned down to something he could carry.

A tie. Two years of work, two years of building Walking the Line from a garage rehearsal into something real, and the judges couldn't pick a winner. Split the money. Share the studio time. Like two kids fighting over a toy and the teacher saying "you'll just have to take turns."

Two hundred and fifty dollars. Grace would put it toward the Buick's engine repair. She'd been nursing that car through winter on prayer and starting fluid, and the mechanic on Route 20 had told her last month that the timing belt was living on borrowed time. The money wouldn't fix it. The money would delay the inevitable.

Knuckles on the passenger window.

Nick's hand went to the door lock before he saw who it was. Jackie Marsh stood outside the truck, his breath ghosting in the cold, his collar turned up. He wore his father's peacoat — Nick recognized it from a hundred Saturday mornings when Dr. Marsh dropped Jackie off for Little League, back when they were twelve and the world was simple.

Nick rolled the window down another three inches.

Jackie held up the contract. The paper was already softening at the folds from being handled. "We have to decide about the studio time."

"You take your half."

"Doesn't work that way." Jackie turned the contract toward the sodium light and pointed at the clause. "Says 'shared time.' We use it together or forfeit."

"Then we forfeit."

"A hundred hours, Nick. In a professional studio. With an engineer."

"I know what it is."

"Do you know what it's worth?"

Nick looked at him through the window gap. Jackie's face was half-lit, half-shadow — split down the middle like a mask, like the two versions of himself he'd been running since middle school: the classical prodigy his father built and the rock musician his hands kept insisting he was.

"What do you want me to say?" Nick asked.

"I want you to get out of the truck."

---

They walked three blocks to Maple Street without speaking. The town was quiet the way small towns get quiet after eleven — not silent, but muffled. Dogs behind fences. A television's blue flicker through a second-story window. The Hendersons' porch light left on, moths batting against the glass like tiny drunks trying to get into a bar.

Nick's garage sat at the end of the driveway, detached from the house, the door closed. He hadn't been inside in a week. The band rehearsed at Stephen's now — Stephen had a finished basement with carpet samples on the floor and egg cartons on the walls for soundproofing. The garage was where Nick played alone, when he played alone, which was less and less.

He pulled the door up. The springs groaned. The smell hit them both.

Sawdust and motor oil. WD-40 and cold concrete. Dan Johnson's workshop, preserved like a museum exhibit nobody had curated on purpose. His tools hung on pegboard in the outlines Grace had drawn with a Sharpie so Dan would put them back in the right places — a system Dan had ignored and Grace had enforced and that now served as a kind of shadow portrait of the man, each outline a ghost of the tool it held.

The workbench ran along the back wall, covered with a vinyl tablecloth Grace had put down after the funeral to protect the surface Dan had sanded and sealed himself. Milk crates stacked in the corner held extension cords, coffee cans of screws, a transistor radio with a cracked dial that still picked up AM if you held the antenna at the right angle. Nick's amplifier sat on a moving blanket near the water heater. Two folding chairs. A music stand with a broken hinge.

Jackie stopped in the doorway.

He hadn't been here since the summer before eighth grade. The summer of the Brubeck album, the summer they'd spent three weeks learning "Take Five" because Jackie said the time signature was impossible and Nick said impossible was just a word for things that hadn't been done yet. Dan had brought them lemonade in Mason jars and sat on the workbench and listened, his boots swinging, sawdust in his beard, grinning like a man watching a magic trick he couldn't explain.

"It's the same," Jackie said.

"Yeah." Nick plugged in his amplifier. The tubes warmed with a low hum. "Sit down or go home."

Jackie sat. He pulled the folding chair to the middle of the floor and looked around for a keyboard. There wasn't one. But leaning against the workbench, behind a bag of road salt, was the Casio — the same cheap electric keyboard Jackie had left here in 1976, its power cord coiled on top like a sleeping snake.

He plugged it in. Three keys stuck. Middle C had a crack that changed its timbre to something between a piano and a harpsichord. He pressed it twice, listening. It sounded like memory.

Nick strapped on his guitar. A '72 Stratocaster, tobacco sunburst, bought with paper route money and a loan from Dan that Nick was still repaying to a dead man. He hit an E chord. The garage walls shivered.

"What do you want to play?" Jackie said.

"'Walk This Way.'"

"Fine."

Nick counted off — one, two, three, four — too fast. Anger tempo. His right hand attacked the riff, all downstrokes, percussive and mean, the way Joe Perry never played it because Joe Perry had groove and Nick had fury. The garage filled with sound. Too much sound. The notes piled up like cars in a chain collision.

Jackie came in on the keyboard, but he played it controlled. Too controlled. He flattened the melody into something clean and mathematical, stripping the swing out of it, making it precise the way his Juilliard audition piece was precise — every note accountable, no room for accident or breath.

They crashed into each other at the chorus. Nick pushed faster. Jackie pulled tighter. The song buckled between them like a bridge with traffic moving in both directions.

Nick stopped. The last chord rang out and died against the concrete floor.

"That was garbage," he said.

"Your tempo was—"

"My tempo was fine. You played it like sheet music."

"You played it like a bar fight."

They stared at each other across the garage. Six feet of oil-stained concrete. The amplifier hummed between them.

"'Bohemian Rhapsody,'" Jackie said.

"No."

"You said I could pick."

"I didn't say that."

"It's implied. You picked first."

Nick exhaled through his teeth. "'Fine."

Jackie counted off. Proper tempo. The opening piano chords — Is this the real life, is this just fantasy — and his hands knew the piece so well they moved without instruction, the way a tongue knows its own mouth. But he played it too carefully. Too respectfully. He played it the way you play something you're afraid of breaking.

Nick came in on guitar for the operatic section, translating Brian May's harmonics into single-string runs that spiraled up the fretboard. But he overplayed. He dug in too hard, tried to bend the song toward something rawer than it wanted to be, and the notes came out strained and sharp, like a voice cracking under pressure.

The song fell apart at the hard rock section. Nick went heavy. Jackie went theatrical. They sounded like two different bands playing two different songs in the same room.

Silence.

"We can't do this," Jackie said.

"No."

"We sound like strangers."

"We are strangers."

The amplifier hummed. A car passed on Maple Street, headlights sweeping across the garage window and vanishing. Somewhere in the house, a faucet ran and stopped.

The side door opened.

Grace stood in the frame, backlit by the kitchen. Her Friendly's uniform still on — brown polyester, name tag, the white sneakers she'd bought at Bradlees with the insoles she replaced every two months because twelve-hour shifts on tile floors ate through foam like acid. Her hair was down, which meant she'd already taken out the pins she wore for work. Her face was tired the way it was always tired now, a tiredness that went past sleep into something structural.

She looked at Nick. She looked at Jackie. She looked at the Casio plugged in on the floor.

"Your father would've liked that," she said. "Both of you winning."

She went back inside. The door closed with a click.

---

The silence that followed was different from the silences before it. Those had been walls. This one was a window.

Nick sat on the edge of the workbench. He put his guitar in his lap and rested his hands on the strings, muting them. Jackie stared at the closed door where Grace had been.

"She's right," Jackie said. "Dan would've liked it."

"Don't talk about my dad."

"He used to bring us lemonade."

"I said—"

"In Mason jars. With too much sugar. And he'd sit right where you're sitting and he'd listen to us play like we were the greatest band he'd ever heard, and we were thirteen and terrible."

Nick's throat closed. He swallowed against it. The workbench was solid under him, real — Dan had built it from reclaimed oak, sanded it so smooth you could run your palm across it without catching a splinter. Nick could feel the grain through his jeans.

"Remember the Brubeck thing?" Jackie said. "Summer before eighth grade?"

Nick's hands loosened on the strings. A harmonic escaped — thin, bright, gone.

"I remember."

"'Take Five.' You said the time signature was impossible."

"You said that."

"Did I?"

"You sat right there and said 'Five-four time is impossible, nobody plays in five-four' and I said 'Brubeck does' and you said 'Brubeck is an alien.'"

Something happened to Jackie's face. Not a smile — too much scar tissue for that — but the muscles around his eyes shifted, loosened, and for a second he looked like the kid who'd sat in this garage with lemonade in a Mason jar and believed that music was the only honest language.

"Want to try?" Jackie said.

"Try what?"

"You know what."

Nick did know. The mashup. Summer of '76. "Sweet Emotion" into "Bohemian Rhapsody" — they'd spent three afternoons working out how to bridge them, and the bridge had been Nick's idea: the Aerosmith riff transposed into D minor, which was the key center of the Queen ballad section. It shouldn't have worked. It worked.

Nick counted off. Slower this time. Not angry. Not controlled. Just honest.

His guitar found the "Sweet Emotion" bass line — the talk-box riff, low and grinding, but he played it clean, without distortion, letting the notes breathe the way Dan used to say you let wood breathe before you sealed it. The riff looped. Once. Twice. Finding the groove.

Jackie came in on the Casio. Not with "Bohemian Rhapsody" — not yet. He played a chord. Just one. D minor. He let it hang. Then he played it again, and this time he added a ninth, and the chord opened up like a door into a room that had been locked for two years.

Nick heard it. His hands responded before his brain could object. The riff shifted — same pattern, different color — and suddenly the Aerosmith groove was sitting inside the Queen harmony, and the two songs were breathing the same air.

Jackie added the melody. Tentative. "Is this the real life" over the bass groove of "Sweet Emotion," and it shouldn't have worked, it was two different decades and two different bands and two different ideas about what rock and roll was for, but the notes found each other the way old friends find each other in crowded rooms — not by looking, but by listening.

Nick opened up the guitar. Not loud. Not aggressive. Just present. The Stratocaster sang the way it only sang when he forgot to be angry, when his hands stopped fighting the instrument and started talking to it. The riff evolved. Fragments of "Walk This Way" crept in, not as a separate song but as a texture, a color, a thread in the weave.

Jackie felt it happen. The thing he couldn't explain to his father, couldn't write on a Juilliard application, couldn't translate into the language of classical theory. The blend. The frequency where his piano and Nick's guitar stopped being two instruments and became one voice with two throats.

The tools vibrated on the pegboard. A screwdriver walked to the edge of its hook and hung there, buzzing. The garage breathed — old wood expanding, concrete humming, the air itself thick with overtones that neither instrument was producing alone.

They played for eleven minutes. No structure. No arrangement. Just two musicians remembering what their hands had always known and their lives had made them forget.

When they stopped, the silence rang.

Nick's fingers trembled on the fretboard. Adrenaline. Or something older.

"That's what we could have been," he said.

Jackie looked at him. Two years of silence. Two years of choosing sides and crossing streets and pretending the other didn't exist. "We could still do this."

The phone rang.

Nick stared at it. The kitchen phone, audible through the side door Grace had left unlocked. It rang four times. Five. Grace's footsteps on the linoleum, then her voice, muffled by the wall.

The side door opened. Grace, one hand on the frame, the receiver stretched to the end of its cord.

"It's for both of you," she said. "The woman from the radio station."

Nick took the phone. Maxanne's voice came through like a broadcast — clear, warm, carrying the particular urgency of someone who had been listening to something she wasn't supposed to hear.

"Get down to the station," she said. "Now."

"It's midnight."

"I know what time it is. Bring whatever you just played."

She hung up. Nick held the dead receiver and looked at Jackie.

"WBCN," he said. "She wants us there now."

Jackie looked at the Casio. At the garage. At the tools on the pegboard, still trembling.

"Okay," he said.

Grace drove. She didn't ask questions. She drove the way she did everything — forward, steady, both hands on the wheel of a Buick with a dying timing belt, into the Boston night.

CHAPTER 32
STUDIO C

The hallway to Studio C smelled like tape oxide and stale cigarettes. Maxanne walked ahead of them, her boot heels cracking on the linoleum, not looking back to see if they followed. She'd been waiting in the lobby when Grace's Buick pulled into the lot — standing under the WBCN sign with her arms crossed and her leather jacket zipped to her throat, looking less like a radio legend and more like a woman who'd just bet something she couldn't afford to lose.

Grace stayed in the car. She'd said "Go" the way she said everything — once, without elaboration — and Nick had gotten out and found Jackie already standing on the sidewalk, his hands in his peacoat pockets, breath clouding, waiting.

It was 2:14 AM.

Studio C was smaller than Nick expected. He'd imagined something palatial — walls of equipment, a mixing board the size of a car, the kind of room where Aerosmith or the Cars or the J. Geils Band laid down the tracks that Maxanne spun into gold. Instead: a room the size of his mother's living room. Acoustic panels on the walls like quilted armor. Two microphones on boom stands, facing each other, four feet apart. A window into the control room where a kid in a Red Sox cap sat behind a board that was, in fact, the size of a small car.

"Mike," Maxanne said, knocking on the glass. "They're here."

The kid looked up. Twenty-five, maybe. Wire-rimmed glasses. The kind of enthusiasm that radiated off him like heat from an engine. He gave a thumbs up through the glass and started flipping switches. Lights changed on the board — red, green, amber — and the room began to hum with the particular frequency of electronics waking up.

"Two mics," Nick said. "Where's the amp?"

"DI box." Maxanne pointed to a small metal rectangle on the floor near the right microphone, a cable snaking from it to the wall. "Plug your guitar straight in. Mike will handle the tone from the board."

"I've never played through a DI."

"Tonight you have."

Jackie stood in the center of the room, turning slowly. An upright piano sat against the far wall — not a Casio with cracked keys, but a Yamaha U3, its black lacquer reflecting the overhead lights in long bright streaks. He lifted the fallboard. The keys were perfect. Weighted. Responsive. He pressed middle C and the note bloomed through the room with a clarity that made the garage version sound like it had been played through a blanket.

"Sit," Maxanne said, pointing Jackie to the piano bench and Nick to a stool near the DI box. "Tune up. Mike needs five minutes for levels."

She walked into the control room and closed the door. Through the glass, Nick watched her lean down to Mike's ear and say something. Mike nodded. His hands moved across the board.

Nick plugged into the DI box. Without an amp, his guitar sounded thin and naked through the monitor speakers — all string and no body, like a voice without a chest behind it. He played a chord. The monitors shaped it, added warmth, gave it room. Mike's hands on the board. The kid knew what he was doing.

Jackie ran scales. His fingers moved on the Yamaha the way a swimmer moves in open water — long strokes, effortless, the technique so internalized it had become invisible. The piano responded like it had been waiting for him.

Maxanne's voice came through the overhead speakers, studio-clear: "Here's the deal. One take. No overdubs. No punch-ins. What you played in that garage — play it here."

Nick looked at Jackie through the space between the two microphones. Four feet. "She was listening?"

Jackie's hands stopped on the keys. "How?"

Maxanne: "Grace's kitchen window was open. I called the house ten minutes before I called you."

Nick thought about that. His mother's kitchen window, cracked two inches because the radiator made the room too hot. The sound of their playing carrying through the gap, across the yard, through the phone line to Maxanne's ear. Like a frequency searching for a receiver.

"One take," Jackie said.

"One take," Maxanne confirmed.

Mike's voice, younger and less steady: "Levels are good. Whenever you're ready."

Nick and Jackie looked at each other. Four feet of studio air between them. Two microphones. Two instruments. Thirty years of music between them if you counted forward from this moment, which neither of them could do.

"You start," Nick said.

Jackie put his hands on the keys. He closed his eyes. For three seconds, nothing. Then:

D minor. The chord that had opened the door in the garage. But here, on the Yamaha, in the acoustic treatment of Studio C, the chord didn't just open a door. It knocked down a wall. The overtones spiraled into the room and hung there, suspended, ringing, alive.

Nick's hands found the bass line. "Sweet Emotion" — the riff he'd played a thousand times in the garage, at rehearsals, alone at night when sleep wouldn't come and music was the only thing that kept the dark from getting too close. But he played it different now. The DI stripped away the distortion, the amp growl, the feedback that masked imperfection. What was left was the melody underneath — clean, exposed, vulnerable. The bones of the song without the muscle.

Jackie heard it and responded. His left hand picked up the riff, doubled it an octave higher on the piano, and for four bars they played the same line in unison — guitar and piano, the same phrase, the same breath. Then Jackie's right hand drifted upward, and the Queen melody entered.

"Is this the real life." Not sung — played. The piano carried the vocal line the way a river carries a leaf, without effort, without destination. And underneath it, Nick's guitar held the Aerosmith groove, steady and patient, a foundation the melody could trust.

The songs merged. Not blended — that was too gentle a word. They merged the way tributaries merge, each one losing its name but not its current, the combined flow stronger than either stream alone. The "Sweet Emotion" groove became the heartbeat. The "Bohemian Rhapsody" melody became the breath. And in the space between — the harmonic space that no textbook described and no theory explained — something new existed. Something that belonged to neither Aerosmith nor Queen but to two boys in a studio at 2 AM who had found, in the overlap of their separate obsessions, a sound that was theirs.

Nick's eyes were closed. He didn't need to see the fretboard. His fingers knew the way. The riff evolved — "Walk This Way" crept in, Tyler's strut translated into guitar, and then the Aerosmith and Queen fragments wove together, trading phrases, answering each other, carrying on the conversation they'd started in a garage when they were thirteen and didn't know enough to be afraid.

Jackie opened the piano up. The Yamaha's full range — bass to treble, thunder to glass — and he used all of it. The operatic section of "Bohemian Rhapsody" arrived not as theater but as architecture, each chord a room inside a house the two of them were building in real time. Galileo. Figaro. Magnifico. The words were absent but the structure was there, and Nick's guitar sang the counter-melody that Brian May had written for three overdubbed guitars, except Nick played it on one, and the single line cut through the piano chords like light through a prism.

Four minutes. The music breathed. It stumbled once — Nick's fingers slipped on the twelfth fret, caught themselves, turned the mistake into a bend that Jackie matched with a crushed note on the piano — and the stumble became part of the song, a crack that let the light in, proof that this was live and real and happening now and would never happen again in exactly this way.

The hard rock section hit. Nick dug in. Jackie hammered the keys. For thirty seconds they played loud enough to rattle the acoustic panels, the monitors feeding back the sound in a loop that built and built until it crested and broke, and then —

Silence.

Two beats of silence.

Jackie played the final chord. D minor again, where they'd started, but this time with the sustain pedal down, the note ringing into the room, fading like the last light of a day neither of them wanted to end.

Nick lifted his hands from the strings. The monitors hummed. The tape rolled.

Four minutes and forty-three seconds.

---

Through the glass, Mike sat motionless, his hands flat on the board. His mouth was open. Maxanne stood behind him, one hand on the back of his chair, the other pressed flat against the glass.

She pressed the talkback button. "Stay there."

She walked into the studio, crossed to the tape machine, and rewound. The reels spun backward with a whirring sound that filled the room like a held breath being taken back.

"Listen," she said, and pressed play.

The sound came through the monitors — their own playing, returned to them, both familiar and strange the way a recording always is, because a recording captures not just the music but the room, the air, the moment, and hearing it played back is like watching yourself through a window.

Nick heard his guitar and didn't recognize it. The DI had captured something the amplifier always hid — a clarity in his phrasing, a tenderness in the way he bent notes, a quality he'd been taught to bury under volume because volume was what rock guitar was supposed to be. Without the distortion, he sounded like himself. Not like Joe Perry. Not like Jimmy Page. Like Nick Johnson, seventeen, playing in the middle of the night because the music asked him to.

Jackie heard his piano and felt his chest tighten. The Yamaha had captured everything — the classical training, the rock instinct, the war between them, and the moment where the war ended and the two traditions stopped fighting and started listening to each other. He sounded like no one he'd been trained to sound like. He sounded like what he'd been afraid to become.

The recording ended. The monitors hissed. Tape silence.

Jackie pressed his fingers to his eyes. His shoulders drew up. When he dropped his hands, his face was wet.

"This is what we could have been," he said.

Nick looked at him. The wetness on Jackie's face caught the studio lights.

"We could still be this," Nick said.

Maxanne leaned against the tape machine. She crossed her arms. She looked at both of them with the expression of a woman who had been in rooms where music happened for thirty years and knew exactly what she'd just witnessed and exactly what it cost.

"You could walk away from everything," she said. "Juilliard. The Army. Forget the plan. Choose this."

The room changed. The air thickened. The hum of the monitors became the only sound, and it sounded like a question that had been asked a hundred thousand times in a hundred thousand rooms where young musicians sat across from the choice between what they were and what they were supposed to become.

Nick looked at the clock on the wall. Digital. Red numbers. 3:17 AM.

He thought about Grace.

She was in the parking lot. Right now. At 3:17 in the morning, in a Buick with a dying timing belt, wearing a Friendly's uniform she'd been in since six that morning. Twenty-one hours. She'd driven him here without asking why, without complaining, without mentioning that she had to be back at the restaurant in five hours for the Sunday brunch shift that she'd volunteered for because Sundays paid time and a half.

She'd stood up at the PTA meeting when Dr. Taub tried to cut the music program. She'd stood alone — the only parent in the room in a work uniform, the only one without a college degree, the only one who couldn't cite studies or statistics — and she'd said, "My son plays guitar. That's all I know. But you should hear him." And the room had gone quiet, and the program had survived.

She'd confronted Dr. Taub again at the school board meeting when the Battle of the Bands funding was threatened. She'd walked up to the microphone during public comment and said, "This matters," and when Dr. Taub asked her to elaborate, she said, "No. It just matters," and sat down, and three people in the audience had started clapping.

She worked doubles so Nick could have a childhood that wasn't consumed by the same survival calculus that consumed hers. She cashed her checks and paid the mortgage and kept the house Dan had built and never once said the words that Nick knew she thought every night: I can't do this alone.

He couldn't tell her he was walking away from college. He couldn't tell her he was giving up the Army — the tuition, the steady pay, the health insurance, the path out of the working-class bargain that had killed his father — to chase a sound in a studio at three in the morning. He couldn't walk out to that parking lot and lean into the Buick's window and say, "Mom, I'm going to be a musician," and watch her face do the thing it did when she was terrified but wouldn't show it.

He couldn't. Not because he didn't want to. Because she had already given everything, and asking for more was a kind of theft he wasn't capable of.

Jackie sat at the piano bench. His hands rested on his thighs, away from the keys, as if touching them might make the decision for him.

He thought about his father.

Fourteen years of lessons. Every Saturday, the drive from Sudbury to Boston — forty minutes each way through Route 9 traffic, Dr. Marsh's Volvo smelling like leather and the Bach cantatas he played on the tape deck because he said Bach was the foundation of everything. Fourteen years of recitals, competitions, auditions. The Juilliard application his father had helped him write — three drafts, each one more careful than the last, each one a collaboration between a son who played and a father who had stopped playing and never said why.

Dr. Marsh had saved for Juilliard since Jackie was five. A separate account. Monthly deposits. Jackie had found the bankbook once, in the desk drawer in his father's study, and the column of numbers had stretched across three pages, twelve years of deposits, and next to each one, in his father's precise handwriting, the date and the amount and nothing else. No notes. No annotations. Just the numbers. As if the act of saving were its own explanation.

His father had given up the guitar. Packed it in a case and put it in the attic and chosen the career, the stability, the rational path. And every day since, he'd been pouring whatever he'd sacrificed into his son — not the same dream, but a version of it, translated into classical training and Juilliard and the life of a professional musician that Dr. Marsh believed was the safe version of the dangerous thing he'd wanted.

He was wrong. Jackie knew that. His father believed in the wrong version of him — the recital version, the competition version, the version that played Chopin with impeccable technique and empty hands. But the belief itself was real. The fourteen years were real. The drives, the deposits, the three drafts of the application. His father had built a bridge from his own abandoned dream to his son's future, and Jackie couldn't burn it. Not even for this.

"I can't," Nick said.

Jackie closed his eyes. "Me either."

The words fell into the room like stones into water. The ripples spread to the walls and came back changed.

"It's not giving up," Nick said. His voice was rough. He cleared his throat and it didn't help. "It's choosing the people who already chose us."

Maxanne pushed off the tape machine. She walked to the window that looked into the control room, where Mike sat with his headphones around his neck, watching them through the glass with the expression of a man who understood he was witnessing something he'd spend years trying to describe.

"That's what I told myself in 1980," Maxanne said. Her back was to them. Her reflection floated in the glass — translucent, superimposed over the mixing board and the meters and the blinking lights of the equipment that recorded and preserved and broadcast other people's music into the dark.

The room held still. The tape machine had stopped. The reels were motionless.

Jackie stood. He looked at Nick. Nick looked back. Two years of silence. One night of music. A recording on a tape that might never be played again.

"If things were different," Jackie said.

"Yeah," Nick said.

Jackie extended his hand. Nick took it. They shook — one firm grip, held for two seconds, released. Not a hug. A hug would have been too much. A hug would have acknowledged what they were losing, and neither of them could afford to acknowledge it and still walk out of the room.

Nick went through the studio door. Jackie went through the control room. Separate exits. The hallway split and they walked in opposite directions, toward opposite ends of the building, toward the separate lives that were waiting for them the way debts wait — patient, certain, compounding.

---

Maxanne stood alone in Studio C. The acoustic panels absorbed her reflection. The microphones stood on their boom stands, angled toward empty chairs, pointing at the space where the music had been.

Mike appeared in the control room window. He pressed the talkback button.

"What do we do with this?" he asked. He held up the tape reel — seven inches of magnetic tape, four minutes and forty-three seconds, everything.

Maxanne looked at the reel in his hands. She thought about Aerosmith in 1973 — five guys in a van, playing a club in Kenmore Square, and her at the board, knowing before the first chorus ended that they were going to change everything. She'd been right. She'd played their demo on WBCN and the phones had lit up and the rest was history, the kind of history that happened because one person in one room at one moment said yes.

This was different. This wasn't a band she could break. This was a recording of a thing that no longer existed — a partnership dissolved, a harmony abandoned, a choice made in the dead space between 3 AM and dawn. You couldn't break a band that had already broken itself.

"We preserve it," she said. "Because somebody needs to remember this was possible."

Mike handed her the reel through the pass-through window. She held it in both hands. Light. Lighter than it should have been for what it carried.

She put the tape in her purse. She turned off the studio lights. She walked out of WBCN into the 3 AM dark, where the parking lot held one car — Grace Johnson's Buick, engine idling, headlights off — and two empty spaces where two boys had already gone.

CHAPTER 33
THE SEPARATIONS

I.
NICK
September 1979 — Fort Benning, Georgia

The barracks smelled like floor wax and sweat and Pine-Sol, and underneath all of it, red clay. Georgia clay got into everything — boots, seams, nostrils, the spaces between teeth. By the second week of basic training, Nick had stopped noticing the taste. By the fourth week, he'd stopped noticing most things.

The body adapted. That was the trick the Army knew and didn't advertise: exhaust the mind and the body takes over, and the body is obedient in ways the mind never was. Run. March. Crawl. Repeat. His legs learned distances his brain refused to calculate. His hands learned the M16 the way they'd once learned the Stratocaster — by repetition so thorough it became reflex, muscle replacing thought, fingers finding positions without being told.

The guitar lived in his footlocker. A case inside a case — he'd wrapped the Stratocaster in a bedsheet before sliding it into the battered hardshell, because the lock was broken and the latches were loose and the instrument was the one thing he owned that couldn't be replaced. The footlocker sat at the end of his bunk, regulation distance from the wall, combination lock threaded through the hasp. Thirty-eight men in the barracks. He trusted none of them with this.

After lights out, when the breathing settled into the rhythm of collective sleep — thirty-eight men inhaling and exhaling in almost-unison, the human equivalent of a room full of machines powering down — Nick opened the footlocker. The combination was his mother's birthday. He'd told himself that was for security. He knew it was for something else.

He didn't plug in. The barracks had no amplifier, and even if it had, the sound would have brought the drill sergeant and the drill sergeant would have brought consequences Nick couldn't afford. He played unplugged. The Stratocaster whispered. An electric guitar without amplification was a ghost of itself — all structure, no voice. The notes existed in the space between his fingers and the fretboard, audible only to him and to anyone lying awake within three feet.

He played "Sweet Emotion." The bass riff, slow, the way he'd played it in Studio C. Without the amp, the melody was skeletal — the architecture of the song stripped to its beams, a blueprint you could hold in your hands. He played it every night for a week. Then he played "Take Five" in five-four time. Then he played nothing he could name, just sounds, his fingers moving on the strings the way a man paces in a small room, not going anywhere, just proving he still can.

Specialist Haywood, two bunks down, listened for a month before he spoke.

"You any good?" Haywood asked one night. Whispering. The barracks dark.

"I was."

"What happened?"

Nick played a D minor chord, so quiet it sounded like paper tearing. "Joined the Army."

The drill sergeant found him on a Tuesday. Week seven. Nick had his back against the footlocker and the guitar in his lap and his eyes closed, and he didn't hear Sergeant Decker's boots on the waxed floor because Decker walked soft when he wanted to — a skill he'd learned in two tours that he never discussed.

Nick opened his eyes. Decker stood over him. The sergeant's face was unreadable in the dark — a topography of shadows and discipline. He looked at the guitar. He looked at Nick. He said nothing.

He walked away. His boots made no sound. Nick sat in the dark and waited for the consequence that never came.

Grace wrote every week. Her letters arrived on Tuesdays in envelopes she'd bought in bulk at Woolworth's — white, standard, her handwriting tight and efficient, the same handwriting that filled out inventory forms and shift schedules and bank deposit slips. She wrote about the restaurant. The new assistant manager was stealing from the register and Grace was building a case. The Buick's timing belt finally snapped; she'd gotten a ride from Helen Gorski to the mechanic and the repair had cost four hundred dollars, which was a hundred and fifty more than the estimate but what are you going to do. The leaves were turning. Mrs. Abernathy's dog had dug up the Hendersons' tomato garden again.

She never asked about the guitar. She never asked if he was happy. She asked if he was eating and if he'd gotten the package she sent (oatmeal cookies, wrapped in wax paper, half-crushed by the postal service) and if he needed new socks.

Nick wrote back on lined paper torn from a government-issue notepad. He wrote about training — the obstacle course, the rifle range, the ten-mile march in full pack that left blisters on his heels the size of quarters. He wrote about the food, which was bad, and the weather, which was worse. He wrote about Haywood, who was from Detroit and could bench-press two-forty and sang Motown in the shower with a voice that belonged on a stage.

He didn't mention the guitar. He didn't mention the tape. He didn't mention the nights when he lay in his bunk after playing and stared at the ceiling and thought about a studio at 3 AM and a chord that sounded like the rest of his life.

The $250 had gone to Grace's car payment. She'd protested — "That's your money, your prize" — and he'd left the check on the kitchen table next to the salt shaker and said, "Cash it." She had. She'd cashed it the way she cashed everything — without ceremony, without sentiment, because money was a tool and tools were for using and sentimentality was a luxury she'd been stripped of the day they lowered Dan into the ground.

Nick lay in his bunk. Fort Benning breathed around him — thirty-eight men, red clay, wax and Pine-Sol, the distant sound of someone's radio playing country through a single earphone. He was trading his body for education. The American working-class bargain: three years of service for tuition, room, board, and the chance to become something his father never had the chance to become.

Dan would have understood. Dan had made his own bargain — his body for a paycheck, construction sites for a mortgage, physical labor for a house on Maple Street where his son could play guitar in a garage that smelled like sawdust.

The bargain killed him.

Nick closed the footlocker. He set the combination. He lay down and stared at the ceiling until the staring became sleep.

II.
JACKIE
August 1979 — Manhattan

The room was nine feet by eleven. Jackie knew because he'd measured it, which was the kind of thing he did when he was anxious — quantify the space, reduce the unknown to numbers, make the unfamiliar precise. Nine by eleven. A twin bed, a desk, a dresser, a window that looked onto West 66th Street where taxis moved in yellow procession and the sound never stopped. His bedroom in Sudbury had been fourteen by sixteen with a view of the oak tree his mother had planted the year he was born.

The piano was better. A Steinway Model B, seven feet, in the practice room on the fourth floor. Jackie touched the keys the first time and understood what serious meant. This instrument didn't tolerate hesitation. It responded to intention the way a blade responds to pressure — immediately, precisely, without forgiveness.

He practiced six hours a day. Chopin etudes, Bach inventions, Debussy preludes. His technique sharpened. The classical training did what classical training was designed to do: it standardized. It took the raw, eccentric thing his hands wanted to do and sanded it into something consistent, reproducible, professional. His professors praised his precision. His left hand was called exemplary. His sight-reading was called exceptional.

His roommate, Paul Nakamura from Portland, Oregon, played cello and listened to jazz and kept a turntable in the corner that he fed a steady diet of Miles Davis and Thelonious Monk.

"What kind of music do you listen to?" Paul asked, their second night.

"Classical," Jackie said.

He didn't mention Queen. He didn't mention the Crown Jewels, or the Battle of the Bands, or the Casio with the cracked middle C, or the garage on Maple Street, or the studio at 3 AM. He said "classical" the way you close a door — firmly, with your back against it.

October. His first recital. Chopin's Ballade No. 1 in G minor. He'd been playing it since he was fifteen, and by now his fingers held every phrase with the certainty of long acquaintance. The auditorium in the Juilliard building was small — a hundred and twenty seats — but it resonated like a space designed by people who understood that sound was architecture.

He played it perfectly. Every note in its place. Every dynamic marking observed. The rubato was tasteful, the pedaling was clean, the climax built and released with the structural integrity of a well-made bridge.

Dr. Marsh attended. He flew from Boston to LaGuardia and took a taxi to Lincoln Center and sat in the third row in a suit he'd pressed himself because Jackie's mother was visiting Elizabeth at UMass that weekend. He sat with his hands on his knees and his eyes on his son's hands and his face carried an expression that Jackie recognized from fourteen years of recitals: pride. The specific, complicated pride of a man watching his son do the thing he'd been unable to do himself — not the same thing, not exactly, but close enough to feel like redemption.

After the recital, Dr. Marsh said, "That was beautiful." Two words that cost him more than Jackie would understand for another twenty years.

Jackie said, "Thank you." Two words that cost him just as much.

In November, Jackie took the train to Boston. WBCN's studio looked different in daylight — smaller, shabbier, the hallway scuffed and the carpet stained with coffee the color of old pennies. Maxanne was in Studio B, setting up for the evening show. She looked the same. Leather jacket. Boots. The face of a woman who had seen too much music to be surprised by any of it.

"I wanted to say goodbye," Jackie said.

Maxanne leaned against the console. "You came all the way from New York to say goodbye?"

"I was in town. Thanksgiving."

"How's Juilliard?"

"Fine. Different. We don't talk."

Maxanne's eyes narrowed. "We?"

"Nick and me. Different worlds."

A silence that tasted like tape oxide.

"The music you made together—" Maxanne started.

"Was a long time ago," Jackie said.

Eight months. It had been eight months. But Jackie said it the way people say "a long time ago" when they mean "a distance I've put between myself and something I can't afford to look at directly."

He took the train back to New York. He practiced Debussy. He did not play "Bohemian Rhapsody." He did not play anything in D minor. He slept in a room that was nine by eleven and dreamed in dimensions he couldn't measure.

III.
MAXANNE
April 1980 — and after

She left WBCN on a Thursday.

No ceremony. No farewell broadcast. She packed a box — headphones, a coffee mug with the station logo, three pens, a Rolodex thick with the phone numbers of people who owed her favors — and carried it to her car and drove to an apartment in Cambridge that she'd rented the week before. The lease started May 1. She moved in early because the station had hired her replacement and the overlap period was unbearable — watching someone else sit in her chair, touch her board, speak into her microphone with a voice that hadn't earned the frequency.

The corporate offer came from Atlantic Records. A&R. Artist and Repertoire — the department that found talent and signed it and shaped it and, more often than not, filed the rough edges off it until what remained was marketable and smooth and barely alive. She took the job because it paid three times what WBCN paid, and because she told herself she could do A&R differently, the way she'd done radio differently — with taste, with conviction, with the refusal to compromise that had made her Maxanne.

She lasted four years at Atlantic before the refusal to compromise became the problem. She moved to a management firm. Then to an independent label. Then to another management firm. Each move was lateral or slightly downward, each one accompanied by the same internal narrative: this time I'll do it right.

Her office — whichever office, they all looked the same after a while — had a locked drawer. Bottom right. Combination lock, the same combination she'd used since college. Inside: a seven-inch tape reel in a cardboard sleeve. On the sleeve, in Mike's handwriting: "Nick/Jackie — un-Authorized — March 1979."

She checked it once a month. Not listened to — checked. Made sure it was there. Made sure the tape hadn't warped, the oxide hadn't flaked, the reel wasn't cracked. She held it up to the light and looked through the tape for signs of deterioration. She put it back. She locked the drawer.

In 1985, she signed a band from Providence. Two guitars, bass, drums, keyboards. They had something — a tension between the lead guitarist and the keyboardist, a push and pull that created friction in their sound, an argument that made the music better. She championed them through development, fought for their first single, secured a tour slot opening for a mid-level act.

They sounded almost like what Nick and Jackie could have been. Almost. The guitarist was technically superior to Nick but played with less hunger. The keyboardist had Jackie's precision but not his capacity for surprise. The band made one album that sold modestly and broke up over money and a girl and the usual accelerants.

The tape stayed in the drawer.

In 1995, the internet arrived — not all at once, but in stages, like water rising. Maxanne watched it from behind her desk. She was in management now, guiding careers, negotiating contracts, doing the work that kept musicians fed and insured and professionally alive. The internet meant distribution without gatekeepers. It meant anyone could upload anything and the audience would find it or it wouldn't.

She thought about the tape. She thought about uploading it — transferring it to digital, putting it on one of the new file-sharing platforms, letting it exist in the world without her name or Nick's or Jackie's attached to it. Just the music. Anonymous. Free.

She decided not to. The tape wasn't hers to share. It belonged to two boys who'd made a choice, and the choice had been private, and making the tape public would turn their private grief into content.

In 2000, WBCN was still broadcasting. But the station she'd known — the station that played what it wanted because the only metric that mattered was whether the music was good — had been replaced by a version of itself that checked demographics and ran focus groups and played songs because an algorithm said they'd retain listeners through the commercial break. She watched from the corporate side, the management side, the side she'd sworn she'd never be on.

The tape stayed in the drawer.

She checked it on the first of every month. Twelve times a year. Twenty years. Two hundred and forty inspections. She never played it. She knew what was on it. She could hear it without the tape, the way an amputee feels the limb that's gone — four minutes and forty-three seconds of phantom music, perfect in memory, untouchable, preserved not on magnetic oxide but in the space behind her sternum where she kept the things she couldn't afford to say out loud.

The tape waited. The tape was patient. The tape had time.

CHAPTER 33A: THE STATION'S FINAL COMPROMISE

August 11, 2009. 7:14 PM. I pull into the WBCN parking lot and sit in my car for a long time.

The building looks the same from outside — squat, brick, the kind of architecture that suggests functionality over beauty, a place where things get made, not displayed. The antenna tower rises above the roof like a finger pointing at something nobody wants to look at. The WBCN logo on the side of the building is backlit, still glowing, but someone has already removed the promotional banner from the front entrance. The glass doors reflect the parking lot back at me — my car, the August sky, the slow heat-shimmer rising off asphalt.

I check my purse. It's there. It's always there.

The demo tape sits in a zippered pocket next to a pack of tissues, a lipstick I haven't worn in months, and a parking stub from a restaurant I don't remember visiting. Thirty years this tape has traveled with me — from Brookline to New York to LA to Nashville to wherever the corporate music machine needed a woman who could identify talent and keep her mouth shut about what talent was actually worth. Thirty years pressed against receipts and breath mints and the ordinary debris of a life spent running from the one night that defined it.

I take it out. Hold it up against the windshield. The label has faded to a color between cream and yellow, the ink of my block letters blurred by decades of body heat and pocket friction but still legible. "Demo - Nick/Jackie - un-Authorized - March 1979." The cassette shell has a hairline crack along one edge — I noticed it in 1993, wrapped a piece of Scotch tape around it, and have checked the repair every few months since, the way a nurse checks a patient's vitals even when nothing's changing.

I close my hand around it.

Thirty years I've carried this tape. Through three apartments, two career changes, one marriage that lasted six years and ended with a handshake that was more dignified than it should have been. Through the eighties, when MTV turned music into television and I helped. Through the nineties, when grunge briefly made us think authenticity had returned, and I knew better. Through the two-thousands, when algorithms replaced instinct and the last DJs who remembered what discovery felt like retired or died or both.

I've carried this tape the way a soldier carries a bullet that missed him — not as a souvenir, but as proof that something happened. That it was real.

I get out of the car.

---

The lobby is a labyrinth of cardboard.

I stop inside the entrance and let my eyes adjust. The reception desk — where for fifty-six years a succession of young women and young men buzzed visitors through, took delivery of promo records, and fielded calls from fans and artists and people who thought they were both — is stripped to its laminate shell. The computer is gone. The phone is gone. The visitor sign-in sheet with its curled pages and chewed-pen-on-a-string is gone. A box marked "RECEPTION — MISC" sits on the desk, its flaps open, revealing a tangle of phone cords and a coffee mug that says "WBCN — The Rock of Boston" with a chip on the rim.

I pick up the mug. The ceramic is cold. I put it back.

The hallway beyond reception is lined with moving boxes, stacked two and three high, labeled in black marker with the clinical shorthand of institutional death. "STUDIO A — EQUIPMENT." "ARCHIVES — 1971-1980." "PROMO — UNSORTED." "AWARDS / PLAQUES." The Gold Records are gone from the walls, leaving rectangular ghosts where the frames blocked decades of nicotine and ambient grime — clean patches on dirty plaster, the shapes of things that used to matter.

I walk the hallway slowly. My shoes click on the linoleum. The building is quiet in a way I've never experienced — not the productive quiet of between-shifts, not the focused quiet of a DJ deep in a set, but the terminal quiet of a body that's stopped breathing. The hum is wrong. For fifty-six years, WBCN hummed with the specific vibration of live broadcast — electricity running through mixing boards, signals traveling through cable to the antenna, the building itself a conductor between music and the world. That hum was the building's heartbeat. I could feel it in the floor when I was twenty-three and running late for my shift. I could feel it in the walls when I pressed my hand to the corridor during a Springsteen broadcast and the plaster seemed to pulse.

The hum tonight is different. Mechanical. The hum of automation systems being tested. In a room to my right — a room that used to be the promotions office — I hear it. A thin, tinny sound bleeding through the closed door. I stop. Listen.

Christian contemporary. Test tones. Someone in that room is running the automation system that will replace us at midnight. Programming the playlist that will begin broadcasting at 12:01 AM on the frequency that Steven Tyler's voice once traveled. The sound is muffled by the door but unmistakable — a synthesized worship track, all smooth edges and manufactured reverence, the sonic equivalent of a greeting card.

The labyrinth. That's what this is. That's what it's always been.

I spent thirty years thinking the labyrinth was a metaphor. Something I could outsmart. The maze that corporate radio built around authentic music — you just had to find the right path, the clever workaround, the rule you could bend or the clause you could exploit. I spent my twenties believing I could navigate it by talent alone. My thirties believing I could navigate it by strategy. My forties believing I could navigate it by compromise. My fifties accepting that navigation was an illusion, that the labyrinth doesn't have exits, only deeper corridors that look like freedom until you turn the corner and find another wall.

But standing in this hallway, listening to Christian contemporary test tones bleed through the wall of a room that used to book Springsteen concerts, I understand something I've been circling for three decades: the labyrinth isn't built around you. The labyrinth is you. Every compromise you make becomes a wall. Every noble lie becomes a corridor. Every time you tell yourself you're gaming the system, you're building the system another room.

I helped build this. Not the boxes in the hallway. Not the buyout. But the architecture that made the buyout inevitable. The day I sat in that meeting with Gerald Kowalski and said "What if we use it to actually find talent" — that was the day I started building walls and calling them doors.

The Christian contemporary test tone shifts key. Up a third. Hopeful. Ascending. Praise music designed to make you feel lifted without having to lift yourself. I press my palm against the wall and feel the vibration — faint, foreign, the frequency of a building learning to speak a language it was never built for.

I keep walking.

---

The station manager's office is open. Lights on, nobody home.

I shouldn't go in. But I've spent thirty years not doing things I should have done, so the irony of doing something I shouldn't seems about right.

The desk is cleared except for three items: a telephone, a desk lamp, and a document. The document is in a clear plastic sleeve, the kind you use for things that need protecting from coffee rings and casual handling. I can read the header through the plastic without picking it up.

ASSET PURCHASE AGREEMENT

Between: WBCN Radio, Inc. (Seller)
And: Educational Media Foundation (Buyer)

I don't need to read further. I know the terms. Everyone knows the terms — they were reported in the Globe, in the Herald, in trade publications that covered the death of independent radio the way medical journals cover the progression of a terminal disease. WBCN sold to Educational Media Foundation, operators of K-LOVE and Air1 Christian radio networks. The frequency — 104.1 FM, the Rock of Boston, the signal that carried Aerosmith and The Cars and Boston and J. Geils and Springsteen and a thousand other sounds that changed people's lives — would be reformatted to Christian contemporary. Effective August 12, 2009, 12:01 AM.

Below the header, a line I haven't seen before, handwritten in blue ink by someone — the station manager, probably, or maybe a lawyer with a human moment:

"56 years. Thank you."

I stand there looking at those two words for longer than I should. Thank you. As if the building could hear. As if the frequency would remember.

The phone on the desk rings. I watch it ring three times, four, five. Nobody answers. Nobody's here to answer. The phone stops and the office is quiet again except for the bleed of Christian contemporary from down the hall, the test tones getting louder now, more confident, the new tenant moving in before the old one's body is cold.

---

Studio B.

I push through the door and here it is — my coffin, my cathedral, my confessional. The room where I played "Dream On" for the first time. The room where Bruce Springsteen said "Hi Mom." The room where I told a city what rock and roll sounded like and the city believed me.

The equipment is still here, at least. The console — an ancient Wheatstone board that's been repaired so many times it's more solder than original circuitry — glows with its familiar amber. The turntables sit on their platforms, dusty now, the needles removed and probably packed in one of the hallway boxes marked "STUDIO B — EQUIPMENT." The cart machines are empty except for one slot where someone has placed the final song.

I walk to the cart machine. The cartridge sits there with a strip of masking tape across the top, labeled in Sharpie: "COMFORTABLY NUMB — PINK FLOYD — FINAL TRACK — DO NOT REMOVE."

Pink Floyd. "Comfortably Numb." That's what they've chosen for the last song that will ever play on WBCN. The station's farewell. Appropriate, I suppose — that guitar solo is the sound of something beautiful being untethered from the earth, rising above pain into something that might be freedom or might be oblivion, depending on how much you've lost.

I touch the cartridge. Leave it where it is.

Then I reach into my purse and take out the demo tape.

In my left hand: "Comfortably Numb." Thirty years of corporate classic rock, safe, beloved, the kind of song that makes people feel something without asking them to risk anything. A masterpiece, yes. But a familiar one. A comfortable one. Comfortably numb.

In my right hand: "Demo - Nick/Jackie - un-Authorized - March 1979." Four minutes and forty-three seconds that no one has ever heard. Two boys who found harmony in a room where they were supposed to be enemies. The only recording that proves the frequency existed. Uncomfortable. Unauthorized. Alive.

I set the demo tape on the console next to the Pink Floyd cartridge.

The ON AIR sign is dark. It won't light up until eight o'clock, when my shift begins. Four hours of the final broadcast. Four hours to tell the truth.

The engineering booth door opens and the kid walks in — the one they've assigned to babysit my final show. Mid-twenties. Bruins hoodie. Phone already in hand. He looks at me, at the console, at the two tapes sitting side by side.

"Ms. Sartori? I'm Kevin. I'll be your engineer tonight."

"Kevin. You got any bourbon?"

He blinks. "I've got Red Bull."

"That'll have to do."

He retreats to his booth. Through the glass, I watch him settle into his chair and immediately check his phone. Twenty-five years old, probably. Born in 1984. He's never lived in a world where the radio DJ chose the music. He's never felt the specific electricity of a broadcast booth when someone drops a needle on a track that nobody's heard before and a city leans in to listen. He's never experienced what it means to be the frequency.

I look at the console. At the two tapes.

"Comfortably Numb" will satisfy everyone. Management. The nostalgic listeners who tuned in for one last cry. The newspaper reporters covering the story, waiting for the poetic final note. It's the expected ending. The approved ending. The ending that makes for a clean obituary.

The demo tape will satisfy no one.

Management will be furious — unauthorized material, no clearance, no rights, no warning. The listeners will be confused — who are these kids? What is this recording? Why is this woman playing some unknown demo tape instead of the song we were promised? The reporters will scramble to explain why WBCN's final moments veered off-script. It will be messy. Unprofessional. Defiant.

It will be the most honest thing I've done in thirty years.

I sit down in the broadcast chair. Adjust the headphones — the MDR-7506s, electrical tape and stubbornness, the only thing in this room older than my regrets. The console lights pulse expectantly, like they've been waiting for me, like the building knows what I'm about to do even if I haven't fully admitted it to myself.

Down the hall, the Christian contemporary test tones swell. A choir synthesized by an algorithm singing about salvation to an empty room. The future of 104.1 FM, rehearsing its takeover five hours early. The labyrinth's newest corridor, clean and well-lit and leading absolutely nowhere.

I pick up the demo tape. Press it against my chest. Feel the warmth of it through my shirt — thirty years of body heat, of being carried close.

Nick Johnson. Jackie Marsh. Two kids who proved that the frequency was real.

Nick joined the Army. Last I heard, he came home, started a construction company in Sudbury. Builds things with his hands, like his father. Married, I think. Kids, I think. A life built on the practical scaffolding of a dream he packed away in a garage that probably doesn't have a guitar in it anymore.

Jackie went to Juilliard. Got his degree. Teaches at Berklee now — music theory, the architecture of sound, the mathematics of harmony. Divorced. No kids. The kind of quiet life that looks successful from the outside and hollow from the inside, like a house with all the lights on and nobody home.

Two casualties of the labyrinth. Two talented boys who chose family loyalty over their own frequency — and both choices were right, and both choices were devastating, and the fact that two right choices can produce a tragedy is the cruelest thing I know about being alive.

And me. The architect. The woman who built the stage they died on and called it opportunity. Carrying the evidence in her purse for thirty years like a penance she's too proud to name.

Three casualties of the same war. Different strategies. Same outcome. The labyrinth doesn't care how you run.

But I still have the tape.

The labyrinth took the boys. It took the station. It took my rebel soul and turned it into a corporate résumé. But it didn't get the tape. Four minutes and forty-three seconds of proof that the frequency existed — that harmony was possible, that two voices from opposite sides of a brook in a suburb of Boston found each other in a room at two in the morning and made something that shouldn't exist but does, that survives, that sits in a zippered purse pocket like a heartbeat waiting to be heard.

I set the tape on the console. Right next to "Comfortably Numb."

In forty-six minutes, the ON AIR sign will light up. In four hours and forty-six minutes, WBCN will die. In four hours and forty-seven minutes, this frequency will carry a different signal — scrubbed, sanctified, safe.

But somewhere in those four hours, I'm going to do the one thing I haven't done in thirty years.

I'm going to press play.

Not "Comfortably Numb." Not the approved finale. Not the comfortable ending.

The demo. The unauthorized, unsanctioned, four-minute-and-forty-three-second proof that Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh once found the frequency, and the labyrinth — for all its walls and corridors and locked doors — couldn't erase it entirely. Because someone carried it. Because someone, even a sellout, even an architect of tragedy, even a woman who became everything she fought — someone kept the evidence close for thirty years, waiting for the right moment to stop being numb and start being honest.

I crack my knuckles.

Forty-five minutes until the last rebel act of WBCN. The last unauthorized play. The last time a DJ in this building will choose the music because she believes in it, not because a chart told her to.

I lean back in the chair. The leather creaks. The console glows. The building hums its wrong hum — Christian contemporary bleeding through the walls like a future I can't stop.

But not yet.

Not yet.

The ON AIR sign stays dark. I wait. The tape waits. And somewhere in Sudbury, twenty miles west of this dying building, Hop Brook still divides the town the way it always has, water running over stones, indifferent to the lives it separates. But the music — the music is right here, in my hands, four minutes and forty-three seconds of something the brook couldn't drown and the labyrinth couldn't kill.

I close my eyes. And I remember what it felt like to hear the real thing for the first time. A bar in Allston. 1973. Twenty people in the room. A singer who moved like he was shaking demons off his skin. A guitar player who looked like he'd kill you and not lose sleep.

The frequency. The signal underneath the static.

I found it once. I lost it. I sold it. I compromised it. I buried it under thirty years of corporate survival and noble lies and rules written in fine print on kitchen tables at midnight.

But I never let go of the tape.

In forty-five minutes, the ON AIR sign lights up.

In four hours, I play it.

Full volume.

CHAPTER 34
THE LAST VOICE OF BOSTON ROCK — PART 2

August 11, 2009. 11:30 PM.

The booth smells like ozone and old vinyl and thirty-six years of someone else's cigarettes embedded in the acoustic foam. I'm sitting in Studio B with half an hour left — thirty minutes until the signal dies and the frequency goes to God, or whoever bought it, which in this case is a Christian broadcasting company out of North Carolina that will rebrand this booth and this board and this microphone into something called JOY FM. The irony writes itself. I refuse to write it.

I've told the whole story tonight. Four hours of broadcast — the longest uninterrupted monologue in WBCN history, which is saying something for a station that once let a DJ read the entirety of "Howl" on air because he lost a bet. I've talked about the bands. The battle. The tie. The studio session. Everything I've carried for thirty years, laid out on the airwaves like laundry on a line, flapping in public for the first time.

The phones have been ringing all night. I've ignored them. Let the board light up. Let the program director pace in the hallway. This is my last broadcast and I will fill it with whatever I choose.

Two objects sit on the console in front of me.

On the left: a Pink Floyd LP. "The Wall." Track B5, "Comfortably Numb." Corporate approved it three weeks ago as WBCN's final song. A safe choice. A crowd-pleaser. The kind of song that lets everyone feel nostalgic without feeling anything.

On the right: a seven-inch tape reel in a cardboard sleeve. Mike's handwriting, faded to the color of weak tea: "Nick/Jackie — un-Authorized — March 1979." The sleeve is soft at the edges from handling. I've checked this tape two hundred and forty times. I've never played it.

Thirty years in my purse. Thirty years in a locked drawer. Thirty years of carrying a piece of evidence that proves two boys once stood in a studio at 3 AM and made something that should have changed their lives and didn't because they loved the people who loved them more than they loved themselves.

11:42 PM.

I run my hand across the console. Every knob, every slider, every button — I've touched them so many times my fingerprints must be layered into the surface like geological strata. This board has been my instrument. I never played guitar, never sang, never wrote a song. I played this. I played the space between the music and the listener, the gap between the artist and the audience, and I made that gap smaller. That was my talent. That was my frequency.

The ON AIR light glows red. It's been red since 8 PM. In twenty-eight minutes it will go dark for the first time in fifty-six years. WBCN signed on in 1968. I was twenty-four. I'm sixty-five. The math is simple. The subtraction is not.

11:47 PM.

I pick up the Pink Floyd LP. Hold it. The vinyl is heavy in my hands — twelve inches of pressed polyvinyl chloride, the grooves spiraling inward like a labyrinth. Comfortably numb. A song about disconnection. A song about the wall between feeling and not feeling. A perfect final track for a station that spent its life tearing walls down.

I put it down.

I pick up the tape.

It weighs almost nothing. Seven inches of Mylar coated in iron oxide, wound on a plastic reel that cost forty cents wholesale in 1979. Four minutes and forty-three seconds of two boys who never became what they should have become, playing in a room that no longer exists in a building that will be rebranded by morning.

11:50 PM.

I lean into the microphone. The pop filter catches my breath.

"I want to tell you about a recording," I say. "I've been carrying it for thirty years. It was made in this building — not in this studio, but down the hall, in Studio C, at two in the morning on a night in March 1979. Two seventeen-year-old kids from Sudbury. They'd just tied in a Battle of the Bands. They didn't like each other very much — or they liked each other too much, which amounts to the same thing when you're seventeen."

The phones light up. I don't look at them.

"I brought them here because I heard them play through an open kitchen window, and what I heard was the sound of two people finding the harmony where all the noise dissolved. Not the noise of competition, or rivalry, or the town that had spent six months turning them into enemies. The noise of everything — parents, expectations, class, ambition, grief. All of it. Gone. Just two instruments and the truth between them."

I hold the tape up, though no one can see it. Habit. Thirty-six years of radio and I still make gestures for an invisible audience.

"They recorded one take. No overdubs. 'Sweet Emotion' by Aerosmith meeting 'Bohemian Rhapsody' by Queen. If that sounds impossible, it was. It is. It happened anyway. Four minutes and forty-three seconds. The best piece of music I've ever heard, and I introduced Aerosmith to this city."

11:55 PM.

"I offered them a choice. Walk away from everything — college, the military, their families' plans — and pursue this. Make this their life. They said no. Both of them. Not because they didn't want it, but because they couldn't ask the people who'd sacrificed for them to sacrifice more. They chose their mothers. Their fathers. The people who'd already chosen them."

My voice holds. I'm surprised by this. I expected it to break.

"I told them that's what I'd told myself in 1980 when I left this station. I was wrong then and they were wrong that night and we were all wrong in exactly the same way — we thought loyalty and art were opposites. We thought you had to choose. Maybe you do. I don't know anymore."

I slide the tape onto the playback machine. The reel fits the spindle like a key in a lock it was cut for. I thread the tape through the heads, across the capstan, onto the take-up reel. My hands know this motion. My hands have always known this motion.

"This tape is the only proof that two boys once found the frequency where everything aligned. I've kept it because somebody needs to remember that this was possible. That it happened. That art doesn't care about your plans."

I put my finger on the play button.

"Before we go dark forever, I want to play you something that was never supposed to be heard."

I press play. Full volume. My signature move — the one the engineering staff hated and the listeners loved, the sudden wall of sound that hit you in the chest before your ears could adjust. The VU meters slam into the red and stay there.

The reel turns.

D minor. Jackie's piano. The chord fills the booth, fills the building, fills the frequency — 104.1 FM, fifty thousand watts, reaching from the Cape to the New Hampshire border. The chord hangs. Then Nick's guitar enters, the "Sweet Emotion" riff stripped clean, played through a DI box at 2 AM by a boy who'd been angry all year and found, in that studio, the one place where anger didn't help.

The songs merge. I've heard this tape two hundred and forty times in my memory. I've never heard it out loud. The speakers give it dimension my memory couldn't — the room tone of Studio C, the breath before phrases, the slight hiss of the tape, the moment at the forty-second mark where Nick's fingers slip on the twelfth fret and Jackie catches it with a crushed note on the piano and the mistake becomes the best thing in the recording.

I close my eyes. The booth disappears. The console disappears. The ON AIR light, the phones, the dead-man switches and the meters and the thirty years of equipment between me and the music — all of it goes. There's just the sound. Two instruments. One frequency. The harmony that exists in the space where two people stop performing and start listening.

The operatic section. Jackie's left hand doubles Nick's riff. Nick's guitar sings the counter-melody. They chase each other through the changes, trading the lead the way they used to trade baseball cards in middle school — without keeping score, without calculating value, just because the exchange itself was the point.

The hard rock section. Loud. Both of them digging in, playing like the room is on fire and the music is the only way out. Thirty seconds of controlled fury. Then silence. Two beats.

The final chord. D minor. Sustain pedal down. The note rings and rings and fades. Four minutes and forty-three seconds.

11:59:30.

The tape hisses into silence. The reel spins. I stop the machine.

I lean into the microphone for the last time.

"This is Maxanne Sartori, signing off for the last time."

I pause. The silence is enormous. Fifty thousand watts of silence.

"The harmony still exists."

Beat.

"The harmony still exists."

Beat.

"The harmony still exists."

11:59:59.

The ON AIR light goes dark. The red glow dies. The booth goes gray — not black, because the emergency lighting kicks in, casting everything in the color of old newsprint. But the red light that has burned in this building since 1968 is off, and the absence of it is louder than anything I've ever broadcast.

Silence. Real silence. The transmitter silence. The end-of-signal silence that sounds like the moment after a heart stops — not quiet, but empty. A space where something used to be.

12:01 AM.

Static. The speakers crackle with the particular hiss of a frequency being reassigned — the sound of one signal dying and another being born, like a radio station being reincarnated against its will.

Then a voice. Male. Smooth. Mixed too high, processed through compression and reverb, the audio equivalent of a smile that doesn't reach the eyes.

"You're listening to JOY FM, bringing you the good news in music and ministry."

Contemporary Christian fills the booth. Synthesizers. A choir. Lyrics about grace that sound like they were written by a committee.

I laugh. One sharp bark that comes out half sob. The sound bounces off the acoustic foam and dies.

I unplug my headphones. The silence of the booth without them is different — thinner, flatter, stripped of the bass frequencies that headphones add. The room sounds like what it is: a small box with foam on the walls and a dead console and a tape machine that just played its last tape.

I pick up the Pink Floyd LP. I pick up the empty tape case. I leave the reel on the machine. Someone else can deal with it. Someone from JOY FM, inheriting a booth full of ghosts.

The hallway is empty. The offices are dark. I walk past Studio C — the door is open, the room stripped, the acoustic panels removed and stacked against the wall like shed skin. The Yamaha is gone. The microphone stands are gone. The room is nine by twelve and holds nothing.

The lobby. The front door. The parking lot.

August in Boston. The air is thick and warm and smells like asphalt and the harbor. The WBCN sign above the entrance is dark. They'll change it tomorrow. New letters. New name. New frequency.

My car is the only one in the lot. A Volvo wagon, twelve years old, the same car I've driven to this building a thousand times. I sit in the driver's seat and close the door and the silence wraps around me like water.

My phone buzzes.

I look at the screen. A text from Sophie, my niece. She's nineteen. She goes to Emerson. She thinks radio is what old people did before podcasts.

"Aunt Max, did you play that song? Someone recorded your broadcast and put it on YouTube."

I stare at the screen. My thumbs are suddenly thick and stupid.

"What?"

"It's blowing up. Like actually blowing up. Check YouTube."

I open my laptop. The screen lights the car's interior blue. YouTube loads on the station's WiFi, which still works because nobody's thought to turn it off yet.

The video is titled: "Two Guys Who Should Have Been Famous — WBCN Final Broadcast." Posted by someone called BostonRockRIP. Twelve minutes ago. The thumbnail is a photograph of the WBCN antenna tower, pulled from somewhere, overlaid with text in Impact font.

50,000 views.

The comments scroll. I read them the way I used to read the request line — fast, scanning for signal in the noise.

"Who are these guys?"
"This is incredible."

12:30 AM. 200,000 views. The number climbs like a living thing, like something with a pulse, each refresh adding thousands as the video spreads through the network of people who are awake at midnight and looking for something real.

#WBCNFinalBroadcast. Trending. I don't know how Twitter works but Sophie's told me enough to understand what trending means. It means the signal has escaped the frequency. It means the music has left the booth and the building and the parking lot and entered the place where things can't be taken back.

1:00 AM. One million views.

My phone rings. A 212 number. New York. I don't answer. It rings again. A 617 number. Boston. I don't answer. A 310 number. Los Angeles. The calls stack up like planes circling Logan.

A voicemail from a producer at NPR. A voicemail from a blogger at Pitchfork. A text from Carter Alan: "Maxanne, what did you do?" A text from an unknown number: "Do you know where they are now?"

I watch the numbers climb. I'm crying. Not sad tears. Not happy tears. Something else. Something that has no name because it lives in the space between grief and gratitude, between what was lost and what was just found, between the drawer where the tape lived for thirty years and the million glowing screens where it lives now.

Nick stayed authentic. The Army, then construction, then teaching guitar out of the same garage on Maple Street. He never signed a deal. He never recorded again. He played for his students and for Grace, who sat on the porch and listened through the screen door the way Dan used to listen from the workbench. The system crushed him the way it crushes everyone who refuses to bend — slowly, without malice, the way gravity crushes.

Jackie compromised. Juilliard, then session work, then film scoring. A career. A good one. Grammy-nominated once, for a documentary score that the academy called "restrained and elegant," which was their way of saying "technically perfect, emotionally absent." He never played rock again. He never played anything in D minor. The system crushed him the way it crushes everyone who bends — slowly, with promotions and praise, the way a vine crushes the thing it climbs.

I fled. Left the booth. Went corporate. Told myself I could change the industry from inside. The industry changed me. The system followed me through every door I opened and sat in every chair I occupied and wrote every contract I negotiated. The system doesn't chase you. It waits. It's already there.

We all failed. Every one of us. We failed the music and we failed each other and we failed the promise of that night in Studio C when two boys played a song that proved the walls between genres and classes and expectations were thinner than anyone admitted.

But the music didn't fail. The tape sat in a drawer, then in a purse, patient as stone. One broadcast. One upload. One million strangers listening at one in the morning, discovering what I discovered through an open kitchen window in 1979: that the harmony exists. That it always existed. That art doesn't die when you lock it in a drawer. It waits.

The labyrinth wins every time. Always. The system is older and bigger and more patient than any of us. But art doesn't compete with the labyrinth. Art doesn't play the game. Art sits in a locked drawer for thirty years and sounds exactly the same when you finally press play. The signal survives the static, if you remember how to listen.

I close the laptop. The screen goes dark. The car is black. The parking lot is empty — one Volvo, one woman, one night that has passed into whatever comes after.

The antenna tower stands against the sky, its red light off for the first time in fifty-six years. The building is silent. The booth is empty. The frequency belongs to someone else now, filled with music that means nothing to me and everything to someone, because that's how frequency works — it doesn't care who's listening, only that someone is.

But somewhere in the dark, through a million glowing screens in a million rooms where people sit alone at 1 AM with their headphones on and their hearts open, two boys are still singing. Still harmonizing. Still playing a mashup of Aerosmith and Queen in a studio that no longer exists, on a tape that no longer matters, through a signal that will never stop because the harmony was real and real things don't need permission to survive.

Walk this way, I think. Yeah. Walk this way.
