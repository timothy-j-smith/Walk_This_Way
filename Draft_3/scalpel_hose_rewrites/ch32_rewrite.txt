CHAPTER 32: STUDIO C

The hallway smelled like tape oxide and old cigarettes. Maxanne walked ahead of them, boot heels cracking on linoleum, not looking back. She'd been in the lobby when Grace's wagon turned off Boylston — standing under the WBCN sign with her arms crossed and her leather jacket zipped to her throat, looking less like a radio legend than a woman who had just bet something she couldn't afford to lose.

Grace stayed in the car. She'd said Go the way she said everything — once, without elaboration — and Nick had climbed out and found Jackie already on the sidewalk, hands in his peacoat pockets, breath clouding, waiting.

It was 2:14 AM.

Studio C was smaller than Nick expected. He'd imagined something palatial — walls of equipment, a mixing board the size of a car, the kind of room Aerosmith or the Cars laid down the tracks Maxanne spun into gold. Instead: a room the size of his mother's living room. Acoustic panels on the walls like quilted armor. Two boom mics facing each other four feet apart. A window into the control room where a kid in a Red Sox cap sat behind a board that was, in fact, the size of a small car.

"Mike," Maxanne said, knocking on the glass. "They're here."

The kid looked up. Twenty-five, maybe. Wire-rims. The kind of enthusiasm that came off him like heat off an engine. He gave a thumbs-up through the glass and started flipping switches. The lights on the board shifted — red, green, amber — and the room hummed with the particular frequency of electronics waking up.

"Two mics," Nick said. "Where's the amp?"

"DI box." Maxanne pointed at a small metal rectangle near the right mic, a cable snaking from it to the wall. "Plug in. Mike takes the tone off the board."

"Never played through a DI."

"Tonight you have."

Jackie was turning slow in the middle of the room. An upright sat against the far wall — not a Casio with cracked keys, but a Yamaha U3, black lacquer reflecting the overheads in long bright streaks. He lifted the fallboard. The keys were perfect. Weighted. He pressed middle C and the note bloomed through the room with a clarity that made the garage version sound like it had been played through a blanket.

"Sit," Maxanne said, pointing Jackie to the bench and Nick to a stool near the DI. "Tune up. Mike needs five minutes for levels."

She stepped into the control room and closed the door. Through the glass Nick watched her lean down to Mike's ear and say something. Mike nodded. His hands moved across the board.

Nick plugged in. Without an amp the Strat sounded thin through the monitors — all string and no body, a voice without a chest. He played a chord. The monitors shaped it, added warmth, gave it room. Mike's hands on the board. The kid knew what he was doing.

Across the four feet of studio air, Jackie ran scales. His fingers moved on the Yamaha the way a swimmer moves in open water — long strokes, effortless, technique so internalized it had gone invisible. The piano responded like it had been waiting for him.

Maxanne's voice came through the overheads, studio-clear. "Here's the deal. One take. No overdubs. No punch-ins. What you played in that garage — play it here."

Nick found Jackie through the space between the microphones. "She was listening?"

Jackie's hands stopped on the keys. "How?"

Maxanne: "I was parked at the end of your road. You were audible from the brook. I drove to the Store 24 on Twenty and called the house."

Nick thought about the kitchen window, cracked two inches because the radiator made the room too hot in May. The sound of them playing carrying through the gap, across the yard, down Dutton Road. A frequency looking for a receiver.

"One take," Jackie said.

"One take," Maxanne said.

Mike's voice, younger and less steady: "Levels are good. Whenever."

Nick and Jackie looked at each other. Four feet of studio air. Two mics. Two instruments.

"You start," Nick said.

Jackie put his hands on the keys. He closed his eyes. For three seconds, nothing. Then — D minor. The chord that had opened the door in the garage. On the Yamaha, in the acoustic treatment of Studio C, the chord did not open a door. It knocked down a wall. The overtones spiraled into the room and hung there, suspended, ringing.

Nick's hands found the bass line. The Aerosmith riff he'd played a thousand times — in the garage, at rehearsals, alone at three in the morning when sleep wouldn't come. He played it different now. The DI stripped away the distortion, the amp growl, the feedback that masked imperfection. What was left was the melody underneath. Clean. Exposed. The bones of the song without the muscle.

Jackie's left hand picked up the riff and doubled it an octave higher, and for four bars they played the same line in unison — guitar and piano, the same phrase, the same breath. Then Jackie's right hand drifted upward, and the Queen melody entered. Not sung. Played. The piano carried the vocal line the way a river carries a leaf.

The songs merged — not gently. They merged the way tributaries merge, each losing its name but not its current, the combined flow stronger than either stream alone. The Aerosmith groove became the heartbeat. The Queen melody became the breath. In the space between, something new existed, something that belonged to two boys at 2 AM who had found in the overlap of their separate obsessions a sound that was theirs.

Nick's eyes were closed. He didn't need to see the fretboard. The riff evolved — a phrase of "Walk This Way" crept in, Tyler's strut translated into six strings, and then the fragments wove together, trading, answering, carrying on the conversation they had started in a garage when they were thirteen and didn't know enough to be afraid.

The Yamaha opened up — bass to treble, thunder to glass. The operatic architecture arrived. Nick's guitar sang the counter-melody Brian May had written for three overdubbed guitars, except Nick played it on one, and the single line cut through the piano chords like light through a prism.

The music stumbled once — Nick's fingers slipped at the twelfth fret, caught themselves, turned the slip into a bend Jackie matched with a crushed note on the piano — and the stumble became part of the song, a crack that let the light in, proof that this was live and would never happen again in exactly this way.

The hard rock section hit. Nick dug in. Jackie hammered the keys. For thirty seconds they played loud enough to rattle the acoustic panels, the monitors feeding back in a loop that built and built until it crested and broke, and then —

Silence.

Two beats.

Jackie played the final chord. D minor again, where they had started, but this time with the sustain pedal down, the note ringing into the room, fading like the last light of a day neither of them wanted to end.

Nick lifted his hands from the strings. The monitors hummed. The tape rolled.

Four minutes and forty-three seconds.

---

Through the glass, Mike sat with his hands flat on the board and his mouth open. Maxanne stood behind him, one hand on the back of his chair, the other pressed flat against the glass.

She thumbed the talkback. "Stay there."

She came into the studio, crossed to the tape machine, and rewound. The reels spun backward with a whirring that filled the room like a held breath being taken back.

"Listen," she said, and pressed play.

Their own playing came back through the monitors, familiar and strange the way a recording always is, because it captured not just the music but the room, the air, the moment. Nick heard his guitar and didn't recognize it. Without distortion, without the amp, he sounded like himself. Not like Joe Perry. Not like Jimmy Page. Like Nick Johnson, seventeen, playing in the middle of the night because the music asked him to.

Jackie heard the Yamaha and his breath went short. The tape had caught everything — the classical training, the rock instinct, the war between them, and the place in the middle where the war stopped and the two traditions started listening to each other. He sounded like no one he had been trained to sound like. He sounded like what he had been afraid to become.

Jackie pressed his fingers to his eyes. His shoulders drew up. When he dropped his hands, his face was wet.

"This is what we could have been," he said.

Nick looked at him. The wetness on Jackie's face caught the studio lights.

"We could still be this," Nick said.

Maxanne leaned against the tape machine and crossed her arms. She looked at both of them the way a woman looks who has been in rooms where music happened for thirty years and knows exactly what she has just witnessed and exactly what it costs.

"You could walk away from everything," she said. "Juilliard. The Army. Forget the plan. Choose this."

The hum of the monitors was the only sound. It sounded like a question that had been asked in a hundred thousand rooms where young musicians sat across from the choice between what they were and what they were supposed to become.

Nick looked at the clock on the wall. Digital. Red numbers. 3:17 AM.

He thought about Grace.

She was in the parking lot. Right now. In a wood-paneled wagon with a hundred-and-eighty thousand miles on it, wearing a Friendly's uniform she had been in since six that morning. Twenty-one hours. She had driven him here without asking why, without complaining, without mentioning she had to be back for Sunday brunch in five hours because Sundays paid time-and-a-half.

He couldn't walk out to that parking lot and lean into the wagon's window and tell her he was throwing the Army over for a sound in a studio at three in the morning. He couldn't watch her face do the thing it did when she was terrified and wouldn't show it.

He couldn't. Not because he didn't want to. Because she had already given everything, and asking for more was a kind of theft he wasn't capable of.

"I can't," Nick said.

---

Jackie sat at the piano bench. His hands rested on his thighs, away from the keys, as if touching them might make the decision for him.

He thought about his father.

Fourteen years of lessons. Every Saturday, the drive from Sudbury to Boston — forty minutes each way on Route 9, Dr. Marsh's Volvo smelling like leather and the Bach cantatas he played on the tape deck because Bach was the foundation of everything. Fourteen years of recitals, competitions, auditions. The Juilliard application his father had helped him write — three drafts, each one more careful than the last, each one a collaboration between a son who played and a father who had stopped playing and never said why.

Dr. Marsh had saved for Juilliard since Jackie was five. A separate account. Monthly deposits. Jackie had found the bankbook once, in the desk drawer in his father's study, and the column of numbers had stretched across three pages, twelve years of deposits, the date and the amount and nothing else.

His father had packed the guitar in a case and put it in the attic and chosen the career, the stability, the rational path. Every day since, he had been pouring whatever he sacrificed into his son — not the same dream, a translation of it, the classical version his father believed was the safe version of the dangerous thing he had wanted.

He was wrong. Jackie knew that. His father believed in the wrong version of him — the recital version, the competition version, the version that played Chopin with impeccable technique and empty hands. But the belief itself was real. The fourteen years were real. The drives, the deposits, the three drafts of the application. His father had built a bridge from his own abandoned dream to his son's future, and Jackie couldn't burn it. Not even for this.

Jackie closed his eyes. "Me either."

The words fell into the room like stones into water.

"It's not giving up," Nick said. His voice was rough. He cleared his throat and it didn't help. "It's choosing the people who already chose us."

Maxanne pushed off the tape machine. She walked to the window that looked into the control room, where Mike sat with his headphones around his neck, watching them through the glass with the expression of a man who understood he was witnessing something he would spend years trying to describe.

"That's what I told myself in 1977," Maxanne said. Her back was to them. Her reflection floated in the glass, translucent, superimposed over the meters and the blinking lights of the equipment that recorded and preserved other people's music into the dark.

Jackie stood. He looked at Nick. Nick looked back.

"If things were different," Jackie said.

"Yeah," Nick said.

Jackie extended his hand. Nick took it. They shook — one firm grip, held for two seconds, released. Not a hug. A hug would have been too much.

Nick went out through the studio door. Jackie went out through the control room. Separate exits. The hallway split and they walked in opposite directions, toward opposite ends of the building, toward the separate lives that were waiting for them the way debts wait — patient, certain, compounding.

---

Maxanne stood alone in Studio C. The acoustic panels absorbed her reflection. The boom mics angled toward empty chairs.

Mike appeared in the control room window and pressed the talkback.

"What do we do with this?" He held up the reel — seven inches of magnetic tape, four minutes and forty-three seconds, everything.

Maxanne looked at the reel in his hand. You couldn't break a band that had already broken itself.

"We preserve it," she said. "Because somebody needs to remember this was possible."

Mike passed it through the window. She took it in both hands. Light. Lighter than it should have been for what it carried.

She slid the reel into a cardboard sleeve and tucked it under her arm. She turned off the studio lights. She walked out of WBCN into the three-AM dark on Boylston, where the parking lot held one car — Grace Johnson's wagon, engine idling, headlights off — and two empty spaces where two boys had already gone.
