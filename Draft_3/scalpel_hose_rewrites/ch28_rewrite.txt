CHAPTER 28: NICK'S PERFORMANCE

He was in the wings when Jackie's voice cracked.

Nick had been watching the gap between the curtain and the proscenium wall — a slice of stage, the back of Jackie's head at the piano — running the set in his head the way a guy runs a road before he drives it. Then Jackie hit the note in *Somebody to Love* and the note did not come out the way eleven years of lessons had built it to come out. It cracked. The auditorium did the thing eight hundred and fifty people do when they are holding one breath together — a half-second where nobody coughed.

Nick felt it in the knuckle of his left hand.

He had come up the side stairs planning to drop a hammer on Jackie — louder, faster, dirtier — and what had just happened out there was not something a hammer could answer. The set was the set. Four songs.

The Tele was Mr. Wentworth's. Late-sixties butterscotch blonde, clearcoat rubbed off where the teacher's forearm had lain for twenty years. Wentworth had met him at the music-room door at seven, handed it over in its gig bag without taking it out, and said *Back Friday*, and then, two steps away, over his shoulder, *The neck'll pull sharp under the lights — keep your ear on the B.* No speech. Nick had not trusted his voice to say thanks.

He wore his least-torn jeans. A black t-shirt with nothing on it. Dan's work boots, a size too big, two pairs of socks, the left heel lifting a quarter-inch when he walked.

Crown Jewels came off the way bands come off a stage they have just done the thing on. Jackie passed him six feet out, did not look at him. The bass player touched Nick's shoulder with two fingers going by. Nick did not try to say what that meant.

"Walking the Line," the stage manager said, not loud. "On deck."

He rolled his neck once. Closed his left hand, opened it. The tips of his fingers hit the calluses and stopped, and the calluses meant the hand was still his. He followed Stephen and David and Mark through the curtain-break.

The heat hit first. Four par-cans twelve feet up, putting the light down the way a kitchen window in August puts the sun down. His scalp started sweating before he reached the monitor. The stage smelled of somebody else's hairspray and the gaffer's tape on the setlist. The microphone smelled of Lysol. Below the lip of the stage the first three rows were pale ovals and the rest of the house was dark. He found his mother in the third row without trying. Purse on her lap, both hands on the clasp.

Stephen's Univox was humming, cord curled at his ankle the way Stephen kept it. David sat behind his uncle's Ludwig with the cracked crash riveted back together by a brass washer his father had drilled on the basement bench. Mark was at the borrowed Rhodes, the one that buzzed on the B below middle C. He had his thumb off that key already.

Nick plugged the Tele into the Fender Twin on loan from the band room. He hit the low E with the side of his thumb. The amp said yes. He ran the volume to seven, the tone to eight, and stepped to the microphone. He did not lean into it.

"Thanks," he said.

One word. He stepped back.

He did not look for anyone else. Not yet.

He did not look at the wings stage-right where Jackie had gone.

David counted it under his breath — *one-two-three-four* — and Nick dropped the opening riff of *Walk This Way* on the downbeat.

The riff was Joe Perry's thing — three fingers on the fretboard, the pick on the downstroke, the muted chug between the two accents, the accent on the two-and, and the auditorium got hit with it at once because that was how the song worked. You did not ease into *Walk This Way*. It came in at your waist and lifted. Stephen's bass got underneath him on the second bar. David's kick tucked in. Mark laid a Rhodes chord on top like a sheet going over a bed.

Nick did not sing the verses. Tyler's syllables — *backseat lover on the side of the road* — lived in a mouth nobody on this stage had. He carried the vocal line on the top two strings of the Tele, octaved, syllable-tight, and the chorus he hit with the open A and let the band find him. The northeast section came up out of their seats on the third bar. A kid in front yelled something that was not a word.

They ended on the tag. David hit the crash and the brass washer rattled against the bronze and the cymbal sounded like a trash-can lid. The house was loud.

Nick waited for it to come down. He did not speak. He turned the Tele's volume knob a half-notch and looked at Stephen.

Stephen started *Sweet Emotion*.

The descending figure Tom Hamilton had built the song on — low G, F-sharp, E, D. Stephen had practiced it in his bedroom in North Sudbury for a month and played it now like he had owned it his whole life. Nick let him walk alone for four bars. Mark laid a Rhodes pad under, barely there. The house got quiet because the song wanted the house quiet.

Then Nick brought the talk-box line in without a talk-box. You couldn't buy a Heil for love or money on Dutton Road. He had worked the part out with the tone knob pulled halfway off and a borrowed wah held at the sweep-middle under his boot. He fingered the line on the D and G strings and rocked the toe of the boot in a slow quarter-inch arc, and what came out of the Twin was not a talk-box; it was a nasal rub, a close-mouthed vowel, the shape of Joe Perry's trick without Joe Perry's gear. It worked because it had to work.

In the third row Grace Johnson did a thing Nick caught only in the corner of his eye — she let go of her purse clasp. Her hands lay flat in her lap.

He did not look again. The song demanded the fretboard.

They took it through the break and out the far end, and when David hit the final crash the feedback off the Tele caught the Twin and went to a squeal, high and clean, and Nick brought his right palm down on all six strings at the bridge and the squeal died mid-note. His palm stung. He did not look at it.

He nodded at Mark. Mark laid in the one-bar figure for the next one on the Rhodes. Nick hit the riff.

*Train Kept A-Rollin'*.

The Aerosmith version — heavier, meaner, the stutter on the downbeat Joe Perry had built off Jeff Beck and made his own. The stutter was in the pick hand. Nick muted with the heel of his right palm and the riff came out of the Twin like somebody hitting sheet metal with a tire iron in rhythm. Stephen locked on. David's kick took the stutter and doubled it. Mark kept off the B.

Halfway through the second verse he felt the tremor. It started in his left pinkie — the shake that had been showing up in rehearsals since Sunday, the one he had not told anybody about — and he fingered it out by tightening his grip a half-pound. The train kept rolling. The taste at the back of his throat was copper.

They took it out on the double-time and David hit the crash and the cracked cymbal rattled again, and Nick stood with the Tele against his hip and his chest heaving.

Three down. One left.

He looked at Mark.

Mark played the piano intro of *Dream On* on a Rhodes that buzzed on the B below middle C.

The Rhodes was not a piano. It had a bell in the top that a Steinway did not have, and it rolled in a way an upright did not roll, and when Mark laid the intro down it was not the Tyler piano part from the record — it was Mark's version of it, on a borrowed electric piano with a buzzing B, and it was the thing Nick had been hearing since the summer he was fourteen in the garage on a nicked-up copy of the first album.

Nick came in on the open A.

He did not sing. He played the vocal line on the Tele, single-note, clean tone, no wah, Wentworth's forearm-worn butterscotch handling the melody the way his mother handled a plate she didn't want to break. Stephen went to whole notes. David went to the ride. Mark stayed off the B.

He let his eye travel. He did not want to. He found her again — third row, aisle seat, the navy dress she had worn to the Curtis talent show in '75, to St. Elizabeth's on Sundays, and to the graveside at Beechwood on a Wednesday in November seventeen months ago when the ground had not yet frozen. Her purse was in her lap. Her hands were not on the clasp anymore. Her hands were flat on the dress the way they had been at the kitchen table the morning after, waiting for the next thing.

She was looking at him and she was not looking away. Across eight hundred and fifty people he held her there for the length of the solo's pickup bar. If he held her any longer he would not finish the song. He looked back down at the fretboard.

The solo came up. The Dan solo. The one Dan had bent over him in the garage one Sunday afternoon the summer he was fourteen and put the fingering into his left hand with a hand of his own — a hand that had hefted ninety-pound bags of Quikrete and turned a wrench on Fords since 1952. *Thumb anchored, Nicky. Bend on the second. Breathe on the pause.* Nick took it slow. He took it the way Dan had taken it. The bend on the second fret bit his fingertip because the pad there was not the pad he had rehearsed with — that was the acoustic's pad — and the Tele's strings were lighter and cut differently. He let the bite happen.

He came out of the solo and let the band ride the chord. Mark held the Rhodes. Stephen went long. David went to a wash on the crash.

Nick did not sing *Dream on, dream on*. There was no Steven Tyler on the Lincoln-Sudbury stage. He played the melody of the last chorus on the top string of the Tele, clean, and it cut the par-can haze and went out over the third row and the fourth and the back of the house, and he came down off the high note and back to the A, and Mark closed it on the Rhodes chord and David let the ride die on its own.

For a half-second the house was quiet the way Jackie had made it quiet.

Then it came.

The applause was not the *Walk This Way* applause. It was something else. Nick did not have a word for it. He stood with the Tele against his hip and did not lift his arm. He nodded once, not at anybody in particular.

He unplugged the Tele. The cord coiled at his feet the way Stephen's always coiled at Stephen's. He laid the Tele across the top of the Twin the way Mr. Wentworth had shown him — flat, strings up, not leaning. Stephen was saying something behind him that had the word *Nicky* in it and ended with a clap on his shoulder.

Nick walked off stage left.

In the wings the stage manager had a clipboard and a pencil and a headset on one ear. He did not look up.

"Ready Rumours," the stage manager said into the headset.

Nick walked past him. His left hand was shaking a small shake and he put both hands in the pockets of his jeans and kept walking.
