CHAPTER 19: GRACE LOSES HER GRACE

Grace Johnson carried the two pots the way she had carried them for twenty-three years: regular in her right hand, orange collar on the glass; decaf in her left, brown collar. Dr. Taub drank decaf. Had drunk decaf since 1956, the year she started and he was still new to his practice, thirty-two and just back from Korea with his wire-framed glasses and a cardigan his wife had knit him and a bedside manner he had not yet grown into. Grace went to his table with the brown-collared pot already tilted, because at Friendly's on Route 20 you did not ask Dr. Taub which coffee he wanted. You poured the decaf and you said good morning, Dr. Taub, and you did not stand any longer at his booth than the pour required.

Booth twelve was the booth by the window. The window looked out on the parking lot and the Route 20 traffic going east toward Wayland, and the Impala in the second row and Grace's wood-paneled '67 station wagon in the first. Above her, the fluorescent flickered the way it had flickered since Tom Andrews had taken over in '71 and failed to fix it. The ice cream case against the back wall hummed through its blue light. MINT CHOCOLATE CHIP, the little cardboard flag said in its Friendly's-green script, stuck into a tub gone frost-bristled at the edges. At booth seven a kid was working on a Jim Dandy with a long spoon. A booth down from him, two cops split a Fishamajig basket. The air was what the air at Friendly's always was — fryer oil laid over coffee laid over the bleach on the rag in her apron pocket.

Dr. Taub had the Sudbury Weekly folded open to page three. The picture on page three was Nick, head down over the Les Paul copy in the music-room shot they had run with the WBCN story, the same shot that had been on WBCN's newsbrief last Thursday when the PTA fight went statewide for eleven seconds. Dr. Taub was a man of seventy now. The suit jacket hung over the back of the booth. Under it he wore the same kind of cardigan he had worn when she was twelve and his wife had been Ruth Lindstrom, in the L-S class of '55 two years behind her, and the two of them had sat at the same lunch table in a cafeteria that did not exist anymore. He did not remember that. He had never needed to.

"Grace," he said. He did not look up from the paper. "I've been reading about this Battle."

"Yes, Dr. Taub." She poured. Brown collar. Steady.

"I worry about these boys. Yours. The Marsh boy." He touched the photograph with his index finger, the way he touched a chart. "The competition sets them up. Expectations. Talent alone — you know — it takes training. Connections. Most of these kids are going to be disappointed."

There was a small brown stain on the lapel of his cardigan he had not noticed. Coffee, or something coffee-adjacent, from a morning older than today. She noticed it.

"Disappointed," she said.

"I delivered that boy, Grace." He said it the way a man lays down a trump. "I delivered Nick in March of '62 at Emerson. I'm not against him. I'm *for* realism."

The pot in her left hand was half full. She did not set it down. She did not slam it. She held it.

"My son has been playing guitar since he was eight."

She said it the way she would have said, *the oven's on.* And she was looking, as she said it, past Dr. Taub's cardigan shoulder, through the window of booth twelve, and the late-morning sun on the parking lot caught the chrome handle of the station wagon in a flash she would not have let herself notice any other day — the handle of her third car, the wagon she'd bought used in '74 after Dan's truck needed a clutch, and for a single beat the light on the chrome was the light off a different door handle on a different car in a different driveway a long time ago, and she swallowed around it and did not let her voice shake.

"He practices every day in the garage his father built. He's won every talent show he's entered since he was nine. WBCN called my house. Not his. Mine. And you're drinking decaf in my section at eleven in the morning and telling me he's going to be disappointed."

Dr. Taub opened his mouth.

"Enjoy your coffee, Dr. Taub."

She moved to booth seven. Jim Dandy kid. "You need anything, honey?" He shook his head. She moved to the cops. Refilled them both. Regular. Orange collar. She did not look back at booth twelve. She did not need to. The whole dining room had gone a shade quieter, the way a pond goes quiet when something has fallen in it.

---

Tom Andrews was forty-eight and had been the Friendly's manager on Route 20 for eight of those years. His office was a cinder-block closet behind the walk-in, with a gray metal desk, a swivel chair with duct tape on the arm, a bank calendar from Middlesex Savings still turned to March. He had grown up on Horse Pond Road. He had poured concrete on the same crew as Dan Johnson for two summers in the early sixties. He had been at the funeral.

"Sit down, Grace."

"I'll stand, Tom."

"Sit down."

She sat.

He did not look at her while he said the sentences his job required him to say. Professionalism. Customer relations. The uniform meaning something. The word *uniform* was doing work in his mouth that the word was not built for. He finished. He set both hands flat on the desk.

"Fifteen more cents an hour."

"Thanks, Tom."

"Get back to work, Grace."

"Yes, sir."

She stood. She untied and retied the apron. Her name tag had come loose on the left pin; she straightened it. GRACE, in the raised plastic letters, and under it MANAGER, and the small gold foil star the company had sent in February. She went back out to the floor.

---

He came in at four-ten, through the glass door with the Friendly's-cone sticker on it, and he stood by the register with his L-S jacket on and his hands in his pockets and his weight on his right leg. Dan had stood like that. Waiting for Grace to finish a shift, Dan had always tilted the weight off the bad knee, and Nick had never once in his seventeen years been told to copy the stance, and there it was. Grace saw it from booth four, where she was wiping syrup off a formica edge, and went still for a half-second and then kept wiping.

He crossed to her.

"Mom."

"It's fine, Nick."

"I heard."

"You deserve a chance."

"You didn't have to do that."

"Yes, I did."

She folded the rag. She did not look at him right away. When she did, his face had the Friendly's sign in the window of it — the cursive neon red reflected on the lens of his reading glasses, which were her reading glasses, which he had grabbed off the counter when he came in, and he did not know he was wearing them. She almost said so. She didn't.

"Wait on the bench by the door," she said. "Two minutes."

He waited on the bench by the door. She took the apron off in the back. She hung it on the hook that had her name taped above it in Tom's block print. She clocked out. She walked past the ice cream case with its blue light and the mint chocolate chip and the Jim Dandy sign and out through the glass door, and Nick stood up off the bench and walked out beside her, and above them the Friendly's sign in its ice-cream-cone cursive threw red over the hood of the station wagon and red over the shoulders of her polyester and red, a softer red, over the side of Nick's face. Eight days to the Battle.

They did not speak in the parking lot. He opened the driver's side for her the way Dan used to. She got in. He walked around to the passenger side. For a moment, before he opened his door, he stood in the lot with his hand on the roof of the wagon and looked back at the diner where his mother had just, without once saying the word, defended him in front of the doctor who had delivered him and the town that had watched her serve coffee for twenty-three years.

Then he got in, and she started the engine, and they went home up Route 20 with the red sign fading in the rearview and the spring light going long over the Cavicchio fields.
