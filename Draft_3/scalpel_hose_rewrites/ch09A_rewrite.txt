CHAPTER 9A: MAXANNE'S DISCOVERY

Three hours and nineteen minutes.

The talent show story settles over the booth like the last note of a song nobody wants to follow. Two thirteen-year-olds in a gymnasium, harmonizing like they'd stumbled onto the frequency the rest of us spend our lives scanning for.

I take a pull of the Pappy. Let the burn settle.

"You want to know how I got here?" I say into the microphone. "Not the booth. Not WBCN. I mean *here* — the woman telling you about two kids whose dreams she helped kill. You want to understand how a person goes from rebel to executioner in six years? You've got to go back further than Sudbury. Further than 1979. You've got to go all the way back to a bar in Allston that smelled like spilled beer and ambition, and a twenty-three-year-old who was stupid enough to believe that talent and conviction were the same thing."

I spin the chair toward the album wall. Third row, left side. My hand finds it without looking — Aerosmith's debut, the blue wings on cream, January '73. The vinyl's in pristine condition because I never let anyone else touch it.

"1973. Twenty-three years old. About to find the real thing for the first time."

The memory takes me like a riptide.

---

Tuesday in January. The kind of cold that makes your teeth hurt and your breath hang in the air like tiny ghosts. I drove my Dodge Dart down Commonwealth Ave — heater worked when it felt like it, which was almost never — heading to a bar on Brighton Ave called the Brickyard because a sound engineer named Paulie DeLuca had cornered me at the station that afternoon and grabbed my arm.

"Maxanne, there's a band playing the Brickyard tonight. You need to hear them."

"What kind of band?"

"The singer moves like he's trying to shake demons off his skin. The guitar player looks like he'd kill you and not lose sleep over it. They sound like what you'd get if the Stones and the Yardbirds had a kid and raised it behind a dumpster."

Paulie had seen ten thousand bands and been impressed by maybe six. So I went.

The Brickyard sat between a laundromat and a sub shop, a narrow room with a ceiling so low the tall guys hunched, a stage the size of a dining room table, and a sound system that had been state-of-the-art around the Eisenhower administration. The floor stuck to your shoes. The bathroom door didn't lock. The neon Budweiser sign in the window buzzed like a dying insect. The room held maybe twenty people. Half of them teenagers with fake IDs and nowhere warmer to go. A woman at the bar read a paperback, unbothered by anything. The bartender — a thick-necked guy named Sal — wiped glasses with a rag dirtier than the glasses.

Then five guys walked onto that postage-stamp stage, and the air in the room changed.

The singer first. Twenty-four, rail-thin, lips that belonged on a different species, hair that looked like it had been in a fight with a light socket and lost. He wrapped his hands around the microphone stand like it was the only thing between him and the floor. Didn't say hello. Didn't introduce the band.

He looked at the guitar player — dark-haired, cheekbones sharp enough to cut glass, eyes that said he'd already seen things he wasn't going to share with you — and nodded.

The guitar player hit the opening riff.

I've spent my whole career trying not to sound like a tent-revival preacher, so I'll just say this: the sound that came off that stage was alive the way a downed power line is alive. Dangerous, electric, throwing sparks into the night and not caring who gets burned.

Steven Tyler sang like a man who'd swallowed fire and was coughing up something beautiful before it consumed him. Joe Perry played guitar like each note was a personal argument he intended to win. The bass player laid down grooves that producers would spend thousands trying to replicate. The drummer kept time like a heartbeat in a chest that was running out of reasons.

They played forty-five minutes. Four people left during the set — the woman with the book, a couple who'd been fighting since I walked in, some kid who probably had a curfew. The rest of us stood with our mouths open like we'd forgotten how jaws worked.

I had my portable Panasonic cassette recorder in my bag. I carried one everywhere — the day I talked my way into WBCN I swore I'd never let a sound I believed in go unrecorded. The tape quality was garbage. Tinny, distorted, the Brickyard's acoustics making everything sound like it was being played inside a coffee can. But underneath the noise you could hear it. The signal underneath the static that said *this is real*.

After the set I walked up to the singer. He leaned against the wall by the emergency exit, smoking a Camel, T-shirt soaked through.

"Maxanne Sartori. WBCN."

He looked at me like I'd told him I was from Neptune. "The radio station?"

"The one where I'm going to play your album tomorrow night."

He stared. Then he smiled — this enormous, disbelieving grin that transformed his face from dangerous to heartbreakingly young. "We have an album. Nobody plays it."

"I'm going to play it."

"You're going to play it."

"On WBCN. Tomorrow. Eleven o'clock. If I can find a copy."

He reached into a duffel bag and handed me a record like it was a relic. "Dream On," he said. "If you're only going to play one, play that."

I took the album and walked out into the January cold, and the air tasted different. Cleaner. Like the frequency had been adjusted and I was hearing the world properly for the first time in my life.

---

The next night I walked into WBCN at ten-fifteen. Charlie Morris, the station manager, was still in his office — unusual for Charlie, who believed the night shift ran itself or it didn't deserve to exist. He had his feet on the desk and a joint between his fingers and looked up at me with the tolerant suspicion of a man who'd built his station on the principle that rules were suggestions.

"You've got that look," he said.

"I found something."

"You always find something."

I put the Aerosmith album on his desk. "Track three."

He studied the cover. Studied me. Dropped the needle.

For four and a half minutes, neither of us breathed. When the piano came in, Charlie closed his eyes. When Steven Tyler's voice hit that open-throated scream, Charlie opened his eyes and looked at me like I'd just told him where they buried the treasure.

"Where'd you find this?"

"A bar in Allston. Tuesday night. Twenty people in the room."

Charlie picked up the album. Turned it over. Pointed at me with the hand holding the joint, ash dropping onto the desk. "This is what we DO, Maxanne. Find the real shit. Play it tonight. Eleven. Full volume."

Eleven o'clock, I dropped the needle on "Dream On" without announcing it, without context, without checking with the program director who'd left at six. Steven Tyler's voice pouring out of radios across greater Boston — into dorm rooms at BU and Northeastern, into apartments in Cambridge and Somerville, into cars on the Pike, into a city that didn't know yet what it was hearing.

The request line lit up before the song ended. Every light on the board, blinking like a switchboard plugged into something too powerful for its wiring. I played it again at midnight. Twice more before my shift ended at two. By Wednesday afternoon, kids walked into record stores asking for the album by name. By Friday, it had sold more copies in Boston than in the rest of the country combined.

That was discovery. A woman with a cassette recorder in a half-empty bar, hearing something true and having the nerve to share it.

---

Three months later, a kid walked into Studio C for his first radio interview. Twenty-three, skinny as a parking meter, leather jacket that had seen better decades, a bandana that looked like he'd stolen it from a gas station mechanic. He sat down across from the microphone and his hands shook — not nerves, exactly, but the vibration of a body that contains more energy than it knows what to do with.

"You're Bruce Springsteen," I said.

"That's what they tell me."

"From New Jersey."

"Asbury Park. Born to be anywhere else." He grinned. Wild. Unpracticed.

I leaned into the mic. "WBCN, 104.1. I'm sitting here with Bruce Springsteen, who just released his debut album, *Greetings from Asbury Park, N.J.* Bruce, welcome to Boston."

And this kid — unknown, unpolished, absolutely electric — leaned into the microphone and said, with the kind of pure unguarded joy that fame will burn out of you before you ever learn to protect it:

"This is my first time on the radio. My mom lives out in California. HI MOM!"

The control room cracked up. I cracked up. And somewhere out in California, a woman probably pressed her hand to her mouth and understood that her son was going to be all right.

That was 1973. The year I found Aerosmith in a bar in Allston and Springsteen walked in off the street with nothing but a guitar and his mother's love. The year WBCN was exactly what a radio station is supposed to be.

---

I open my eyes. 2009. Studio B. The ON AIR sign still pulsing its cardiac red.

The album wall stares back at me. Aerosmith. The Cars. Boston. J. Geils. All discovered within spitting distance of this booth.

"That was what we were," I say into the mic. My voice sounds old to me now. When did that happen? "We didn't market. We discovered. We didn't focus-group a goddamn thing. We listened. We put our ears to the ground and when the ground shook, we told people about it. And the ground shook often enough that we were right more than we were wrong, and the times we were right — the Aerosmith nights, the Springsteen mornings — those were the times that justified everything. Every bad call, every sleepless night, every screaming match with management about playlist freedom. Because we'd found the frequency. We knew what real sounded like."

I pick up the demo tape. Turn it in my hands. *Demo — Nick/Jackie — un-Authorized — March 1979.*

"But that was 1973. Six years later I had changed. The woman who walked into a bar in Allston and recorded a band on a Panasonic cassette — she was still inside me somewhere. Buried under six years of learning how the business really worked. Six years of watching the frequency get jammed by consultants and demographic reports and suits who thought music was a product you could optimize rather than a wild animal you could only hope to set free."

I set the tape down. Gently.

"In 1973, I found Aerosmith and my first instinct was to share them with the world. In 1979, I found Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh and my first instinct was to figure out how to use them. I told myself I was still discovering. I told myself the Battle of the Bands was about finding talent. But here's what thirty years of insomnia have taught me: you can lie to management, you can lie to your audience, you can even lie to two seventeen-year-old kids who trust you because you're the woman who broke Aerosmith. The one person you can't lie to forever is the woman staring back at you from the dark window of a radio booth at midnight."

Outside, a bus pulls away from the curb on Boylston, its taillights red as the ON AIR sign.

"Six years. From dropping 'Dream On' unauthorized at full volume to designing a Battle of the Bands with corporate sponsors and demographic targets and a tie clause I buried in the fine print because I thought I could engineer reconciliation the way I'd once engineered discovery."

I lean back. The chair creaks like old leather does — tired, holding on.

"But we're getting ahead of ourselves. Before the Battle, before the rules I wrote in the dark, before the meeting where everything turned — first you need to hear what happened to Nick and Jackie after that talent show. How the golden moment tarnished."

I cue "Dream On." The album cut. The piano intro that still sounds like someone praying in a church they're not sure they believe in.

"Because you can't understand the war until you remember the peace. And the peace between those boys was the most real thing I'd encountered in four decades of rock and roll. What happened to it — what we let happen, what I helped happen — is the reason I'm sitting in this booth, burning the last of my borrowed time."

I press play. Steven Tyler's voice fills Studio B, fills the airwaves, fills whatever's left of a city that once believed a sound from a dive bar could change the world.

I close my eyes and let the music say what I can't.

**END CHAPTER 9A**
