CHAPTER 8: BEFORE THE TALENT SHOW

Three hours and forty-seven minutes left.

I'm sitting here in Studio B, the clock on the wall ticking past two a.m., and behind the glass my engineer — a college kid in a Bruins hoodie who thinks music starts with a laptop — is queued up on Fleetwood Mac's "Go Your Own Way." The opening guitar figure bleeds through the monitors at low volume, Lindsey Buckingham picking at something raw, and I realize I've been feeding you the end of the story without ever serving up the start. The Battle of the Bands in 1979 — that's the big finale, sure, but tonight we need to rewind the tape. Not the first garage jam, not even the friendship between Nick and Jackie, but the instant two best friends looked at a middle school talent show and thought, hell, why not give it a shot?

I take a sip of coffee — gone cold hours ago, but it's fuel either way — and I think about May 15, 1975. The day Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh stopped being two kids who played music in a garage and became a problem for every parent who thought the world came with a seating chart.

May 15, 1975. The annual Ephraim Curtis Middle School Talent Show. Open to all comers, one winner, and a gymnasium full of parents who'd spent the afternoon convincing themselves that their children were special without being too different, talented without being too unusual, competitive without being too ambitious.

Outside the booth windows, Boston glitters in the October darkness, but I'm seeing Sudbury in May, the kind of spring afternoon that makes thirteen-year-olds believe anything is possible.

I spent fifteen years at WBCN looking for kids who could win a Rumble at The Rat. Middle school talent shows in suburban Massachusetts in 1975 were the opposite of that. They weren't about discovering new voices. They were about maintaining the social order — proving that the kids who were supposed to win would win. The Rat rewarded danger. Talent shows rewarded safety.

I reach for the cassette on my desk, the one labeled *Demo — Nick/Jackie — un-Authorized — March 1979*. But tonight I'm not playing that tape. Tonight I'm telling you about the day they decided to make it.

Nick and Jackie had been playing together for almost two years by then. Garage sessions on Dutton Road, acoustic guitars and a keyboard Jackie hauled over on his bicycle, two years of arguing about whether Mercury or Tyler understood something deeper about what a voice could do. They were tight, and they were good, and nobody outside that garage knew it.

The decision happened on a Tuesday afternoon in April. Nick was in the garage, working on a riff he'd caught on WBCN — Zeppelin's "The Ocean," that heavy descending lick he was trying to crack on his acoustic. Jackie sat on Dan's workbench, feet dangling, watching Nick's fingers on the frets.

"We should enter the talent show," Jackie said.

Nick looked up from his guitar. "What talent show?"

"The middle school one. May 15th. Mrs. Reed mentioned it in music class today."

Nick went back to his riff, but the idea was taking hold.

"Why would we do that?"

"Because we're good," Jackie said, and there was something in his voice that surprised both of them. Not bravado. Recognition. "Because we're really good, and nobody knows it except us."

I glance at the console — the old Neve 8068 with its rows of faders and the coffee ring I've been meaning to wipe off since 1986 — and let the moment settle.

That was the hinge. Not when they became friends, not the first garage jam, but when Jackie said "nobody knows it except us" and Nick realized he was right. Proof that nobody sees doesn't change anything. You have to carry it out and let people argue about it.

They spent three weeks preparing. Not practicing — preparing. Translating garage magic into something that would land in a gymnasium full of fluorescent lights, folding chairs, and parents who thought Freddie Mercury was a Roman god.

Their final choice was a mashup — Queen meets Aerosmith, classical training meets garage-band raw power. Jackie's descending Bohemian Rhapsody chords laid across the groove of Nick's Aerosmith riff, the same collision they'd stumbled into the previous October, but tighter now, arranged, with vocal harmonies they'd rehearsed until the garage walls rang. Risky as hell. Mashups demand that the audience understand both songs and recognize that what they're hearing isn't performance but creation. Middle school talent shows aren't built for that kind of risk.

An ambulance wails down Tremont. Behind the glass, my engineer looks up from the mixing board, peering at me the way you watch a pot that might boil over. I crack my knuckles and keep going.

The day of the talent show, Nick and Jackie arrived with their guitars and no expectations beyond proving something to themselves.

Karen Bowers was closing the show — blonde, privately coached, voice like a weapon built to spec. Singing "Tomorrow" from Annie, because of course she was.

The gymnasium was packed. Eight acts. Karen anchored the last slot — the traditional winner's position. Nick and Jackie drew sixth.

The first five acts went the way first five acts always go. A girl did "Feelings" on a recorder. Twin brothers performed a magic trick that ended when the rabbit escaped into the bleachers. A boy in a clip-on tie played "Für Elise" on a piano missing its D above middle C, and every time the melody hit that note the silence was louder than the music.

Then Nick and Jackie walked out.

Two kids. Two acoustic guitars. No clip-on ties, no sheet music, no safety net.

You know that night I put Springsteen on the air for the first time — kid leans into the mic and yells *HI MOM!* because he didn't know what else to do with the terror. Nick and Jackie didn't have that kind of loose charm yet. They stood there for a second under the fluorescent lights, two best friends from opposite sides of Hop Brook, and looked at each other. Nick nodded. Jackie counted them in — one, two, three, four — and they hit it.

The Bohemian Rhapsody chords rang out first, Jackie's fingers finding that cathedral sound on the acoustic. Then Nick came in underneath with the Aerosmith groove, that dirty, swinging riff, and the two lines locked the way they'd locked in the garage — Nick's guitar the engine, Jackie's the wingspan. The sound bounced off folding chairs, cinder-block walls, basketball hoops, and came back as something the gymnasium had never held before.

Nick sang in his rough, throaty voice — "sweet emotion" — and Jackie answered with "Is this the real life" in a clear tenor that cut through everything, and the two vocal lines braided and pulled apart and braided again. You could feel the room shift. Not the standing ovation kind. Something quieter and more dangerous. Parents looking at each other. Teachers along the wall no longer whispering. Even the kid whose rabbit was still loose under the bleachers forgot about the rabbit.

They played for four minutes. When they stopped, the silence lasted three full seconds — that airless pause before the gymnasium remembered it was supposed to react.

Then the applause came. Not polite. Not measured. The kind of noise that starts in the gut and doesn't ask permission.

I lean back in my chair and the springs creak. The Fleetwood Mac track has ended and the monitors hum soft static — that warm analog hiss that sounds like the space between songs.

Here's what the judges didn't know how to do with Nick and Jackie. They didn't fit a category. They weren't classical, weren't rock, weren't a novelty act. They were two kids who'd built a sound out of argument and love, and the rubric on the judges' clipboards didn't have a box for that.

Karen Bowers won. Of course she won. She earned it — I'm not taking that away from her. But earning it and deserving it aren't always the same thing, and every parent in that gymnasium knew it, even if they'd never say so at the PTA meeting.

What mattered wasn't the trophy. What mattered was the four minutes when two kids from different sides of Hop Brook proved that the most threatening thing you can do in a town built on categories is refuse to pick one.

Clock on the wall says I'm running out of night. Out there, Boston sprawls under neon and streetlamp while my voice drifts from this booth to whoever's still tuned in.

But we're not done. We haven't gotten to what happened after — the way the loss hit Nick and Jackie differently, the way it cracked something open the garage sessions had kept sealed. That's next. Stay with me.
