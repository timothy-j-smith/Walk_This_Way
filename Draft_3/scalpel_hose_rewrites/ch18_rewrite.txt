CHAPTER 18: THE TOWN WEIGHS IN

By the last week of April the town had made up its mind without anyone calling a vote.

Grace Johnson felt it first at the Star Market on Route 20 with a ten-pound sack of Maine potatoes in the crook of her arm. The plastic netting was cutting a red line across the inside of her elbow and she was waiting her turn at register three. The cashier — Donna, the daughter of the older Donna who had worked this same register since Grace was a girl — rang the potatoes through without looking at them.

"Nick nervous?"

"He's fine."

"My Tommy says the whole school's picking sides."

"Tommy's a sophomore."

"Still."

She took her change in quarters because Donna was out of ones, and on the way to the door the pharmacist behind the raised counter of the Osco lifted two fingers and called across the shampoo aisle — *tell him good luck for us, Grace* — the way he might have said it at a wake. Grace lifted two fingers back. She did not break stride.

She put the potatoes in the back of the wagon and stood for a second with her hand on the tailgate, looking at the parking lot. A boy maybe nine years old was pushing a line of carts toward the corral. Two old men were talking by the ice machine. Nothing was happening. Everything was happening.

---

Tuesday was her one day a week off from Friendly's and she sat at the Sudbury Diner on Route 20 the way she had sat there every Tuesday for six years, in the back booth where the vinyl was split along the seam and the crack had been mended with a piece of black electrical tape. The Globe was open in front of her. The Red Sox had lost to the Yankees again.

The voices came over the top of the newsprint.

*— real talent versus noise —*

*— Marsh boy's had lessons since he was six —*

*— heart doesn't win anything —*

She did not look up. She turned the page. A Bradlees ad for lawn chairs.

"Grace."

Mary Murphy sat down across from her without asking. Mary's husband drove for the post office and had a brown Chevy Impala with a dented right-rear quarter panel where he'd backed it into the mailbox at the Fairbank Road turnaround in March and never gotten it fixed, and you could see the car from the diner window right now, the dent catching the late sun. Their son Stephen played bass in Nick's band.

"He holding up?"

"He's fine."

"Stephen says they're in the garage six hours a night."

"Something like that."

Mary leaned in. Her voice dropped half an inch. "Frank at the barber shop's got a pool going."

"On what."

"On Nick."

"Jesus, Mary."

"My Dennis put ten dollars down."

The waitress came by with the pot. The button over her apron pocket was a white circle with red letters — **WBCN 104.1 — THE ROCK OF BOSTON** — with the peace-sign logo underneath, slightly crooked where the pin had gone in a dozen times. She topped Grace's cup without asking, topped Mary's, moved on to the booth behind.

"The Weekly ran it again yesterday," Mary said. "Front page."

Grace had seen it. The photograph was Nick in the garage, three-quarters to the light from the open door, Dan's tools outlined in black marker on the pegboard behind him and the 1975 Aerosmith calendar hanging exactly where it had hung for four years. *BATTLE OF THE BANDS: TWO STUDENTS, TWO WORLDS, ONE STAGE.* Sponsored in part, the last paragraph said, by WBCN-FM. The reporter had called three times before Grace picked up. She had given him seven sentences.

"Dr. Marsh didn't like it," Mary said. "My neighbor works in the office. He called the school. Said they were turning a talent show into class warfare." Mary put quotes around *class warfare* with two fingers on each hand.

Grace sipped her coffee.

"There's a PTA meeting Thursday night. Emergency session. He's going to try to cancel it."

Grace set the cup down.

"Cancel it."

"Postpone until fall. For the kids' own good. You know the song."

"The Battle's in two weeks."

"I know."

Grace looked across the booth at Mary Murphy. Fifteen years they had lived three streets apart. Fifteen years of Little League and parent-teacher nights and hand-me-down snowsuits. Mary's house was the same house as Grace's house — the same square of chain-link, the same sagging gutter over the back door — paid for one month at a time.

"I work Thursday," Grace said.

"Get it off."

"I can't."

"Grace."

"I'll get it off."

---

Thursday the Lincoln-Sudbury library smelled the way it had smelled in 1953 — lemon oil the librarian rubbed into the oak shelves every morning before the doors opened, varnish gone amber on the long card catalog by the reference desk, paper and dust and the faint electric warmth of the fluorescent tubes overhead. Grace had checked out a copy of *The Yearling* from these shelves in the spring she turned fifteen. She had forgotten until she stepped through the door and the smell came up and found her.

She did not think about it for long. She did not have time.

The room was full. The good oak chairs filled first, in rows facing the librarian's desk; then the metal folding chairs along the walls, scraped out of the supply closet; then parents standing in the doorway. Someone's baby was fussing at the back. The PA system crackled once — a cough of static — and went quiet. Principal Sargent sat at the front with his hands flat on a legal pad, the expression of a man who had not asked for this.

Grace came straight from work. She stood along the side wall next to a radiator that was ticking. Brown polyester uniform. *GRACE — MANAGER* over her left breast. The smell of fryer oil was on her whether she wanted it to be or not.

Dr. Marsh sat in the front row with his wife. Blazer, gray slacks, the easy posture of a man who had sat on a dozen nonprofit boards and knew how long to wait before raising a hand. He raised it.

"Thank you, Brad." He used the first name the way a man uses a key he has been given. "I think we all want the same thing. And I think we all know that this — competition — has become something none of us signed up for. Students skipping class. Arguments in the hallway. A newspaper poll asking seventeen-year-olds to pick a side as if this were a selectman's race."

Around the room, heads moved. A woman two chairs over pressed her lips together and nodded once. A man in a Red Sox windbreaker folded his arms across his chest.

"What troubles me most," Dr. Marsh said, "is that we have let a talent show turn into a referendum. On what, I'll let each of you decide. I am asking this board to postpone the Battle of the Bands until the fall, when cooler heads —"

Grace said, "Excuse me."

Her voice was not loud. It carried because the room had gone still for it.

Principal Sargent turned his head. "Mrs. Johnson."

She walked down the center aisle between the oak chairs. She was conscious of her sneakers on the waxed floor and the fryer smell coming off her uniform and the fact that she had not had a sentence ready when she left her car. She stopped at the front. She did not go behind the little lectern Sargent had set up. She put her right hand on the back of the nearest oak chair and gripped it. Her knuckles went white around the wood.

Dr. Marsh, half-risen, sat back down. His right hand lifted and moved to his left breast pocket as if to take something out of it. The pocket was empty. His hand rested there a second and then came down to his knee.

"I'm Grace Johnson," Grace said. "Nick's mother. I work at Friendly's. I worked a double today."

She stopped. The coffee she had drunk in the car an hour ago had gone cold in her mouth and she worked it down once before she went on.

"Dr. Marsh is right. This stopped being about a talent show. Our kids didn't do that. We did."

A chair creaked at the back.

"My son plays a twenty-five-dollar guitar in a garage his father built. His father is dead. He works Saturdays at a body shop for strings. Two weeks from now he gets to stand on a stage and play for five hundred dollars and a day in a studio. That's not nothing. That's the whole thing."

She looked at Dr. Marsh. She did not make a speech of it.

"You want to postpone it until fall. Fall is when Jackie goes to conservatory. Fall is when my son is fixing cars."

Someone in the metal folding chairs said, quiet, *that's right.*

Grace's hand was still on the oak chair. She could feel the grain under her thumb. The baby at the back had stopped fussing.

"Let them play," she said. "Win or lose. Let them play."

She did not walk back to the side wall. She walked down the aisle and out the double doors and into the parking lot. The sodium lights had come on. Her hands were shaking by the time she got the key into the door of the wagon, and she sat behind the wheel with both hands on it for a minute before she turned the ignition.

She drove home down Route 20 past the lit sign of the Star Market and the dark sign of the feed store, and the speedometer needle sat an even five miles over the limit the whole way, because that was the speed she drove.

---

Mary called at ten-forty.

"Forty-two to eleven."

"Alright."

"Grace. You should've seen his face."

"I'm glad."

"Brad called the vote five minutes after you walked out. Wasn't even close."

"Alright, Mary."

"That was the bravest —"

"I have to be there at five-forty."

A pause on the line.

"Get some sleep," Mary said.

"You too."

Grace set the receiver back in the cradle of the rotary phone on the kitchen wall, and the small internal click of the hanger-bar dropping into place was the last sound in the house. The kitchen clock ticked. Upstairs the garage amp was off. She stood with her hand still on the receiver for a second, then she turned off the kitchen light and went up the stairs in the dark the way she had gone up them ten thousand times, her right hand on the banister where the varnish had worn thin to the grain under her father's hand and her own.
