CHAPTER 25: EVE OF THE BATTLE

---

Dutton Road was quiet at a quarter to ten. Nick lay on top of his bedspread in his jeans and listened to the house tick the way houses did after everyone was supposed to be asleep. The refrigerator. A pipe in the wall. His mother's shoes coming off in the kitchen.

He got up.

The side door of the house opened onto the breezeway and the breezeway onto the garage, and in the garage the one bare bulb came on the way it always did — a half-flicker and then the yellow. The April 1975 Aerosmith calendar was still tacked over the bench where Dan had left it, the page turned to a month that would never get flipped again. The red Craftsman toolbox sat against the far wall, latches rusted shut. On the plywood lid, a Maxwell House can held a 9/16ths socket and a worn spark plug. Nick had not moved either thing in three years.

The $25 acoustic was on its stand by the door. He picked it up. He did not plug in. The Lincoln boys had gone home an hour ago — Stephen's Nova pulling away, Mark's laugh from the passenger window, David shouting something about tomorrow — and the garage still smelled of their sweat and his own, and of the motor oil that had soaked into the concrete long before any of them were born.

He sat on a milk crate in front of the toolbox.

He played "Dream On" in the dark, unplugged, the pick against the strings barely louder than the ticking house. He played it the way Dan had showed him one Sunday — thumb anchored, bend on the second fret, breathe on the pause. Halfway through the solo his left hand stopped where his father's hand had always stopped to nod, and he nodded at nobody, and he went on.

He finished. He set the guitar across his thighs. His left hand was cramping; he flexed it once on the fretboard and stopped.

He looked at the latches.

He laid a palm flat on the plywood lid, the way he had seen his mother lay a palm flat on the kitchen table when she was tired. The Maxwell House can did not move. The 9/16ths socket did not move.

He did not open the toolbox.

After a while he turned off the bulb and sat in the dark with the guitar warming his legs, listening to a truck downshift on Route 20 a half-mile away, and he understood that tomorrow he would play for his mother and for the ghost in the toolbox and for the truck driver and for nobody, and that the order of those things was the only thing he still got to decide.

In the kitchen, his mother's light went out at ten-thirty.

---

Two miles north, on Maple Street, Dr. Richard Marsh's house faced a row of hedges clipped to the same height every April by the same man. Jackie's bedroom was on the third floor, under the eaves. The Steinway was two floors below him and might as well have been in another country.

He sat on the wide sill of the dormer window with his knees drawn up and the curtain half-drawn behind him. The streetlight on the corner laid a pale strip across the rug. His mother had gone to bed. His father was in his study, reading. The house was the kind of quiet that measured itself.

On the desk behind him, the Juilliard envelope sat on top of a stack of recital programs. Acceptance or waitlist, he hadn't opened it. He had promised himself he would not open it until after tomorrow, for reasons he had not examined.

Face down on the blotter, in a drugstore frame, was a Polaroid from Camp Elbanobscot, 1975 — three kids at the end of a dock, a borrowed guitar across one lap, a girl between the two boys laughing at something the photograph had not caught. He had turned it face-down in March. He had not turned it back.

Under his pillow, the little transistor radio was already on.

WBCN, 104.1, Boston, playing what WBCN played at a quarter to eleven on a Monday in May — some Dire Straits track, "Sultans of Swing," the guitar clean as a knife through paper. The DJ's voice came on at the end. It was not Maxanne's. Maxanne had been gone two years by then, though the station still sounded like a room she had walked out of.

He pressed his ear closer to the pillow. He had done this since he was eight, since he had first understood that the house had listening ears and that the radio was where people talked about music like it was a thing you loved, not a thing you graded.

The DJ announced the next song. Jackie did not catch the name.

He thumbed the dial. The radio clicked off.

Two floors below him, the Steinway sat closed in the dark, polished, waiting. He put his forehead against the window glass and watched the streetlight flicker once on Maple Street at ten-thirty.

---

On the other side of Sudbury, past the orchard and the cornfields and the long dip where Landham Road crossed the brook, the Lally farm sat low against the pines. The hogs had been fed and watered. The barn lights were off. A sow was grunting somewhere in the back pen, a soft complaint at nothing, the way sows did when they were settling.

Patty's bedroom was at the back of the house, over the mudroom. Wide pine floors. A braided rag rug her grandmother had made. A lamp with a yellowed shade that cast the room in the color of old paper. A *Rumours* poster was tacked to the slant of the wall — the black-and-white one, Stevie leaning on Mick — the corners curled from the woodstove heat that rose through the register in winter.

She lay on top of the quilt in her jeans and a white t-shirt, not sleeping.

On the little desk by the window, she had a sheet of her mother's stationery and a Bic pen. She had written *Dear Nick* and crossed it out, written *Dear Jackie* and crossed it out, written *Dear both of you* and crossed that out too. The sheet was in the wastebasket now, balled up the way she balled up things she meant.

A headlight — a late school bus from the Route 20 yard going home empty — swept across the ceiling on Landham Road and was gone.

Her transistor was on the nightstand, low. A DJ was reading a community bulletin. She was not listening.

She caught herself humming. She had been doing it all night, the way some people chewed the inside of their cheek. *You can go your own way, go your own way* — the bridge, not the chorus. She got two bars in and the hum caught in her throat the way a splinter catches in a finger, and she put her hand to her neck and stopped.

She was not going to fix them. She had told herself that at St. Michael's and she told herself that now. She only wanted to stand on the stage tomorrow and sing a song she loved, and let them hear what that sounded like.

Out in the barn, the sow sighed and was quiet.

The lamp went out at ten-thirty.

---

The Lincoln-Sudbury parking lot lay empty under its one sodium lamp, the stage door locked, the gym dark. A freight train pulled east toward Boston along the Route 20 cut, and the night closed behind it.
