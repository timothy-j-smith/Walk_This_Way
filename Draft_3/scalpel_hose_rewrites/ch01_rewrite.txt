CHAPTER 1: NICK'S WORLD — THE GARAGE

Maxanne Sartori drove up to the Johnson house in her powder-blue Karmann Ghia, the car bottoming out twice on potholes the town never fixed on the working-class side of Hop Brook. Sparks kicked off the undercarriage. She didn't care. She'd heard rumors about a kid on Dutton Road — a kid with a voice that could stop you mid-dial-turn, playing in a garage where the sound punched you in the chest before you opened the door.

The Johnson house squatted like it was tired: mustard paint peeling in long strips, gutters sagging, front steps listing to port. The lawn had surrendered to New England months ago, brown grass poking through dirty snow like stubble on a dead man's face. The bones of the house were good — wide clapboard, solid frame, the kind of place built by someone who knew what plumb meant.

From the detached garage came something alive. Distorted power chords hit the cold March air and traveled through the concrete foundation, through the frozen ground, up through the soles of Maxanne's boots and into her chest. She felt it before she heard it.

Grace Johnson answered the door in her Friendly's uniform, polyester clinging to her frame, name tag reading GRACE in that cheerful corporate font that lies about everything. She smelled like fryer oil and coffee and the particular exhaustion of a woman working her second double this week.

She studied Maxanne — bell-bottoms, oversized sunglasses, standing on the cracked front step like a visitor from a planet where people bought clothes that didn't come from the Jordan Marsh clearance rack.

"You're that lady from WBCN. The one who broke Aerosmith."

"Guilty as charged."

"Nick's in the garage." Grace paused. Something crossed her face — the memory of hope. "Don't fill his head with dreams we can't afford."

"Mind if I listen?"

Grace held her gaze a beat longer than comfortable. "He's been at it since school let out. Won't stop until dinner."

Maxanne crossed the yard toward the garage. The sound grew with each step — bass thrum in the frozen dirt, a snare crack that echoed off the neighbor's siding. The garage door hung open six inches at the bottom, enough to see slivers: a bare bulb swinging on its cord, the silhouette of a kid bent over a guitar, four concrete walls holding more noise than they were built for.

She ducked under the door.

Inside, the air was different — warmer, thicker, heavy with sawdust and motor oil and the metallic bite of old concrete. Dan Johnson's tools hung on a pegboard along the back wall, arranged by size. Claw hammers, box wrenches, a socket set with an empty slot where the 9/16ths used to live — the one he'd had in his pocket the November morning the cable snapped. Tucked behind a pipe wrench was a flattened guitar pick, tortoiseshell, worn thin. Below it, barely visible behind a rusted coffee can, the spine of a spiral notebook with staff lines printed on the pages.

Nick Johnson stood in the center of the garage, mid-solo, fingers moving on a pawn-shop guitar held together with electrical tape at the input jack and faith everywhere else. Twenty-five dollars at the Goodwill in Framingham. New strings from Store 24 when he could scrape together four bucks from mowing lawns. The frets were worn smooth in the first position from a year of playing the same progressions until his calluses cracked and bled and hardened into something useful.

Three other kids backed him — Lincoln boys, Maxanne would learn later, from the even-smaller town that shared a high school with Sudbury but not much else. They stopped when they saw her. That teenage terror of being caught caring about something. The music died mid-note like a bird dropping out of flight.

"Don't stop on my account," Maxanne said, settling onto a paint can. "Play it like you mean it."

They traded looks — four boys deciding whether to trust a stranger from actual radio. Nick decided for them. He launched back into "Dream On" with a fury that made the wrenches rattle on their hooks.

"Every time that I look in the mirror," he sang, "all these lines on my face getting clearer..."

His voice cracked on the high notes. The garage walls seemed to lean in. On the pegboard, a claw hammer vibrated against its nail, buzzing at a frequency only the concrete could hear.

Nick's eyes drifted to the tools. The music faltered — half a beat, barely noticeable, the way a runner flinches passing the spot where someone fell. Then his fingers found the riff again and drove it home.

When they finished, nobody moved. The last note hung in the air. Dust motes froze mid-float.

"What's your band called?" Maxanne asked.

Nick shrugged. "Don't have a name yet. We're just... playing."

"You got a demo?"

The other boys looked at Nick. He shook his head. "Can't afford studio time."

Maxanne pulled out her card. WBCN, 104.1 FM.

"Come by the station. We'll find some time."

Nick hesitated. His eyes moved between the card and the tools on the wall.

"Why?" he asked. "Why would you help us?"

Maxanne stood, knees protesting. "Because Steven Tyler played his first gig in a garage not much bigger than this one. The only difference between him and you is somebody opened a door."

She paused at the garage door, looking back at four boys holding instruments the way drowning people hold wreckage. She wanted to say more. She didn't.

Walking back to the Karmann Ghia, she heard the music start again behind her. "Sweet Emotion" — that bass line crawling out of the garage and across the frozen yard like something that refused to stay buried. Four boys with nothing to lose. The sound carried across Dutton Road, past the potholed asphalt and the bare trees, toward Hop Brook, where it died against the far bank.

Maxanne didn't know it yet, but she was about to meet the other half of the equation. Less than a mile away, on the other side of that brook, a boy sat at a Steinway preparing for a recital.
