CHAPTER 20: TEACHERS WHO FANNED THE FLAMES
**Third Person - May 5, 1979**
**Word Count Target:** 1,500 words

---

Bob Wentworth had been thinking about the photograph in his desk drawer. Nick and Jackie at twelve, arms around each other, holding guitars, smiling. The 1975 talent show. Before everything went wrong.

Tuesday afternoon jazz band rehearsal, two days before the Battle. Seventeen students warming up, the usual chaos of tuning and conversation. Nick on guitar near the window. Jackie at the piano across the room. They hadn't spoken in months.

"I want to try something different today," Wentworth said. He held up sheet music. "Dave Brubeck, 'Take Five.' It's in 5/4 time—off-balance, unexpected. Nick, Jackie, I want you two to work on this together. See if you can reimagine it."

The room went silent.

Nick looked at Jackie across the room. Two years of careful distance between them now.

"Mr. Wentworth," Nick said carefully. "I'm not sure this is a good idea."

"Sometimes the best way forward is to remember where you started."

Jackie was already moving to the piano. "I'll take the bass line." His voice steady, professional. Nothing like the kid he'd been at thirteen.

---

They started to play.

Jackie's left hand found Brubeck's unusual rhythm easily—classical training made the 5/4 time signature second nature. His right hand began adding flourishes, runs that transformed jazz sophistication into something more theatrical. Pure Freddie Mercury.

Nick listened. Found the spaces between Jackie's notes. Started threading blues-rock through the jazz structure. His guitar work tentative at first, testing the ice.

For thirty seconds, it worked.

Jackie's piano breathing. Nick's guitar answering. The rhythm off-balance but finding its center. Like two people remembering a conversation in a language they used to speak fluently.

Jackie added a crescendo. Nick matched it. The room disappeared. Just the music, the space between notes, the ghost of what they'd been.

Seventeen teenagers held their breath.

---

Then Don Gould walked in.

"Sounds like democracy in action!" His voice loud enough to shatter glass. "The American experiment in real time."

Nick's fingers stumbled. Jackie's right hand fell silent. Only the bass line continuing, suddenly lonely.

Gould taught AP American History, wore jeans on Fridays, encouraged students to call him "Don." He believed everything was a teachable moment.

"You know what this reminds me of?" Gould said. "The Lincoln-Douglas debates. Same fundamental tension, different century. Johnson here represents the Jacksonian tradition—man of the people, authentic democracy, rough edges and all. Marsh embodies the Jeffersonian ideal—educated, refined, excellence elevating everyone. Both American traditions!"

Nick stopped playing completely.

"We're just playing music," he said. His voice carrying an edge now.

"Are you though?" Gould smiled, warming to his theme. "Or are you playing out the eternal American tension between populism and intellectualism—"

"Between the kids who matter and the kids who don't?" Nick stood up so quickly his guitar strap caught on the music stand. Sheet music scattered across the floor. "Between the ones whose parents donate new instruments and the ones whose parents die in accidents that get blamed on worker error?"

Gould's face showed confusion. He'd thought he was facilitating dialogue.

"You want to make us a lesson?" Nick unplugged his guitar, movements precise and furious. "Fine. Here's your lesson. Jackie's going to Juilliard or Yale or wherever. I'm going to work construction like my dad. Maybe die quick like him too. That American enough?"

"Nick," Jackie said quietly. The first time he'd said Nick's name in two years.

"What?" Nick turned to face him. All the careful distance dissolving. "You want to pretend this is about music? Like we're equals because we can both play some notes?"

"That's not what I—"

"Your dad made it pretty clear. These boys don't understand boundaries."

Jackie's face changed. "That's not fair."

"No? Then why'd you move? Why'd you step aside like a good little prince letting the peasants pass?"

"Because I was trying to avoid this!" Jackie gestured at the space between them. "Because I thought maybe we could get through one week without—"

"Without what? Without admitting that Hop Brook might as well be the Atlantic Ocean? Without pretending your Queen covers aren't you slumming with real people?"

"Slumming?" Jackie's voice cracked. Suddenly he wasn't the professor's son or the classical prodigy. Just seventeen and tired of being blamed for advantages he'd never asked for. "You think this is slumming? You think I want—"

He stopped. Looked around the room. Their classmates watching. Their teachers judging.

"You know what? Forget it." Jackie's voice cold now. Controlled. "You want a war? Fine. Friday night, we'll see whose America wins."

He walked out. Didn't storm. Evacuated with dignity intact, the way his parents had taught him. The piano bench scraped against the floor.

---

Seventeen teenagers stared at Gould.

"I was just trying to provide historical context—"

"Don." Wentworth's voice carried exhaustion. "Get out."

---

May 7th, two days later. The hallways of Lincoln-Sudbury felt different. Nick and Jackie moved through the building like former friends who'd discovered they were enemies.

The Battle of the Bands was forty-one hours away.

Wentworth sat in his empty music room after school, looking at the photograph from 1975. Two boys, arms around each other, smiling. He'd tried to build a bridge with music. Gould had tried to build one with history lessons.

Both had failed.

You can't bridge a divide by explaining it. Can't heal a wound by describing its characteristics. Can't make harmony happen by pointing out it should be possible.

He put the photograph back in his drawer.

Friday night would answer the only question that mattered: what happens when it all comes crashing down?
