CHAPTER 19: GRACE LOSES HER GRACE
**Third Person - May 1, 1979**
**Word Count Target:** 1,200-1,500 words

---

Grace Johnson had been working at Friendly's for twenty-three years. Twenty-three years of polyester uniforms and name tags, of serving coffee to the same people, listening to the same conversations. She'd started in 1956, nineteen and newly married to Dan. What had seemed temporary became permanent. Problems multiplied. And here she was, forty-two, still wearing the same uniform.

The difference was, now she had a son about to compete in the Battle of the Bands. Now she had a son whose future hung in the balance of other people's opinions.

And she was tired of listening.

---

It started with Dr. Taub in booth twelve, reading the Sudbury Weekly's latest front-page story about the Battle. He looked up when Grace came to refill his coffee.

"I've been reading about this competition," he said. "Quite the controversy."

"Yes, Dr. Taub." Grace poured carefully.

"I'm concerned about how it's dividing the community. All this talk about class and privilege—it's as if people are using music to air grievances." He leaned forward, taking on the tone he probably used with patients who didn't understand their diagnosis. "Take your son, for example. Nick, isn't it? I've heard he's talented. But this competition is setting up unrealistic expectations. These kids will be disappointed when they realize talent alone isn't enough."

Grace's hand tightened on the coffee pot. "I'm not sure I follow."

"I'm just saying it's important to be realistic. The music industry is very competitive. It takes more than talent—it takes connections, resources, training that costs money. I'm concerned this competition is giving these kids false hope."

Grace set the coffee pot down. Not quite a slam. Not quite gentle either. "Are you suggesting my son shouldn't compete because he doesn't have the right training?"

"No, of course not. I'm just saying that talent alone isn't enough. These competitions can be discouraging for kids without the proper background."

"Proper background." Grace's voice changed. Something Dr. Taub had never heard in twenty-three years of her serving him coffee. "My son has been playing guitar since he was eight. He practices every day in the garage his father built. He's won every talent show he's entered. WBCN invited him to compete. And you're telling me he doesn't have the proper background?"

Dr. Taub shifted. "Grace, I didn't mean—"

"What exactly are you suggesting, Dr. Taub? That my son shouldn't compete because he's not from the right family? Because he doesn't have the right connections?"

"I think you're misunderstanding—"

"I understand perfectly." Grace's voice was steady now. Clear. "You're saying my son shouldn't compete because he's working class. Because his mother's a waitress and his father died in an accident. Because he doesn't have advantages."

Dr. Taub's face reddened. He looked around the restaurant. Other customers were listening. No one came to his rescue.

"You've been coming here twenty-three years," Grace said. "You've watched me serve you coffee. Listened to your opinions about everything. But you've never once asked about my son's dreams. Never asked about his talent. Now that he's entering a competition that might give him a chance, you're suddenly concerned about 'realistic expectations.'"

"Grace, I was trying to be helpful—"

"You're not being helpful. You're being condescending. You're telling me my son shouldn't compete because if he loses to someone with his background, what does that say about the world you believe in? The one where success goes to people who can afford it?"

Dr. Taub looked at his coffee cup. "I think we should end this conversation—"

"We're done." Grace straightened her uniform. "Enjoy your pie, Dr. Taub. It's made from apples grown in this town, served by people who live in this town. Maybe think about that next time you discuss what's realistic."

She walked to the next table—booth seven, young couple sharing dessert—and asked if they needed anything else. Her voice back to professional courtesy. As if nothing had happened. As if she hadn't just demolished the polite fiction that allowed Friendly's to function as neutral territory.

---

The manager, Tom Andrews, called her into the back room twenty minutes later. Fifteen years managing Friendly's, never seen anything like it. He had the conversation managers have with employees who violate the cardinal rule: never make customers uncomfortable with your humanity.

But Tom had grown up in Sudbury. Had worked construction with Dan Johnson. Had been to the funeral. Had watched Grace carry herself through circumstances that would have broken most people.

He gave her a lecture about professionalism and customer relations.

Then he gave her a raise.

Fifteen cents an hour.

Grace didn't know whether to laugh or cry. Fifteen cents for finally saying what everyone knew but pretended they didn't. For proving the help had opinions, feelings, children whose futures mattered.

---

The story spread through Sudbury by Monday morning. Everyone had heard some version of what happened at Friendly's. Details varied depending on who told it and what they needed it to mean.

Some said Grace had been rude to a customer. Some said Dr. Taub had been condescending to a waitress. Some said it was a misunderstanding. Some said it was about time someone stood up to the attitude that made working-class people feel like they didn't belong in their own town.

Nick heard about it at school. Three different people told him his mom had "gone off" on a customer. He drove straight to Friendly's after practice, found Grace wiping down tables at the end of her shift.

"Mom. I heard—"

"It's fine, Nick."

"Did you really tell off Dr. Patterson?"

Grace looked at her son. Seventeen years old, guitar calluses on his fingers, Dan's eyes. "I told him the truth."

"What truth?"

"That you deserve a chance. Same as anyone."

Nick was quiet for a moment. Then: "You didn't have to do that."

"Yes," Grace said. "I did."

She finished wiping the table. Folded the rag. Untied her apron.

"Come on," she said. "Let's go home."

They walked out together into the parking lot. The Battle of the Bands was eight days away.
