CHAPTER 24: PATTY RETURNS

May 14th, 1979. St. Michael's, Sudbury, a little after nine. The last car door in the parking lot had been Steve's Chevette — the driver-side hinge whining past halfway — and Patty had counted it the way she counted beats, and then she'd been alone.

Rumours had packed up an hour ago: Kristy's amp, Robin's kick pedal, Steve's snare in its padded bag. They'd hugged her at the parish-hall door the way you hug somebody the night before surgery, a little too long and too careful, and now the sanctuary was what it always was on a Monday night, which was empty.

She had moved the guitar in here on purpose. The acoustic was a hand-me-down from her uncle in Hudson, a Yamaha FG-180 with a scratch along the lower bout where somebody at some wedding had set down a beer bottle. It lived in a soft case with a broken zipper she held shut with a shoelace.

She took it out and sat it across her thigh and tuned by ear. E. Flat. She bent the tuning key a hair. The string came up under her thumbnail with the small sweet creak of bronze against bone nut. A. Fine. D. Fine. G. Sharp; she gave it back. B — B was always the one — she tuned it twice. High E. Fine.

She was wearing jeans with barn mud dried on the left knee, a flannel over her Rumours T-shirt, her hair braided over the right shoulder. Her fingertips had the pad calluses guitar players get. The back of her left hand had a pink line from last Tuesday, a strand of barbed wire that had caught her when she went in after a sow. On her right forearm, the half-circle of a hoof step was going yellow at the edges. She smelled faintly of pig. She knew it. She was not going to apologize for it to a room full of saints.

She got up and walked to the rack of votives by the side altar — three rows of red glass jars on a black iron frame, most unlit on a Monday. She took a long match off the box, struck it, and the head caught with that small chemical pop. She held the flame to a wick in the second row. The wax made a tiny suck of air taking the fire, and the flame straightened into an orange flower the size of her thumbnail, and the red glass around it went alive.

She did not say who it was for. She never said. She blew the match out, set it in the sand tray with the other spent ones, and went back to the pew.

Above the main altar the big window was St. Michael with his foot on the dragon's neck and his sword down and through — the dragon bottle-green in the lead seams, the saint the color of a bruise healing. Sunday's incense still hung in the wood. Melted wax. Worn oak. The Stations of the Cross marched around the nave in plaster relief. Jesus falls. Jesus falls again. Jesus falls the third time.

The hymnal was red. She rolled it back and forth along her thigh, the pebbled leatherette making a soft dry sound on the denim.

She opened the camp notebook.

The letters were inside the back cover. Seven of them, folded small, paper gone soft at the creases. Four from Nick. Three from Jackie. She took Nick's top one and opened it across her knee. The handwriting was cramped and sideways-slanted like he was running out of room even though the page was half empty, and in the third line he had crossed out *my dad* and written *my father* above it and then crossed that out too and put *him*. Three tries on two letters. The pen had pressed so hard on the last one that the paper had dimpled through to the back.

She folded it. She slid it back.

Jackie's top letter was thinner paper, the kind with the faint blue lines. The handwriting went straight across the page like a prayer. Every *t* crossed. Every *i* dotted. At the bottom, where he had written *I am learning the C-minor prelude and I can almost make it breathe*, he had underlined *almost* once, careful, with a ruler. Her thumb rode the crease. She put it back.

Nick's cramped urgent fingers. Jackie's ruler.

She thought about Jackie at the café downtown last fall, lifting a sugar cube with the silver tongs and lowering it onto the spoon and into the tea to watch it dissolve from underneath, the whole small operation while he told her his father thought Queen was a phase. She thought about Nick at Friendly's in March, a tuna melt cooling because he was drumming a tempo on the Formica with a butter knife, the waitress calling him honey twice and him not hearing her either time.

She let the letters sit. She did not want to sort them anymore.

She pulled the guitar back onto her thigh and played a D. Then a G. She slid to a C, her ring finger missed the third fret and buzzed against the second, and she laughed — out loud, in the empty sanctuary, one small startled bark. St. Michael did not move. The dragon did not move. She played the C again and this time it rang.

Then, without meaning to, she was humming. It came up out of her the way weather came up out of a field. *Loving you / isn't the right thing to do.* She didn't sing the words. She hummed the line, just the shape of it, the small step-down at the end, her right hand picking a quiet bass note underneath because the song wanted that bass note and her hand knew.

Camp Elbanobscot, the summer she was fourteen, the lake going copper at the end of the day. Nick's guitar had been out of tune and neither of them had cared. Jackie had played a chord nobody had asked for, a suspended thing with a fourth hanging in it, and her voice had come in over the top without thinking, and for about six seconds the three of them had landed inside one sound — lake water on the dock pilings, the sap-hot smell of pine, three voices and two guitars and nobody keeping count. They had not been trying. That was why it worked. They had been breathing, and the breathing had happened to rhyme.

She hummed another bar. She was on the word the song wouldn't give her — the turn between *you* and *isn't* — when a car door went in the parking lot. Short, metal, far side.

Her thumb went still on the G string. The car started. The car pulled out. It wasn't for her.

Her hand went into her bag and found the little white Panasonic transistor her brother had given her two Christmases ago, antenna bent from too many glove compartments. She almost turned it on. WBCN would be where she'd left it; it was always where she'd left it. She took her hand out of the bag. Not tonight.

She tuned the B string one more time because the B string was always the one. She played the opening of the song she wasn't going to play tomorrow — the first four bars, for herself — and let it die against the stone. She put the guitar in the case and tied the shoelace.

The votive was still going. The red glass was warm when she passed a hand near it, not touching. She dipped two fingers in the holy-water stoup by the door and crossed herself the way her mother had taught her — quick, four points, sternum and shoulders — without closing her eyes.

She pushed the door open with her hip, the guitar case in her right hand and the notebook in her left. The night air came in smelling of cut grass and diesel off Route 20. Behind her the little orange flower kept burning inside its red glass, and St. Michael kept his sword down through the dragon, and the hymnal sat on the pew where she'd left it with the print of her thumb on the cover.

She let the door close on its own soft seal.
