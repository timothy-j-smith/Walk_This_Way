CHAPTER 16: SENIOR YEAR — NICK'S WORLD

The L-S cafeteria smelled like fish sticks and canned tomato sauce and the plastic tang of trays still hot from the dishwasher. Friday menu. Above the steam tables a fluorescent ballast hummed at a frequency Nick's ear sorted, against his will, into a flat B-natural — the note it had hummed at every lunch for four years, the school's own pedal tone. He had his back to the cinder block wall and a tuna sandwich in wax paper Grace had made at five-forty that morning before the pickup left for Friendly's.

Stephen Murphy sat across from him with a chocolate milk. Mark Pianka drummed the edge of the formica with two fingers, unaware, the way he drummed everything. Three weeks since the Battle of the Bands poster had gone up, and the cafeteria had sorted itself without anyone moving a chair. Jackie Marsh's table was two rows over — the college-bound kids, the ones who said SAT the way other kids said Budweiser.

Jackie stood up.

The scrape of his chair on linoleum went through the room like a struck cymbal. A hush traveled outward in rings — first the kids who had been facing him, then the kids alongside, then the back benches where the Ramper girls sat, one of them mid-sentence with her hand lifted. Her hand stayed lifted. Stephen Murphy set his chocolate milk down very carefully, the way you set down a glass you've just learned was full of something breakable.

"'Somebody to Love,'" Jackie said.

He sang.

In the music room his voice had a ceiling. Here it didn't. The cinder block threw everything back at harder angles — a slap rather than a bloom — and Jackie rode the slap, Freddie Mercury through a kid trained since he was eight, operatic at the top, and here and there the trained edge frayed because he was seventeen and because you cannot really sing Queen at lunch without the frayed edge showing. His two bandmates fumbled into the harmonies a half-beat late. The fish-stick steam rose past him in the light. A lunch lady came to the kitchen doorway holding a pair of tongs and did not move again.

When Jackie hit the last high one he had to push, but he got there, and the note held against the block walls and came back at him from the other side. The clap that followed was scattered, dazed, not yet applause.

Nick set down his sandwich and stood up.

"'Dream On,'" he said.

No guitar. Nothing in his hands. His throat was dry. He swallowed and his ear found the note from the hum in the ballast above him and he stepped onto it like a man stepping onto a porch. Stephen Murphy came in under him, third below, rough. Mark Pianka flattened his hands on the formica and tapped the opening figure of the piano part, right hand arpeggio against the tabletop, fingers finding keys that were not there.

Nick sang. His voice was not trained. It went where he had dragged it — out past his father's funeral and back, past a toolbox he hadn't yet opened, past a hundred nights in a garage learning what his fingers could be made to do. On the long word — *dream* — the cafeteria held its breath. He went up for the top of it — *dream until your dreams come true* — and he pushed, and he got there, and the high note was thinner than Jackie's and rawer than Jackie's and nobody in that cafeteria mistook it for anything but what it was.

The silence after was not scattered. It was one silence.

Then the noise, all at once, the way a wave hits.

"Sit down." Mr. Wentworth stood at the end of the table, hands in the pockets of his corduroy jacket, the music teacher's face set in the expression of a man trying not to grin. "Both of you. Principal's office. Now."

---

Brad Sargent had been principal at L-S for twenty years and vice principal for eight before that. His office had a gray metal desk and a carpet the color of wet oatmeal that smelled like thirty years of industrial solvent laid over thirty years of whatever the solvent was laid over. On the desk sat a framed photograph of two kids in a canoe, a pen cup of Bic Clics, and a white ceramic coffee mug that read KEEFE TECH COUGARS — BASEBALL '63 in green letters faded at the rim where his thumb had gone a thousand times.

Nick sat in one chair. Jackie sat in the other. The chairs were molded plastic and exactly the same height.

"I have reports," Sargent said. He looked at a manila folder on his desk and did not open it. "Kids skipping class to rehearse. An argument in C-wing last Thursday. Mrs. Fiore says she can't get through homeroom announcements because of you." He tipped the mug an inch, set it back. "And now I hear singing in the cafeteria."

"We were —" Jackie began.

"I don't want to know what you were." Sargent leaned back. The chair creaked. A fluorescent tube in the ceiling ticked like a cheap watch. "I got a fire marshal who signed off on the auditorium for May 15th. I got WBCN calling my office. I got a superintendent who wants to know why his senior class is using the cafeteria as a rehearsal hall." He looked at Nick. He looked at Jackie. "You want me to cancel it?"

"No, sir," they said. Same word. Same moment.

Sargent almost smiled.

"Then off school property. You want to sing at each other, do it somewhere my secretary can't hear it. You have until May 15th. After that I don't care what you do." He reached for the mug. "Get out of my office."

---

In the hallway the lockers were pale green and their sneakers did not quite squeak on the waxed floor. They walked side by side because the hallway was narrow. Jackie's shoulder was an inch from Nick's shoulder and did not touch it.

"It's on," Nick said.

"Set list coming?"

"Yeah. Yours?"

"Yeah."

They reached the doors. Jackie pushed left. Nick pushed right.

Nick drove the wood-paneled wagon home down Route 20 past the Store 24 where Dennis was working the four-to-midnight, and lifted two fingers off the steering wheel as he passed the window. The sun was low over the trees. The fields behind the Cavicchio place were still brown. He turned up Dutton Road. The Johnson house sat where it always sat, the white paint going gray on the north side, the bay window in the dining room catching the last of the light.

---

The garage door stuck the way it always stuck. Nick leaned his shoulder into it.

Seventeen months. Dan had been dead seventeen months and the garage still smelled like sawdust and motor oil, the smell soaked so deep into the pegboard wood and the raw joists overhead that nothing short of a fire was going to lift it out.

The Aerosmith calendar from 1975 hung above the workbench where Dan had hung it when it was new. It was still turned to April — *Toys in the Attic* era, Joe Perry in a white scarf, Tyler leaning into a mic — the month Dan had turned it to four years ago and then left, because by May he'd bought a new one, and the May after that he'd stopped buying them, and the May after that he was gone. A 1975 April looking at a 1979 April through four years of furnace dust.

On the lid of a Maxwell House can sat a handful of loose hardware — nuts, washers, a bent finishing nail — and Dan's 9/16ths socket wrench, the one he'd carried in his back pocket at work every day for nineteen years. Nick had put it there himself the week after the funeral, because it was the only place he could think to put it.

The toolbox sat under the calendar.

Red. Scarred. Paint chipped at the corners. Latch shut. He had opened it once, the week of the funeral, and seen what was inside — the sheet music, the spiral notebook, the thing his father had never said out loud — and Grace had come in from the kitchen and closed the lid without speaking, and in seventeen months neither of them had touched it since. He had heard the music through the floor for half his life and he had never said he heard it, and Dan had never said he played it, and the closed red box held the proof of both silences. He did not reach for the latch. Not tonight.

He sat down on the milk crate and picked up the guitar.

"You eat?"

Grace was in the doorway. Still in her Friendly's uniform, the brown polyester that never let fryer oil all the way out of it, the little plastic name tag pinned crooked over her left breast — GRACE, and under it MANAGER, and a small gold foil star the company had added when they made it official in February. Her hands were red from dishwater up to the wrists.

"I had the tuna."

"That was lunch, Nick."

"I'll eat."

She came in. She did not sit. She leaned against the workbench, an inch from the toolbox, and her eyes went to it and then away — a glance so short he would not have caught it if he had not been watching for it all his life.

"Your father would've liked this," she said. "You going up against that Marsh kid."

"Yeah."

Her hand flattened on the workbench where Dan used to sit to unlace his boots at the end of a day. She looked past Nick, past the open garage door, past the driveway, toward the house — toward the bay window in the dining room, the one with the crown molding above it that nobody had painted over because nobody could reach. Her mouth opened on something and then closed on it. Whatever it was, it was older than him.

"He always said you had it," she said instead. "Not just the playing. The other thing."

"I know."

"Well." She pushed off the bench. "Come in by nine."

Her footsteps crossed the driveway and went up the kitchen steps and the screen door gave its two-note complaint.

Nick looked at the guitar in his lap. He played the opening of "Sweet Emotion." Thumb muting the low E, the slide up into the E-flat on the fourth string that Dan had shown him the summer he was fourteen, the week he'd blistered both thumbs and kept going. His fingers went without being asked.

The garage rang back. Concrete floor, bare joists, pegboard walls, no carpet anywhere — every note bounced off something hard and came back a half-breath later, doubled. The 9/16ths socket sat on the coffee-can lid. The red toolbox sat shut. The 1975 April looked down at the 1979 April from above the bench, and Joe Perry, caught forever in a white scarf, did not know yet what was going to happen to any of them.

Nick played it again. And again. The strings bit his fingertips. Outside, someone three houses down called a dog in for the night, and a truck on Route 20 downshifted for the light at Horse Pond Road, and the April dusk settled over Dutton Road the way it settled over every road in town — without asking, without promising anything.

He played until his left hand ached, and then he played another hour after that.
