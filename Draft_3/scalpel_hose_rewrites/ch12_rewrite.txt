CHAPTER 12: NOVEMBER 14

Grace was up at five-thirty. She filled the coffee pot, set it on the burner, and stood at the counter in the kitchen light while the house was still dark around her. November dark. The kind that pressed against the windows like it wanted in.

She made Dan's lunch the way she made it every morning. Tuna on white bread, the can opened and drained and mixed with mayonnaise and a little celery salt, the way her mother had made it for her father in this same kitchen when this had been her parents' house and the world had been a different shape. She spread the tuna with a butter knife, her hands moving without thought — hands that were forty-one years old and looked fifty, the knuckles swollen from hot water and fryer grease, the skin across the backs cracked in fine white lines like the glaze on old china. She wrapped the sandwich in wax paper, folded the edges tight, wrapped it again in aluminum foil. An apple. Two oatmeal cookies from Sunday night. Brown paper bag. Thermos.

Dan came down at six-fifteen. Work boots, jeans, flannel shirt. He poured coffee, drank half of it standing at the counter, set the mug in the sink.

"Let him sleep," he said, meaning Nick, who was upstairs in a seventeen-year-old's coma after staying up past midnight working through a chord progression in the garage. "He's got calculus first period."

Grace nodded. She was wiping the counter where the tuna can had left a ring of oil.

Dan picked up the lunch bag and the thermos. He kissed her. His mouth tasted like coffee and his chin was rough because he never shaved before work, said it wasn't worth the blade. He smelled like sawdust. He always smelled like sawdust, even fresh from the shower — the smell had gotten into his skin years ago, part of him now, the way the fryer oil was part of her.

"Home by six," he said.

"Drive safe," she said.

He walked down the driveway. The truck's headlights came on. The engine turned over, caught, settled. Red taillights moved down Dutton Road and disappeared where the road bent toward Route 20.

She rinsed the tuna can and put it in the trash. She poured herself coffee and sat at the table and drank it while the sky went from black to gray. A Monday. Ordinary. That was the thing about it that would break her in half later — how ordinary it was, how much it resembled every other morning, how there was nothing in it to grab onto and say here, here is where I should have known.

---

The Prudential Tower project rose thirty-seven stories above Back Bay. Dan had been on it since September, working steel. Nineteen years in the ironworkers' union, never a lost-time injury, never a citation. He kept a 9/16ths socket wrench in his back pocket like other men kept a wallet.

At 10:17 AM, a crane cable snapped on the thirty-first floor.

The cable had been scheduled for replacement two weeks earlier. The replacement had been delayed. Cost savings. Somebody in an office somewhere had looked at a number on a spreadsheet and decided the cable could hold a little longer, the way somebody always does, the way the math always works until it doesn't.

Four steel I-beams dropped.

Dan was in the construction trailer below, checking blueprints. The tuna sandwich Grace had wrapped that morning was half-eaten on the desk beside him, the wax paper peeled back, the aluminum foil balled up next to his thermos.

The beams came through the roof of the trailer like it was made of paper.

---

The call came to Friendly's at 11:47 AM.

Grace was working the lunch rush. Tom Andrews, the manager, came out of the back office and stood at the edge of the dining room with a look on his face that made her hands stop moving.

She followed him to the office. The phone receiver was lying on the desk. She picked it up. Her hands — the same hands that had wrapped the sandwich six hours ago, that had spread tuna with a butter knife, that had folded wax paper with the unconscious precision of ten thousand mornings — went white around the plastic.

A man's voice. Frank Morrison, Boston Construction. An accident. The Prudential site. Your husband. I'm sorry.

Grace sat down in Tom's chair.

Tom drove her to the hospital. She sat in the passenger seat with her hands in her lap, still wearing her Friendly's uniform, the name tag pinned crooked over her left breast. She didn't cry. She looked out the window at Route 9 and the strip malls and the trees that were bare now, November-bare, every branch visible against the white sky like cracks in something.

---

At Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School, Nick was in calculus.

Mrs. Delano was at the board, chalk tapping in a rhythm that had nothing to do with music but that Nick's brain sorted into music anyway because that was what his brain did. The radiator clanked. A pencil rolled off a desk and hit the floor with a sound like a rim shot on a snare.

The classroom door opened.

Mrs. Patterson stood in the doorway. Low-heeled shoes the color of oatmeal. Gray cardigan buttoned to the neck. The look on her face that adults get when they've been told something terrible and are trying to carry it across a room without dropping it.

"Nick Johnson? Could you come with me, please?"

The hallway was empty and bright. Fluorescent tubes buzzing at their sixty-cycle frequency. The linoleum reflecting the light in a long pale stripe that ran the length of the corridor. Mrs. Patterson's oatmeal shoes clicked on the floor. Nick's sneakers made no sound. He followed her past the trophy case, past the gym doors, past the water fountain with its permanent puddle, and none of it seemed real — all of it too sharp, too detailed, the way things get when your body knows something your mind hasn't caught up to yet.

Grace was sitting on the brown couch in the principal's office. Still in her Friendly's uniform. Her face was a color Nick had never seen on a living person.

"Mom?"

"Sit down, Nick."

He sat.

"There was an accident at Daddy's job. A cable broke. Some beams fell." She was looking at a point somewhere past his left shoulder, somewhere behind the wall, outside the building, outside the town. "He's gone, Nick."

The words entered his ears and traveled to his brain and his brain converted sound to meaning, and the meaning was clear and simple and it broke something in his chest that he would spend the rest of his life trying to fix.

He didn't cry. Not then. He sat on the brown couch and looked at his mother's hands in her lap — the cracked knuckles, the fryer burns, the wedding ring that was now something else — and he felt the world tilt and resettle into a new configuration where Dan Johnson was not in it.

---

Closed casket. The doctor used the word trauma the way you'd use a term in a manual. Grace signed papers. Date of birth. Social security number. Next of kin.

Nick sat in a plastic chair in a hallway that smelled like floor cleaner and something under the floor cleaner that floor cleaner exists to cover up. He counted the ceiling tiles. Sixty-four. He counted them again. Sixty-four. This small consistency felt like the only thing in the world he could trust.

The construction company would pay fifty thousand dollars, the minimum required by law. The insurance company would fight the claim for eight months.

Tom Andrews drove them home. Grace went into the bedroom and closed the door. Nick stood in the kitchen where six hours ago his father had been alive, drinking coffee, saying let him sleep. The coffee mug was still in the sink. Nick picked it up. It was cold. He washed it and put it on the drying rack and stood there with the water running over his hands until the water went cold too.

---

The funeral was Saturday. St. Mary's. Closed casket, dark wood, brass handles. Union men from the construction site sat in the back pews in their one good suit, hands in their laps, faces set in the particular expression of working men who understand that the next body could be theirs.

Grace received every handshake with a stillness that people would later describe as strength but that was really just the absence of any other option. Nick stood beside her in Dan's old sport coat, the shoulders too wide, the sleeves past his wrists, and he shook every hand that was offered and held himself together the way a cracked glass holds water — carefully, and only for so long.

Jackie came with his parents. He stood at the back of the church after the service, and when Nick finally saw him, Jackie didn't say anything. He just stood there, and his face held the thing he couldn't say, and Nick nodded once, and that was it. Two boys standing six feet apart in a church that smelled like candle wax and chrysanthemums, and the distance between them already beginning to mean something different than it had a week ago.

---

Three days after the funeral, Nick went into the garage.

The door stuck the way it always stuck, the wood swollen with November damp, and he had to lean his shoulder into it the way Dan used to, and the motion of it — his body repeating his father's body — sent something through him that he didn't have a name for.

The garage smelled like sawdust. It would always smell like sawdust. Twenty years of Dan in here, the fine pale dust settled into every crack and joint of the concrete floor until the smell became the room itself. The tools hung on the pegboard where Dan had placed them, each one outlined in black marker so you'd know where it went — the system of a man who believed that order was a form of respect.

Nick's guitar sat in its case on the milk crate in the corner. He didn't touch it.

He looked at the workbench. Dan's red metal toolbox sat where it always sat, heavy and scarred, the paint chipped at the corners. Nick had never opened it. You didn't open another man's toolbox the same way you didn't read another man's mail.

He opened it.

The top tray held what you'd expect. Wrenches, screwdrivers, tape measure, the 9/16ths socket wrench. Nick lifted the tray and set it on the workbench.

Underneath, in the bottom of the box, he found what he did not expect.

Three guitar picks. Fender mediums, tortoiseshell, worn smooth at the edges from use. A stack of sheet music — not store-bought but handwritten on staff paper in Dan's careful block print, the notes drawn with the same precision he used on blueprints. "Stairway to Heaven." "Wish You Were Here." "Landslide." Chords marked in pencil, fingering notes in the margins — barre chord here, capo third fret, hammer-on to the suspended fourth.

Under the sheet music, a spiral notebook. The cover was soft with handling, the wire binding bent from being shoved into the toolbox a hundred times. Nick opened it.

Pages of chord progressions in Dan's handwriting. Not copied from anywhere. Original. Sequences that moved from major to minor and back in ways that surprised Nick's ear even reading them off the page — he could hear them, could feel where they wanted to go, and they were good. They were genuinely good. A page of lyrics, crossed out and rewritten and crossed out again:

    Sawdust hands and a borrowed name
    The house remembers what I became

Nick sat on the concrete floor with the notebook in his lap and the picks in his palm.

Dan had been a musician.

Not in public. Not in the garage when Nick was practicing, not anywhere his family could see. In the hours after Grace went to bed, in the early mornings before anyone was up, Dan had sat in this garage and played guitar and written songs and never told anyone. He'd hidden it the way some men hide drinking — not out of shame but out of the belief that the thing you love most is the thing you can least afford to need. He'd had a dream and he'd put it in a toolbox and closed the lid and gone to work.

The fingering was right. The progressions were sophisticated — not noodling but the work of someone who understood theory, who had studied, who had put in years of quiet practice that nobody knew about.

All those nights Nick had been in the garage learning guitar, Dan had been inside the house, listening through the wall, knowing everything Nick was discovering because he'd discovered it himself, alone, in secret, and chosen not to say. He'd taught Nick. He'd bought the guitar, built the practice space, shown him the basics. But he'd never said I play too. He'd never sat down beside his son and played.

Nick put the picks in his jacket pocket where they clicked together like small bones.

When Grace found him an hour later, he was holding the notebook open to the chord progressions. She looked at it and her face did something complicated — a recognition that moved through grief into something older.

"You knew," Nick said.

"He played after I went to sleep. I'd hear it through the floor." She leaned against the doorframe. "He was good, Nick. He was really good."

"Why didn't he—"

"Because we needed the money. Because he had a family. Because where he came from, music wasn't a job, it was a luxury, and luxuries were for other people." She wiped her eyes with the back of her hand. "He gave it to you instead. Everything he couldn't let himself have, he made sure you had it."

Grace reached over and closed the toolbox lid. The latch clicked shut with a sound that was quiet and final.

She couldn't open it again. The toolbox would sit on the workbench for years, and Grace would clean around it and never touch it, because inside it was the version of Dan she hadn't been able to save.

Nick would open it again. Many times. He would take out the notebook and study his father's chord progressions and learn them by heart, and when he played them on his own guitar the notes would sound like inheritance — not money, not property, not the things that pass from father to son in families that have things to pass. Something that couldn't be delayed for cost savings or dropped from thirty-one stories or sealed in a closed casket.

Music. The one thing Dan Johnson had that was entirely his own. And now it was Nick's.
