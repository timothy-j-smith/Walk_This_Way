CHAPTER 22: JACKIE'S REHEARSAL

May 14, 1979. Early evening. Eighteen hours to the Battle and Jackie Marsh was alone in the basement on Maple Street, running the intro of "Bohemian Rhapsody" in B-flat because his left hand had asked for it the second he sat down.

The room had been finished the summer he turned fourteen. Foam panels on the walls, the two-inch studio kind — white once, now going yellow at the glued edges the way a paperback goes yellow on a south-facing shelf. A Shure SM58 stood on a boom stand at the center of the floor, black grille, the XLR running in a clean loop to the four-track against the wall — a Tascam, four little red LED indicators glowing dim as pilot lights in a kitchen at night. Against the long wall, the upright. A 1920s Baldwin, its walnut case gone plum-dark at the corners, its ivory top keys chipped in two places from a thumbnail that had been Eileen's mother's in 1931 in a parlor on Beacon Hill. The Steinway lived upstairs in the good light. The Baldwin lived down here with the amps. That had been decided early.

Jackie played the opening six bars and let them stop themselves. His right thumb rested on the D. The ballast in the overhead fluorescent made its small even buzz and the foam drank it.

He had heard about Grace Johnson's speech at Friendly's. Everyone in Sudbury had heard about it by Wednesday. The knowledge sat in his chest the way a swallowed pill sits before it starts to go down.

---

They came in at six-thirty the way they always came in, Tuesday-Thursday-Saturday, the Crown Jewels coming down the side stairs in the order of their cars.

Ace Callahan first, because Ace always came first — the Premier kit was already set up down here, red-sparkle shells, the kick drum head with the black CROWN JEWELS decal he'd rubbed on with a pencil and a sheet of Letraset. He sat and ran a double-stroke roll on the snare without a word and the foam ate most of it. Max Wesslen came in behind him with the Fender Precision — black body, rosewood neck, the bass his father had bought him at E. U. Wurlitzer in the Back Bay for his sixteenth birthday — and plugged into the Ampeg cabinet and played four notes of the Queen bassline to warm the hand.

Elizabeth came last, because she lived upstairs and there was nothing to carry but the case. Thirteen years old. Violin under her arm in its student Gewa case. She sat on the arm of the chair Dr. Marsh sat in and opened the case on her lap and took out the chamois and wiped the rosin dust off the belly of the violin in small slow circles. On the left side of her neck, above the collar of her cardigan, a small pink callus had been forming for two months now where the chinrest lived. She was not aware of it, which was the point. She had cleared the bar so young that nobody had thought to put a bar there. She got grandfathered into freedom before she knew there was a cage.

"From the top," Jackie said.

They ran the Chopin étude first — Opus 10, No. 3, the one called "Tristesse," the one anyone's mother recognized — arranged for four pieces, Jackie's arrangement, the melody in the violin, the harmony voicings on the Baldwin, Max walking the root under Ace's brushes. Jackie had written out every chart in his own hand, the ink neat as a ledger. It came out of the four of them the way a good clock comes out of its case. Nothing missing.

Then "Somebody to Love" in A-flat major — the Mercury gospel piled up in stacked thirds, Elizabeth doubling the top line with the bow, Max and Ace holding the pocket. Then "Bohemian Rhapsody," the whole six-minute architecture, B-flat opening, the operatic middle stacked, the hard-rock vamp, the coda. The harmonies locked. Every chord where it went. The kind of rehearsal a band videotapes and sends to a label.

Dr. Marsh came down during the last verse.

He came down with the leather notebook under his left arm, the one he used for patients and lectures and his children. He did not sit right away. He walked across the carpet behind Jackie, behind Ace's kit, and stood at the Baldwin for a breath — and as the last chord rang Jackie, whose eyes were on the keyboard, did not see his father's right hand come out and settle, and did not see the thumb touch middle C. Not press. Touch. Half a beat. The key did not sound. The thumb came off. His father's hand closed, went to the spine of the notebook, steadied it. Then Dr. Marsh crossed to the chair by the wall and sat, and opened the notebook on his knee.

Jackie played the last resolving chord and lifted his hands.

"The Chopin was excellent," his father said. He was smiling the small careful smile. "Elizabeth, your intonation in the second phrase was exactly right."

Elizabeth said thank you and put the chamois back in its corner of the case.

Dr. Marsh stood. He walked to the Baldwin and rested his hand on Jackie's shoulder, the weight of it careful as a stethoscope.

"The Queen, Jackson. When you play it like that — controlled. Precise. Every note exactly where it belongs — " He paused. He was choosing his word. He found it. "You remind me of Glenn Gould. That kind of technical mastery. Mercury is a talent, but he is undisciplined. You've taken his chaos and made it civilized."

He squeezed the shoulder once. The notebook went back under his arm.

"Keep this up," he said, "and tomorrow will take care of itself."

The side stairs took him back up. The door at the top closed on its soft seal.

Civilized. Jackie sat with the word on him.

---

Ace packed first, because Ace always packed first, sticks into the stick bag, cymbals into the gig bag with a folded bath towel between them. Max coiled his cord over-under and slid the Precision into its case. Goodbyes at the side door. Feet going up the driveway in sneakers and a Chuck Taylor, a car door, a second car door, an engine, another engine. Quiet.

Elizabeth was still on the chair arm.

"You okay."

"Fine."

"Jack."

He looked at her. She had the chamois folded into its small square now and was closing the case in the way she closed books — not snapping the latch, pressing it shut.

"You know he stopped asking me to practice last year," she said. "I'd already done the job. He lets me alone." She looked at the Baldwin, not at him. "But you keep trying to make him hear something. And he doesn't hear music, Jack. He counts notes."

Jackie did not answer.

"Go to bed," she said. "Tomorrow's long."

She took the violin upstairs. The door at the top closed again.

---

He sat on the bench another minute with the fluorescent buzzing and the four little red Tascam LEDs glowing at the wall and the foam doing its patient work on every sound.

Then he played "Bohemian Rhapsody" for himself.

Not the arrangement. The record. He started at the operatic middle section where the voices stack, and he sang the *Galileo, Galileo, Galileo figaro* in his head because you could not sing it in a Marsh basement, and his left hand made the big jumping accompaniment that wasn't in any of the charts, and his right hand did the Mercury flourishes the charts had taken out, and for three minutes he was not an early-admission to The Juilliard School, 144 West 66th Street, New York. The ballast buzzed. The foam drank. The Tascam's LEDs held their small steady red.

Then the song ended.

Classical earned his father's approval. Rock earned his breath. He couldn't have both.

---

He gently lowered the piano lid. The soft wooden click went across the foam and was absorbed before it finished. He killed the basement lights at the switch by the stairs.

He went up.

He passed the study door, open a hand's width — his father at the desk inside, textbooks fanned out in the green pool of the banker's lamp, a pencil in his hand, checking one book against another for tomorrow's lecture. He passed the living room. His mother in the wing chair under the floor lamp, a novel on her lap, her eyes on the page and not on the page. He passed Elizabeth's door, closed, the thin line of her bedside light under it.

His own door closed behind him with the quiet click of a door hung right.

On the desk, the Juilliard envelope lay where it had lain for three weeks, cream laid paper, the letter knife slit along the top. Next to it, face down on the stack of old Curtis programs where he had put it in March, the small silver frame held the photograph he could not look at tonight.

He did not turn the photograph over.

He did not open the envelope again.

He took off his shoes, set them at the foot of the bed, and got under the covers in his clothes.

**END CHAPTER 22**
