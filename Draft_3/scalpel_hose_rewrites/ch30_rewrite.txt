CHAPTER 30: THE ANNOUNCEMENT

The judges' room smelled like burned coffee and indecision.

It was the guidance-office conference room off the main hall, requisitioned for the night — four metal folding chairs around a formica table, the fluorescent tube overhead hitting an uneven ballast, a low hum riding under the voices like a note somebody had left on. The coffee urn on the warmer by the door had been brewed at five and was now the color of wet tar. The cafeteria smell of sloppy joe leftovers came in every time somebody cracked the door. Nobody had cracked the door in twenty minutes.

Maxanne Sartori sat with her back to the door, her fingers wrapped around a Styrofoam cup she hadn't drunk from in twenty minutes. Across from her, Carter Alan leaned on his elbows, jaw working. Chuck Morrison had his legal pad covered in notes he kept crossing out, the yellow legal yellow against the gray formica. Bob Walker from Strawberries stood at the window watching the parking lot fill with headlights as families arrived for the announcement.

"We've been at this forty minutes," Carter said.

"I'm aware." Maxanne set the cup down. A ring bloomed on the table beneath it.

"Walking the Line played the tighter set," Chuck said. He tapped his pen against the pad. "Johnson's guitar work on *Dream On* — that wasn't a cover. That was a conversation with the original. Kid played like he had something to prove and nothing to lose."

"Crown Jewels had the superior arrangement," Bob said from the window, not turning. "The Marsh kid's piano on *Somebody to Love* — I've heard session players with less control. And the sister on violin. That wasn't rock. That was architecture."

Carter looked at Maxanne. They were all looking at Maxanne.

She'd been quiet through most of it. Listening. Running the same calculation she'd been running since both bands walked off — a calculation she already knew the answer to because she'd written the equation herself, two and a half years earlier, in a clause buried on page four of a contract no seventeen-year-old would read twice.

"How do you choose between authenticity and excellence?" she said.

The room went still.

Chuck stopped tapping. Bob turned from the window. Carter's jaw went slack for half a second before he caught it.

"Maybe you don't," Maxanne said.

She pulled the contract from her bag. Four pages, photocopied on the Sudbury High office machine, the toner already fading at the margins. She opened to page four and laid it flat, her index finger landing on Section 7, Subsection C.

"'In the event of a tied decision among the judging panel, prize money shall be divided equally between tied parties. Studio recording time, as specified in Section 3, shall be designated as shared time, to be used collaboratively or forfeited in full.'"

Carter read it twice. "You wrote this."

"I wrote the whole contract."

"When?"

"Fall of '78. I was at Elektra by then. The contest was a consulting deal back to WBCN."

"Before you'd heard either band play."

Maxanne picked up her cup and drank the cold coffee. It tasted like pennies. "I heard them both play in '75. Curtis Middle School talent show. They did a mashup — Aerosmith and Queen. Two twelve-year-olds who had no business sounding that good together. I wrote the tie clause because I thought there was a chance — a small one — that they'd grow up and both be too good to choose between."

"That's not judging," Chuck said. "That's engineering."

"Yes," Maxanne said. "It is."

Bob Walker sat down. The chair squeaked against the linoleum. "So we're calling it a tie."

"I'm not calling anything. I'm asking you three a question. Can you honestly pick one?"

Silence filled the room the way water fills a glass — completely, with nowhere for air to go.

Carter shook his head first. Then Chuck. Bob looked at his hands and said, "No."

"Then it's a tie."

---

The auditorium held four hundred and forty-three people and sounded like it held twice that. Nick Johnson stood stage left with Stephen Murphy, David Cassel, and Mark Pianka behind him. The Telecaster hung from his shoulder, the strap digging into a sunburn he'd gotten helping his mother reshingle the garage roof on Saturday. Jackie Marsh stood stage right, Elizabeth beside him with her violin case at her thigh. Ace and Max Wesslen flanked them.

Between the two bands: twelve feet of stage boards taped in black gaffer at four-foot intervals, and a single Shure SM58 on its chrome stand, the cable coiled at the base.

Maxanne walked to the microphone. The house noise dropped to a murmur, then to breathing.

"This has been one of the finest competitions I've witnessed in my career." The FM warmth that had introduced Aerosmith to Boston in '73 came out of her like she'd never left the booth. "Both bands performed at a level that would be remarkable for professionals. For high school musicians, it borders on the extraordinary."

She paused. Let the silence build. She knew how to use dead air the way a drummer knew how to use the space between beats.

"The judges' decision is unanimous. The 1979 WBCN Battle of the Bands ends in a tie."

The auditorium inhaled.

Then the noise came — not cheering, not booing, but the confused electric sound of four hundred people trying to process something that wasn't in the script. Murmuring swelled, broke into scattered applause, died back to murmuring. A whistle from the back, high and piercing.

"Prize money of five hundred dollars will be split equally. Two hundred and fifty dollars to Walking the Line. Two hundred and fifty dollars to Crown Jewels."

She held up the contract. The paper caught the par-can light and glowed.

"One hundred hours of recording time at WBCN Studio C on Boylston Street has been awarded as shared time. Per the contract, that time must be used collaboratively by both bands — or it is forfeited entirely."

Now the murmuring stopped. Now the auditorium understood.

Nick's head turned toward Jackie. Jackie's head turned toward Nick. Twelve feet of stage boards between them. The microphone stand at knee height. The paper Maxanne was holding up thin enough that the light came through the margins.

Maxanne stepped back. "Gentlemen. Come forward and sign the award acknowledgment."

They walked from opposite directions and met at the microphone like two tides converging. Maxanne held the contract between them. Nick took the left edge. Jackie took the right. They stood shoulder to shoulder holding the same piece of paper, and neither one looked at the other.

Nick stared at the contract. The words swam. Two hundred and fifty dollars. His mother could use it. His mother could always use it. The studio — a hundred hours in a professional room on Boylston — came with a chain: shared, collaborative, with Jackie Marsh, who had not spoken to him since the funeral.

Jackie stared at the contract. His father would tell him to forfeit. Walk away clean. Take the money and the moral victory and pack for Juilliard.

They signed. Separate pens. Same line.

---

In the third row on the left, Grace Johnson stood.

She didn't applaud. She didn't shout. She stood the way she'd stood at Dan's funeral — straight-backed, dry-eyed, holding herself together with the same iron discipline that got her through double shifts and mortgage payments and PTA meetings. She'd left Friendly's at four and changed at home: the navy dress with the buttons, the dress she'd worn to Dan's funeral, the dress she'd worn to Curtis Middle School in '75 to watch her twelve-year-old play Aerosmith with the Marsh boy on piano. She would not wear it again after tonight. Her winter coat was folded over the seat behind her because she had nothing else to carry.

She watched her son on the stage holding a contract with the Marsh boy, and she thought about Dan. Dan who would have been in the front row. Dan who would have been shouting loud enough to embarrass Nick. Dan who would have grabbed both boys in a bear hug and said *that's how you do it* and meant every syllable.

Two hundred and fifty dollars.

*Take it*, Dan said in her head, as clear as if he were sitting one seat to her right. *We need it.*

Grace sat down. She folded her hands in her lap and waited.

---

Three rows back and across the aisle, Dr. Richard Marsh unclenched his hands.

He had not realized he had been gripping the armrests. The wooden fronts had left red grooves across his palms, two parallel tracks in each hand. He stared at the marks a moment, and then he looked up at his son.

Jackie stood in the stage lights with someone else's contract in his hand, and for one flashing unbearable second Dr. Marsh saw himself at twenty-two. A piano lid he was about to close. The rational choice. The correct choice.

The pinky of his left hand uncurled once, on its own, an octave-reach his thumb had not been asked to make since he was twenty-two.

He closed his hands again. Gently this time. The marks would fade.

---

In the back row, Patty Lally sat alone.

She hadn't won. She hadn't officially placed. Her name wasn't on the contract, wouldn't appear in the Sudbury Town Crier's write-up, wouldn't be mentioned when people talked about the Battle at the diner tomorrow morning.

But she'd played *Go Your Own Way* barefoot on that stage with her eyes closed and her voice wide open, and for four minutes and thirty-eight seconds, the auditorium had belonged to her. To a girl from the Sudbury Southeast pig farms who sang Fleetwood Mac because Stevie Nicks understood what it meant to love something that was already leaving.

She hummed it now. Quiet. Just for herself. *Go your own way.*

People were filing out. The parking lot would be chaos — Suburbans and pickups and Volvos trying to exit through the same two lanes, families clumping on the sidewalk, talking in that animated way that meant everyone had an opinion and no one was sure what had happened.

Patty watched them go. She didn't move. She'd wait until the auditorium was empty, and then she would walk. Three miles on Route 20 past the dairy farm and the reservoir, humming the whole way.

---

Outside, the mid-May night was the temperature of a held breath. Sixty degrees. Damp. The maples along the high school drive were leafing out and the new leaves caught the sodium arc lamps in the parking lot and threw shadow patterns across the asphalt. From the Sudbury Dump a half-mile west, the spring peepers were at it — thin silver sound carrying on the low air the way only spring-peeper sound carries.

Nick and Jackie emerged from separate doors, the contract folded between them like a custody agreement for something that hadn't been born yet. Lights were on in kitchen windows down the hill. Television glow through curtains. The distant hiss of Route 20 traffic going east toward Wayland.

Somewhere in that town, two hundred and fifty dollars would pay an insurance bill. Somewhere else, two hundred and fifty dollars would sit in a savings account already thick with Juilliard money, barely noticeable.

A hundred hours of studio time hung in the air between them, unclaimed.

Grace Johnson came out the lobby door with her winter coat over her arm and walked to the wood-paneled '67 wagon in the second row of the lot. She opened the driver's door. The dome light came on briefly and then went off. The wagon started on the second turn of the key, the way it did in weather that wasn't cold, and she sat for a moment with her hands on the wheel before she put it in gear.

On Route 20 a quarter mile east, Patty Lally was already walking, barefoot boots in her left hand, the guitar case in her right, the hum in her throat.

**END CHAPTER 30**
