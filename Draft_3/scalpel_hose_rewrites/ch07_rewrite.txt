CHAPTER 7: NICK'S WORLD

The Johnson house on Dutton Road had crown molding in the dining room. Not the cheap pressed-foam kind you bought at the lumber yard — real plaster, hand-carved, with acanthus leaves that curled at the corners where the ceiling met the walls. Grace ran her fingers along the baseboard every time she vacuumed, the way you'd touch a scar without meaning to. Most of the molding was painted over now, three or four coats of landlord white, but if you looked where the living room met the front hall you could still see the original detail underneath — vines and small flowers cut into plaster by someone who'd been paid to make a house beautiful, back when this house was the kind of place where people got paid to do that.

Nick didn't know any of this yet. He was thirteen, and the house was just the house.

---

March, 1976. Nick came through the back door at four-fifteen with a brown paper bag from Store 24 and mud on both knees. The bag held a set of guitar strings — Martin Marquis, medium gauge, $3.49 — and a Slim Jim he'd already eaten in the parking lot.

Grace was at the kitchen table, still in her Friendly's uniform, the brown polyester one with the orange piping that made her look like a UPS driver. Her name tag was crooked. She had the bills spread in front of her — electric, mortgage, the Sears card — arranged in a row like a hand of cards she hadn't decided how to play.

"Boots," she said without looking up.

Nick kicked them off at the door. "Hey, Mom."

"How was school?"

"Fine."

"Fine like you went to all your classes, or fine like you went to some of your classes?"

"All of them." He held up the bag. "Got new strings."

Grace glanced at the bag, then at his knees. "You walked to Store 24 in the mud."

"The shortcut's faster."

"The shortcut goes through the Hendersons' yard."

"Mrs. Henderson doesn't care."

"Mrs. Henderson called me last week, Nick." Grace set down her pen. Her eyes were the color of strong tea and they didn't miss much. "Your father's working late. There's leftover chicken in the fridge. Do your homework before you touch that guitar."

"Yes ma'am."

He didn't do his homework first. Grace knew he wouldn't. She listened to him cross the kitchen in his socks, heard the side door open and close, heard his feet on the two concrete steps down to the garage. She picked up her pen and went back to the column of numbers that never came out right.

---

The garage smelled like sawdust and motor oil and the cold mineral breath of a concrete floor that never fully dried. Dan Johnson's workbench ran the length of the back wall — a slab of three-quarter-inch plywood on two-by-four legs, scarred with saw cuts and drill holes and half-moon burns from coffee mugs set down hot. His tools hung on pegboard above it, arranged by type: wrenches in descending order, screwdrivers slotted into a rack he'd built from a piece of oak scrap, the socket set open in its case like a jaw full of silver teeth. Everything had a place. Everything was where it belonged.

Nick's corner was the opposite. A milk crate for a chair. His guitar — a twenty-five-dollar pawn shop Stratocaster copy held together with electrical tape at the strap button — leaned against the wall next to a shoebox full of picks and a dog-eared copy of a Mel Bay chord book he'd gotten from the Sudbury library and never returned.

He sat on the crate and changed the strings. The old ones came off stiff, the wound wire unraveling where his pick hand had worn through the nickel plating. He threaded the new ones through the bridge, wound them at the tuning pegs, stretched each string by pulling it away from the neck and retuning until it held. The fresh strings were bright under the single overhead bulb — clean steel, tight and sharp against the rosewood fretboard.

He played for an hour. The same riff, over and over. The opening to "Walk This Way" — that nasty, syncopated thing Joe Perry played, all muted scratches and pull-offs, the notes tumbling over each other like they were in a hurry to get somewhere bad. Nick could hear it perfectly in his head. His fingers were slower than the song. He'd get through the first four bars clean and then his ring finger would land a fret too high and the whole thing collapsed into a buzz.

He played it again. And again.

---

Dan's truck pulled into the driveway at six-forty. Nick heard it before he saw it — the particular rattle of a 1968 Ford F-100 with a loose heat shield and 140,000 miles of opinion about how it should be treated. The engine cut. The truck door opened and closed. Work boots on gravel.

Dan came through the garage door sideways, turning his shoulders to clear the frame. He was still in his work clothes — Carhartt jacket with drywall dust on the shoulders, jeans that had gone soft as chamois from washing, steel-toed Red Wings caked at the soles. His hands were enormous. The calluses on his left fingertips were thick as dimes, and not just from construction — they wrapped around the pads of his fingers in a way that only came from pressing steel strings against a fretboard, though Nick had never seen his father hold a guitar.

"Hey, bud." Dan set his lunch pail on the workbench. "That 'Walk This Way'?"

"Trying to be."

Dan pulled off his jacket and hung it on the nail by the door. He stood behind Nick and watched his hands. "You're rushing the second bar. Slow down."

"It sounds wrong slow."

"Sounds worse fast if the notes aren't clean." Dan reached past him and pressed Nick's ring finger down flat against the fret wire. His hand was warm and rough and smelled like joint compound. "Here. Not behind the fret — right on top of it. Feel that?"

Nick pressed. The note rang out clean, no buzz.

"Now the pull-off. Don't lift straight up. Pull down toward the floor. You're snapping the string, not just releasing it."

Nick tried. Better. The note had a voice now instead of just a click.

"Again," Dan said.

Nick played the first two bars. Dan's hand stayed near the neck, not touching, just hovering — ready to correct. When Nick hit the transition to the third bar, Dan tapped the body of the guitar twice with his knuckle, setting the tempo the way a drummer would.

"There," Dan said. "Hear that? You had it."

Nick played it again. This time the riff came out whole — rough, a little ragged at the edges, but the bones were right. The notes fell where they were supposed to fall.

Dan squeezed his shoulder once, hard enough to mean something. "Good. Now play it fifty more times."

He picked up his lunch pail and went inside. Through the screen door Nick heard him say "Smells good" to Grace, heard the faucet running, heard the quiet domesticity of a house settling into evening. He played the riff again. And again. The fresh strings bit into his fingertips, not yet calloused enough, and by the twentieth repetition the pain had become a kind of company — proof he was working, proof the music was costing him something, which meant it was worth something.

---

Dinner was chicken and rice and canned green beans. Grace served it on plates that didn't match — two blue, one white with a chip on the rim that she always took for herself. The kitchen was small, the table pushed against the wall so you had to slide in sideways to reach the far chair. Dan sat with his back to the window. Grace sat closest to the stove. Nick sat between them, still flexing his left hand under the table, the fingertips dented and sore.

"He's getting better," Dan said to Grace, forking rice.

"I can hear." Grace looked at Nick. "You do your homework?"

"Most of it."

"Nick."

"I'll finish after dinner."

Grace held his eyes for a beat, then let it go. She turned to Dan. "The banister's loose again. The one at the top of the stairs."

"I'll look at it Saturday."

"It's been loose since November."

"Then another week won't kill it." Dan said this with half a smile, and Grace shook her head but didn't push. There was a language between them built from years of these small negotiations — what got fixed, what waited, what they pretended not to notice because noticing cost money they didn't have.

Grace stood to clear the plates. As she passed the doorway to the dining room, she stopped. Her hand went to the frame, her thumb finding the groove where the original molding met the casing — a seam you'd only notice if you knew to look for it, if you'd traced it a thousand times as a child, running your fingers along it on your way to a dining room that once held a mahogany table and eight matching chairs and a sideboard with crystal decanters that caught the afternoon light.

"What?" Dan said.

"Nothing." Grace pulled her hand away. "The paint's peeling."

She carried the plates to the sink and ran the water hot.

---

Nick finished his homework at nine — or close enough to call it finished. He brushed his teeth, said goodnight to his parents, and lay in bed with the lights off, listening.

Grace's footsteps in the hallway. Her bedroom door closing. The click of the lamp on her nightstand, and then quiet.

He waited.

Twenty minutes. Maybe thirty. Long enough for the house to decide everyone was asleep.

Then the sound came through the wall. Not from the house — from below it, from the garage, muffled by drywall and floor joists and the particular insulation of a secret kept so long it had become architecture. Guitar. Acoustic, played soft, the notes dampened like someone was cupping the sound with their palm. Nick couldn't make out the song — just the rhythm, steady and slow, a fingerpicking pattern that rose and fell like breathing. Sometimes the player paused, and Nick could almost hear the shift of fingers on the fretboard, the small squeak of callused skin on wound strings. Then the music would start again, patient and unhurried, a conversation between a man and an instrument that happened only when the house was dark.

Dan Johnson playing guitar alone in the garage after his wife went to bed. Nick had heard it for years — since he was nine or ten, since the first night he'd stayed up late enough and the house had gone quiet enough. He'd never mentioned it. He didn't know why. Some things you heard through walls were like prayers — not meant for you, not meant for anyone, and saying them out loud would break whatever made them true.

The music went on for a while. Nick lay still and listened, his sore fingertips pressed against the cool sheet, and the sound came up through the floor and the bedframe and into his chest like a second heartbeat. Outside, the March wind pushed against the windows. Inside, his father played a song that no one was supposed to hear, in a garage that smelled like sawdust and motor oil, in a house where the crown molding was buried under four coats of paint and a woman touched the doorframe every night like she was saying goodbye to a room that used to be hers.

Nick closed his eyes. The riff from "Walk This Way" moved through his fingers against the mattress — pull down, not up, right on the fret, feel that — and somewhere beneath him the guitar kept going, quiet and sure, the sound of a man who'd made his choices and lived with them and still, in the dark, when no one was listening, played.
