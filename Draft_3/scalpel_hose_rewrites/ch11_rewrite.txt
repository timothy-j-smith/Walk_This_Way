CHAPTER 11: THE FIRST FRACTURE

Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School. September 1976.

The cafeteria hit you before you saw it. Fryer grease and industrial tomato sauce and the sweet chemical fog of whatever they sprayed on the tables between periods — a smell that got into your clothes and stayed there, so your mother would sniff your jacket that evening and say you smelled like school, which was another way of saying you smelled like everyone and no one. The noise was a wall. Three hundred kids talking at once produced a sound that had no words in it, just a continuous roar like a river heard from a distance, punctuated by the crack of lunch trays hitting formica and the occasional shriek of a chair leg dragged across linoleum. The fluorescent lights buzzed at a frequency that lived behind your teeth.

Nick and Jackie walked in together that first Monday, carrying brown bags because the hot lunch line was forty minutes long and neither of them knew the system yet. They stood just inside the double doors, scanning the room the way you scan a lake before you swim — looking for the rocks under the surface, the places you can't see until you're already in.

The room had its own geography.

The Rampers owned the raised platform at the back — six tables on a section of floor eight inches higher than everything else, which was all it took. They sat with their textbooks stacked, spiral notebooks open, highlighters uncapped and organized by color. One girl had a day planner. It was September. She was already marking dates in November.

The Rats had the glass corridor, the breezeway between the cafeteria and the arts wing, where the afternoon light came through dirty windows and turned everything the color of weak tea. Someone was always rolling a cigarette. Someone was always sketching in a composition notebook. A boy with safety pins through his jacket collar was reading a paperback with no cover.

The Regs filled the middle — the big undifferentiated mass of tables where you sat if you didn't sit anywhere in particular. Loud, safe, forgettable. The center of the room and the center of the bell curve and they knew it and that was fine.

The Rollers had the two tables nearest the door to the auto shop corridor, and you could smell them before you saw them — motor oil and Brakleen and the metallic sweetness of fresh-cut steel. Their hands were stained in the creases the way Nick's father's hands were stained, the kind of clean that soap can get to and the kind it can't. They ate fast. Lunch was what happened between engines.

Nick and Jackie didn't know any of this yet. They just knew the room was enormous and loud and that they needed to sit down somewhere before the standing itself became a statement.

"There," Jackie said, and pointed with his chin toward a half-empty table in the middle section, and they sat, and for the first two weeks that was the arrangement. Nick and Jackie at a Regs table near the water fountain, eating lunch, talking about the band they were going to start, about Patty's voice, about whether they could book a practice space at the community center on Route 20. The cafeteria roared around them and they were an island in it, the same way the garage had been an island, the same way the practice meadow at Elbanobscot had been an island — a pocket of air where the two of them made sense together and the outside categories didn't apply.

It lasted fourteen days.

On the Monday of the third week, Nick walked into the cafeteria and Jackie wasn't at their table. Nick scanned the room — the Regs section, the corridor, the raised platform — and found him. Jackie was sitting up on the Rampers' section, at the second table from the wall, next to a kid in a polo shirt who was showing him something in a textbook. Jackie had his notebook open. He was laughing.

The distance between their old table and the platform was maybe forty feet. Nick could have crossed it in ten seconds. He stood holding his brown bag and looked at the forty feet and felt something shift in his stomach, a small cold drop, like the moment a roller coaster crests the first hill and you realize gravity has been waiting for you.

He didn't cross the forty feet. He turned and walked to the Rollers' tables near the shop corridor, where Carl Marquis was eating a sandwich with hands that had black grease worked into the knuckles. Carl looked up. Nick sat down. Carl went back to his sandwich. That was the whole welcome. No questions, no audition. You sat or you didn't.

"You got shop second period?" Carl asked after a while.

"Third."

"Harrington?"

"Yeah."

Carl nodded. The conversation was over and it was enough.

Nick ate his lunch. The sandwich tasted like nothing. Across the room, on the platform, Jackie was still talking to the kid in the polo shirt — David Bellenghi, though Nick didn't know the name yet. Jackie's back was angled slightly away from the main floor. Not fully turned. Just angled. The way you angle away from a window when the light's too bright, without deciding to, without even knowing you're doing it.

Jackie didn't look for Nick. That was the thing. He didn't scan the room. He didn't check the table by the water fountain. He was up there and he was fine up there and it hadn't occurred to him that Nick might be looking for him the way a dog looks for the other dog it walked in with.

Or maybe it had occurred to him and he'd chosen not to look. Nick couldn't tell, and in a way that was worse — the not knowing whether it was careless or careful. Both options left him sitting at a table that smelled like motor oil, eating a sandwich he couldn't taste, forty feet from his best friend in a room so loud that even if he'd shouted Jackie's name it would have dissolved before it got there.

Across the cafeteria, near the milk coolers, a girl sat alone at the end of a Regs table. Wheat-colored hair, canvas bag, bare feet tucked under the bench even though the linoleum was cold. Patty Lally ate an apple and watched the room. She saw Nick at the Rollers' table. She saw Jackie on the platform. She saw the forty feet between them, and she saw what it meant, because Patty had grown up at the margin of things — pig-farm girl in a subdivision town — and margins gave you sight lines the center didn't have.

She didn't say anything. She didn't wave. She bit her apple and watched, and something behind her eyes was doing math — not the kind with numbers, the kind where you count the distance between two people who don't know they're moving apart and calculate how long before they notice.

That night, in the garage on Dutton Road, Jackie showed up with his guitar at six-thirty as if nothing had changed. Maybe for him nothing had. He tuned up, ran through a chord progression, waited for Nick to come in on top of it the way Nick always did.

Nick played. The music worked the way it always worked — his raw line and Jackie's architecture, the engine and the wingspan. They ran through three songs and the garage filled with sound and the sound was right.

But in the gaps between songs, in the silences where they used to talk, something was different. The silence had a texture now. It was the silence of two people not mentioning something, which is louder than the silence of two people with nothing to say.

"Good session," Jackie said when they finished, and he said it the way you say good session when you're confirming that the session was good and nothing else, when you're closing a door on everything the session didn't include — like why you were sitting on a platform eight inches above your best friend at lunch today, or why you didn't look for him, or why you laughed so easily with a kid whose name he doesn't know.

"Yeah," Nick said. "Good session."

Jackie packed up his guitar and rode his bike home through the September dark, up Dutton Road toward the north side where the lawns got wider and the porch lights were the kind that turned on automatically at dusk. Nick stood in the garage doorway and watched the red reflector on Jackie's rear fender shrink until it was just a point of light and then nothing.

He went inside. His mother was at the kitchen table, paying bills with the calculator that printed on the little paper tape, the sound of it filling the room — click, whirr, click — like a mechanical heartbeat counting down something. His father's work boots stood by the door, the leather cracked, dried plaster in the treads.

Nick went to his room. He picked up his guitar and played nothing — just held it, the body warm against his ribs, and thought about forty feet of linoleum and the sound of three hundred people between him and the kid who used to sit next to him.

It wasn't a fight. It wasn't even a disagreement. It was a Monday in September, a cafeteria, a brown bag lunch, and the discovery that forty feet can open under your friendship like a crack in a sidewalk — so thin you step over it without looking down, so thin you could tell yourself it was always there, so thin that you won't understand it was the beginning of anything until years later, when the crack has become a canyon and you're standing on one side trying to remember when the ground was whole.

Jackie probably didn't even notice. That was the fracture. Not that it happened. That only one of them felt it.

Outside, September cooled toward October. The leaves on Dutton Road were just starting to turn, the green going pale at the edges, not yet the orange and red that would come but already not what they were — changing so slowly that if you watched them every day you'd swear nothing was different, and then one morning you'd look up and the whole street would be on fire and you'd wonder how you missed it.
