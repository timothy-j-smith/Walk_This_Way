CHAPTER 29: PATTY'S PERFORMANCE

Patty kicked her boots off stage-right and stepped onto the riser barefoot. The stage wood was warm where the lights had been cooking it for two hours. Nick's band had left a smell behind — sweat and amp dust and the faint burnt-resistor tang of a Marshall that had been run hard — and under that, because she carried it with her, there was the faint sour-sweet smell of pig on her flannel, which she knew, and was not going to apologize for to a room of four hundred and forty-three people.

She set the Yamaha on its strap. The scratch along the lower bout caught the gel light the blue of a bruise. The soft case lay open behind her amp, the shoelace she tied it shut with trailing out over the edge of the riser.

Robin came up on her left with the bass. Kristy took the Fender and the second mic. Steve Bankuti was already behind the kit, a kid from Sudbury Southeast whose father drove a milk route; he adjusted his snare stand one quarter-turn and then sat with his sticks crossed on his knee like a boy waiting to be told he was allowed to stand up at the dinner table.

Patty leaned into the mic. She did not say anything. She tuned her B string — B was always the one — and she nodded at Robin.

In the wings stage-left Nick was leaning on a flight case with a towel around his neck. In the wings stage-right, across the width of the proscenium, Jackie was standing the way Jackie stood when he was trying not to be seen, hands at his sides, the black piano jacket still buttoned. Patty saw both of them the way you see a thing you are not going to look at twice. She looked back at the neck of the guitar.

Steve's kick pedal squeaked on beat two. A small high squeak, a dry hinge, the way his father's truck door squeaked. Patty smiled — a small unmeant smile, because something had surprised her — and went into the opening figure of "Dreams."

*Now here you go again, you say you want your freedom.*

She held the word *go*. Not long, the way a singer shows off a hold, but long enough. Her C chord rang out clean under it, the one she had botched in the empty St. Michael's on Monday night and laughed at out loud — her ring finger sat in the third fret now and did not buzz. The auditorium came down off the high Aerosmith ride it had been on. A JV booster-club mother halfway down the aisle with a cardboard tray of Cokes stopped mid-stride between booths and did not put the tray down; she only stopped. In row H a man put his arm around his wife's shoulder and left it there. The held note came down onto the next line and Patty let it.

She did not talk between songs. She tuned the B again. Robin adjusted a tuning peg. Kristy sipped water. Steve wiped his palms on his jeans. The room waited the way a room waits when it has agreed, without anyone saying so, that the person on the stage gets to choose the pace.

She counted them in for "Silver Springs."

This one she had not learned for Nick and Jackie. She had learned it last week in the fluorescent-lit basement of St. Michael's from a cassette Kristy had dubbed, and she had sung it out loud to four middle-aged parish women who came on Thursdays to arrange the altar flowers, and those four women had stood in the doorway of the basement with their hands in their cardigan pockets and listened. Her grandmother had sung in a kitchen in Sudbury Southeast to a radio on top of a Frigidaire in 1954. Her mother had sung in the same kitchen in 1966 with a baby on her hip. When Patty came into the long held second chorus — *you'll never get away from the sound of the woman that loves you* — she was not thinking about Nick. She was not thinking about Jackie. She was thinking about the four women at St. Michael's and the two women in her kitchen, and the private inheritance that lived in her hands was what went out into the microphone. The note bloomed. Kristy came in on the second *loves you* with a harmony a third below, and the two voices braided and held, and Steve's ride cymbal shimmered under it like water moving over flat stones.

The man in row H had his face in his hand. Patty could see him because the house lights were half up, the way Lincoln-Sudbury ran the Battle so the judges could take notes; she could see the whole first fifteen rows as clearly as she could see her own fingers. She let the chorus fall and stepped back off the mic and tuned the B one more time.

Robin looked at her. Patty nodded.

Steve's count-in on the sticks was a count-in she had been hearing in her head since sometime last November — four clean clicks of maple on maple — and then the bass came in on the Lindsey Buckingham pulse, eighth notes driving, and Kristy's rhythm guitar chunked down on the one, and Patty opened her mouth and sang the song that had been living under her breath since the jazz band room.

*Loving you / isn't the right thing to do.*

The hum had been in her for seven months. It had caught on her flute G key in Mr. Wentworth's room with Nick's amp plugged in and Jackie's hands on the keys. It had caught in her throat on the eve, a splinter in a finger, a sow sighing out in the dark barn. It had sat folded with the seven letters in the back of the camp notebook. Now it came all the way out. Not a fragment. Not a hum. The whole song, at full voice, in front of four hundred and forty-three people, her bare feet on a warm stage, the Yamaha FG-180 hanging off her shoulder by a hand-me-down strap.

The chorus came around the first time and the room found her. By the second chorus Kristy's harmony was over her like a second sky, and Robin's bass was walking the Buckingham line the way the record walked it, and Steve hit the snare crack on the two and the four the way a carpenter drives a nail — one hit, clean, no flourish. Patty saw Nick at the back of the stage-left wing for one beat. She saw Jackie at the back of the stage-right wing for one beat. They were not looking at each other. They were both looking at her. She did not hold either look. She went back to her fingers.

*You can go your own way. Go your own way.*

She sang the bridge and the last chorus and let the last chord ring, and she muted it with the flat of her right palm on the strings.

One beat of silence.

Then it came. Not polite. Rows standing up in a wave that started in the middle and went outward. The unstuck acoustic of the auditorium, four hundred and forty-three throats and twice that many hands. Some man near the back was shouting something Patty could not parse through the rest of it, a single syllable over and over, maybe her name, maybe just a sound. The JV booster mother was still standing in the aisle with the tray of Cokes and she was clapping with the tray balanced on her forearm.

Patty lifted the Yamaha off her shoulder. She knelt on the riser and laid it in the soft case and pulled the zipper as far as it would go and tied the shoelace through the broken pull tab, the knot she used at home to shut the barn gate. She picked up her boots by the laces in her left hand and the case in her right.

She walked off stage-left, past the monitor, past the flight case Nick had been leaning on — he had moved — and into the dim of the wing.

Kristy was there. Kristy put a hand on her shoulder and left it there for about two seconds and then took it off.

Steve was there. Steve did not know what to do with his face. He said, "Hi."

"Hi," Patty said.

She stood for a minute in the wing with her boots in her hand and listened to the applause do what applause did when it was not going to stop on its own, which was go on until somebody on the crew brought the house lights up the rest of the way.
