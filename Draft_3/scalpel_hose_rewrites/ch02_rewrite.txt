CHAPTER 2: JACKIE'S WORLD — THE GILDED CAGE

Jackie Marsh woke at 6:47 AM — three minutes before his alarm, the way he always did. The walls were painted a color his mother called Dove Gray. The bookshelves held alphabetized volumes he'd actually read. His piano sat in the corner like a judge waiting to render verdict.

Everything had its place. The tuxedo hung pressed on the closet door for tonight's recital. Sheet music stacked in performance order on the piano bench — Chopin, Bach, Debussy, Rachmaninoff, and the Mozart that would close the evening. His guitar — the electric one his father pretended not to know about — was stored in its case under his bed, gathering dust.

He sat up and looked at the framed photo on his nightstand: himself at seven, hands positioned on piano keys, his mother standing behind him with the kind of smile that suggested she'd finally gotten something right. The photo had won a prize in some parenting magazine — Classical Excellence: Nurturing Your Child's Musical Gift. Jackie remembered the photographer making him hold the position for twenty minutes while they adjusted the lighting.

Through his bedroom window he could see toward the other side of Hop Brook — not that he was looking that direction. Not that he was thinking about someone who used to be his friend before high school taught them they belonged in different worlds.

Tonight was the recital. Tonight he would sit in front of his parents' friends and his father's colleagues and play Chopin and Bach and Debussy like he'd been trained to do since he was five. Everyone would applaud politely and tell Dr. Marsh that his son was remarkably talented, and Dr. Marsh would accept the compliments like tributes to his excellent parenting.

And tonight, if he had the courage, Jackie was going to do something that would remind everyone — including himself — that somewhere beneath all this carefully constructed perfection was a kid who still believed Freddie Mercury understood something Mozart never did.

---

The same week Maxanne Sartori discovered Nick Johnson's garage, Dr. Richard Marsh had called the station personally — a rare gesture, since university professors usually delegated such tasks to their secretaries. He wanted to ensure Maxanne understood just how significant his son's recital would be.

"Jackie trained at the New England Conservatory prep division," he'd said, his voice carrying the careful pronunciation of someone who'd worked hard to lose whatever accent he'd grown up with. "He's being considered for early admission to Juilliard."

Maxanne had wanted to decline. Classical recitals were not her beat. But something in Dr. Marsh's voice — a father's pride laced with something that sounded almost like fear — compelled her to agree. Same desperation she'd heard in Grace Johnson's *don't fill his head with dreams we can't afford*. Different words. Same underlying terror that music might ruin a child's future.

---

By seven o'clock that evening, the Marsh house stood transformed. Folding chairs in rows. A rented Steinway grand that cost more than Grace Johnson made in a year. Champagne chilling in silver buckets. The audience was prep school parents and university faculty — the kind of people who applauded with their fingertips and discussed summer houses on the Cape between selections.

The Marsh house sat on the good side of Hop Brook like it had been placed there by a decorator: Colonial perfection, black shutters against white clapboard, a lawn that would be green come spring. You could walk from Nick Johnson's garage to this front door in twelve minutes, but you'd have to cross Hop Brook — and that narrow stream might as well have been the Atlantic.

Jackie stood in the upstairs bathroom, adjusting his bow tie for the third time. His hands were steady. His mind was not. In two hours he could be back in this bathroom, everyone gone, his parents satisfied that he'd performed exactly as expected. Or he could do what he'd been thinking about for weeks, and everything would change.

---

Jackie entered the living room at precisely 7:30 PM in a tuxedo tailored to fit him, seventeen years old but carrying himself like someone who'd been preparing for this moment since birth. He was beautiful in that way that good nutrition and orthodontics and SAT tutors can manufacture. When he sat at the piano, something changed.

He began with Chopin's Nocturne in E-flat major. His fingers found the keys with practiced precision that spoke of thousands of hours, of lessons that started before memory, of parents who considered anything less than excellence a character flaw. The notes flowed like water, technically perfect, each phrase shaped exactly as his teachers had instructed.

Maxanne was watching Jackie's face. Behind the technical perfection he was dying in there — a boy playing someone else's definition of music, speaking someone else's language with flawless pronunciation and no soul.

The audience nodded, already composing the compliments they'd deliver with their champagne. Dr. Marsh sat in the front row, expression carefully neutral, posture radiating satisfaction.

Jackie moved through the program with mechanical excellence. Bach's Goldberg Variations — excerpts only, because even at a private recital you had to consider your audience's attention span. Debussy's "Clair de Lune." Rachmaninoff's Prelude in C-sharp minor.

Each piece was perfect. Each piece was empty.

Between the Rachmaninoff and the final Mozart, Jackie paused. The pause stretched longer than it should have. Dr. Marsh cleared his throat — a warning about proper recital pacing. But Jackie wasn't listening to his father. He was listening to something else.

His fingers found different keys. The opening notes of "Bohemian Rhapsody" emerged from that Steinway like escapees from prison.

The room froze. You could hear pearls clutching themselves.

"Is this the real life?" Jackie sang, his classically trained voice hitting notes that would have made Freddie Mercury proud. "Is this just fantasy?"

His hands moved across the keys with all the technical skill he'd brought to Chopin, but now that precision served something real. His left hand found a bass line that rumbled through the Steinway's frame and into the hardwood floor. His right hand built harmonies that shouldn't have been possible on a solo piano — Mercury's operatic architecture rendered in ivory and wire and eleven years of lessons finally finding their purpose.

He sang. His voice, trained for Schubert lieder and careful projection, broke open. The champagne glasses on the sideboard vibrated. Dr. Marsh's water glass hummed against its coaster. Four minutes and fifty-five seconds, and Jackie Marsh wasn't performing for anyone in that room.

When he finished, no one clapped. No one moved. Two hundred pounds of social order sat on folding chairs and tried to remember how to breathe.

Dr. Marsh was the first to speak. "That was... unexpected."

"That was rock and roll," Maxanne said, standing up because someone needed to. "That was your son showing you who he really is."

Jackie looked at her then — really looked — and Maxanne saw the same thing she'd seen in Nick Johnson's eyes three days earlier.

"You're Maxanne Sartori," he said. "You play real rock and roll."

Maxanne gave him her card — the same one she'd given Nick. "Come by the station. Bring whatever music lives in you."

Jackie took the card. His hands were still shaking. He looked at it, then at Maxanne, then at his father's face — which had rearranged itself into something Jackie had never seen before. Not anger. Not pride. Something older and more complicated. Dr. Marsh's right hand moved in his lap, pinky and ring finger curling inward once against his thumb — an unconscious reach, the shape the hand had made a thousand times on keys decades ago, years before the Baldwin lid had closed for good.

"Thank you," Jackie said. "For understanding."

The other guests murmured. Mrs. Marsh moved through the room offering champagne, deflecting questions, doing what she'd been trained to do when the social order cracked.

Maxanne walked to her car thinking she'd found two boys worth saving. She didn't know she was setting them on a collision course.
