CHAPTER 18A: THE STATION MEETING

Two hours and forty-one minutes.

Grace Johnson just left a PTA meeting in her Friendly's uniform and told a room full of professionals what they didn't want to hear. Forty-two to eleven. Not even close. I told you that woman had steel in her spine.

I pour another inch of Pappy. Just an inch. I need to be sharp enough to finish this story and drunk enough to tell the truth.

"You know what the difference is between a rebel and a sellout?" I say into the microphone. "About one meeting. One conference room. One moment when you trade what you believe for what they'll approve. You walk in pure and walk out with a company line, and it happens so smooth you don't feel the cut until you're bleeding all over someone else's floor."

I lean back and the chair protests, and I think: I know how it feels.

"The fall of '76. Two and a half years before the Battle of the Bands, six months before I walked out of WBCN for good, a Tuesday morning meeting I thought was going to be about fall programming. Let me tell you about the meeting that changed everything."

---

WBCN's management office sat on the third floor above the studios — a room that had once been somebody's idea of cool and had aged into somebody's idea of a health code violation. The carpet was the color of a bruise, stained with decades of spilled coffee and dropped cigarette ash. The walls held framed gold records from bands the station had broken, hung at angles that suggested whoever mounted them had been in a hurry or half in the bag. Aerosmith. J. Geils. Boston. The Cars. Each frame a trophy for a war we'd already won and were about to surrender.

The conference table was a slab of pressboard that buckled in the middle, covered in ring stains from mugs ten years of people had set down and picked up while they debated what rock and roll owed to its audience. I'd sat at this table when we decided to give Aerosmith their first heavy rotation. I'd sat here when we broke The Cars and the phones rang for three days straight. I'd argued at this table, cursed at this table, once threw a promotional ashtray at Tom Andrews across this table because he wanted to put a jingle between Led Zeppelin and Black Sabbath.

This was where the real work happened.

The meeting started at ten AM. Charlie Morris — station manager, joint-smoker, believer in letting DJs run free — sat at the head, which told me nothing was wrong. But the man sitting to Charlie's right told me everything was wrong.

His name was Gerald Kowalski. New Program Director, hired six weeks earlier from a station in New York. Late thirties, sharp jaw, hair parted with the precision of someone who had never in his life played an instrument or felt the floor shake under a Marshall stack. He wore a suit in a building where nobody wore suits. He carried a leather portfolio the color of money. His shoes were Italian, polished to a shine that reflected the overhead fluorescents like a funhouse mirror.

I hated him on sight, which I'll admit isn't fair. But I've spent my life trusting my instincts about people the same way I trust them about music, and Gerald Kowalski's frequency was static. Pure, manufactured, corporate static. I would be gone from this building in six months. I did not know that yet. He did.

Tom Andrews sat across from me. Tom — at WBCN since '71, who'd broken J. Geils when Peter Wolf was still his overnight DJ roommate, who'd once stayed on the air twenty-seven straight hours during a blizzard because he believed the city needed rock and roll more than it needed sleep. Tom caught my eye and gave me the smallest nod: *I know. Hang on.*

"Let's talk about the future," Kowalski said.

He opened his portfolio and spread papers across the buckled table like a general deploying troops. Charts. Graphs. Demographic breakdowns printed in the kind of typeface that makes everything look scientific and nothing feel human. Columns of numbers, age ranges, household income brackets, pie charts sliced into segments labeled 18–24, 25–34, Male, Female, College-Educated, Blue-Collar.

"WBCN's core audience is aging," he said. "Our 25–34 demo is up seven percent, but our 18–24 — your growth market, your future — is down twelve percent in the last eighteen months. The advertisers who matter — automotive, audio equipment, lifestyle brands — they're buying 18–34. That's where the dollars live."

He tapped a chart like a teacher correcting a student.

"We need to grow the 18–34 demo, or we lose the automotive sponsors. Lose the automotive sponsors, we lose the revenue that keeps this station independent. Lose the independence — " He spread his hands and smiled the kind of smile that knows it's winning. "Well. You all know what's happening to independent radio."

We did. Clear Channel was swallowing FM stations like a python digesting rabbits. One by one, independent voices replaced by automated playlists generated by consultants in offices that had never been within a thousand miles of the bands they programmed. Boston had lost three stations in two years. We all knew the python was circling our building.

Charlie tapped his pen on the table. "Gerald has a proposal."

Kowalski pulled out a single sheet. Clean. Typed. The heading read: WBCN COMMUNITY ENGAGEMENT INITIATIVE — BATTLE OF THE BANDS.

"Regional high school music competition," he said. "WBCN sponsors. We brand it. We market it to schools within a thirty-mile radius of Boston. Kids enter their bands, we provide judges from our DJ roster, winner gets a recording session and airtime exposure. We sell sponsorship packages — Coca-Cola, local car dealerships, Jordan's Furniture — and position WBCN as the station that discovers the next generation of rock talent."

He paused. Looked around the table with the confidence of a man who knows he's selling something people are going to buy.

"It's community engagement that drives 18–34. It's authentic — local kids, local music, local pride. It's sponsorable — brands want to be associated with youth and discovery. And it's repeatable — we build it as an annual franchise that generates revenue every spring."

The silence lasted about four seconds. The time it takes to cross a line you can't uncross.

"Annual franchise," Tom Andrews said. Not a question. A repetition, the way you repeat a diagnosis you're not ready to accept.

"The data supports it." Kowalski tapped his charts. "Stations that run community engagement programming see a fourteen percent lift in target demos within two rating periods. WJLB in Detroit. KROQ in LA. It's proven."

I looked at Tom. Tom looked at me. And in that look was everything: this was where the road forked. This was where WBCN either stayed what it was — a station that found real music and played it at full volume because we believed in it — or became what everything else was becoming. A brand.

I opened my mouth. And what came out surprised me.

"What if we use it to actually find talent?"

The table turned to me.

"Not as a brand exercise," I said. "Not as a demographic play. As what WBCN does. Discovery. We go into these schools and we listen. We send DJs who know the difference between a kid who can play and a kid who can make you feel something. We don't look for the next marketable act — we look for the next real thing. The next Aerosmith playing a bar in Allston that nobody knows about yet."

Tom leaned forward. "Maxanne's right. If we do this — if we put our name on it — it has to be authentic. WBCN discovers talent. That's the brand, Gerald. If this turns into a glorified corporate talent show, we lose the one thing that makes us worth sponsoring in the first place."

Kowalski watched us the way a chess player watches an opponent move exactly where he wants them. "I agree completely," he said.

He agreed. That should have been my first warning. When the man in the suit agrees with the rebels, the rebels just walked into the cage and closed the door behind themselves.

"Discovery. Authenticity. That's exactly the narrative we sell to sponsors. 'WBCN — Where Legends Begin.' We find talent, we give them a platform, sponsors associate their brands with the discovery process. Everybody wins."

Charlie tapped his pen. "So we're doing this?"

"We're doing this," Kowalski said. "First Battle — spring of '79, let's say, gives us the school-year runway. Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School. I've already reached out to their music teacher, a Bob Wentworth, who's got connections to the local scene. He's enthusiastic. We start regional, prove the model, expand next year." He looked at me. "Maxanne, I want you to judge. You're the face of WBCN discovery. Your credibility makes this real."

My credibility. Like it was a product he could borrow.

"I'll judge," I said. "But I'm writing the rules."

Kowalski's smile didn't waver. "Of course. Tom, you co-draft with Maxanne. Get me something by Friday." He gathered his charts and his portfolio and his Italian shoes and stood. "One more thing. This needs to work. First time, every time. The sponsors are watching. Make it pay."

Make it pay.

He said it the way you'd tell a vending machine to deliver the soda after you've already put in the coins.

The door closed behind him and the room exhaled.

"Well," Charlie said, lighting a joint. "That's the future."

Tom and I sat. The gold records on the wall stared down at us — bands we'd discovered when discovery meant something, when it wasn't a line item in a sponsorship deck.

"We can still make this real, right?" I said. Not to Tom. Not to Charlie. To the room itself, like the walls might answer.

Tom rubbed his eyes. "If we write the rules. If we control who judges. If we keep the sponsors at arm's length and the kids at the center."

"Then we write the rules," I said.

---

I wrote them that night.

Alone in my apartment in Cambridge — a one-bedroom with a fire escape that rattled when trucks passed and a kitchen window that looked out on a brick wall close enough to touch. I sat at my kitchen table with a legal pad and a bottle of red wine and wrote the rules for WBCN's first Battle of the Bands like I was drafting a treaty between two countries that didn't know they were at war.

Entry requirements. Judging criteria — originality, emotional connection, technical ability, stage presence, with originality and emotional connection weighted double. Prize structure — five hundred dollars to the winner, and a recording session at WBCN's Studio C for the winner and any runner-up.

And then I got to Article 7, Section 3.

I poured more wine.

Article 7, Section 3: "In the event of a tie in the final round, all tied contestants shall share the studio recording session equally, collaborating on material of their choosing, to demonstrate the spirit of musical cooperation that WBCN's Battle of the Bands is designed to foster."

I read it back. Clean, legal, boring. The kind of clause nobody reads in a rules packet. Boilerplate. Standard contest legalese.

Except it wasn't.

I'd already heard the rumors. Two kids from Sudbury — one working-class, one wealthy, once best friends, now enemies — both entering the Battle. Nick Johnson, the guitar kid from Dutton Road with the dead father's garage and the sound that could pull tears out of people who didn't know they were sad. Jackie Marsh, the classically trained prodigy from Maple Street with the Queen belt buckle and the rock and roll heart trapped inside a Juilliard application.

I'd already visited both of them. Already heard what they could do separately. Already imagined — the way you imagine a song before you write it, hearing the ghost of the melody before a single note is played — what they could do together.

The tie clause wasn't boilerplate. It was an ambush. A carefully engineered piece of fine print designed to force two estranged friends into the same recording studio, because I'd convinced myself that if they played together one more time, they'd remember. They'd hear the frequency they'd lost. They'd find their way back to the harmony that had made a middle school gymnasium stand on its feet four years earlier.

I was engineering reconciliation the way Kowalski engineered demographics. Using rules and structures to produce an outcome I'd already decided was right. Convinced that because my intentions were good — because I wanted those boys to make music together — the manipulation was justified. Noble, even.

I finished the wine. Slid the legal pad across the table and stared at Article 7, Section 3.

"It's just a contingency," I said to my empty kitchen. "Standard contest procedure. Ties happen."

Ties happen. Right. And lightning happens, and asteroids happen, and sometimes the thing you've been hoping for your entire career just falls into your lap. Except this wasn't going to fall. I was going to push it. I was going to score those boys close enough that the tie clause would trigger, and then I was going to put them in a room and lock the door and call it opportunity.

Because I believed — genuinely, desperately, with every molecule of the woman who'd walked into the Brickyard in 1973 and heard a sound that changed her life — that Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh belonged together. That their separation was a crime against music. That if the world wasn't going to fix this, Maxanne Sartori would.

I turned off the kitchen light. Went to bed. Slept the kind of sleep you only get when you've told yourself a lie you really need to believe.

---

2009. Studio B. My hands around the demo tape.

"That was the meeting," I say into the microphone. "Fall of '76. The day WBCN officially became a business instead of a movement. The day I watched a man with demographic charts convince a room full of rebels that rebellion could be monetized, and the rebels — the rebels meaning me — said yes because we thought we could outrun the cage."

I turn the tape in my hands. The label in my block lettering. Evidence.

"And that night, alone in my kitchen, I wrote the trap. Dressed it up as a contest rule. Told myself it was boilerplate when it was a loaded gun. I wrote Article 7, Section 3 knowing exactly what I was doing, and I lied to myself about it the way people always lie to themselves when they believe the ends justify the means."

The ON AIR light pulses. The station hums its late-night hum, the machinery of broadcast holding steady even as the clock eats what's left.

"The person who rigs the game because they're sure they know better — that person is the hardest to forgive. Because that person is convinced they love you."

I set the tape down.

"That was the moment I became what I'd fought. I just didn't know it yet. It would take two boys, a recording session, and thirty years of midnight silence for me to hear the frequency I'd jammed."

I cue the next track. "Somebody to Love." Queen. Freddie Mercury searching for what we all search for: someone who sees us as we are and doesn't look away.

"Thirty months from that meeting to the Battle of the Bands. Thirty months I spent convincing myself I'd written a fair piece of fine print. Tonight I'm going to tell you what I actually wrote, and why, and who paid for it."

I press play. Freddie's voice fills the booth — pleading, soaring, desperate for connection in a world that keeps handing him perfection when all he wants is something real.

"They don't know what's coming. Neither do I, not really. We all think we're making our own choices. We all think we're steering the ship."

The music climbs.

"We're not. The rules are already written. The cage is already built. And the lock — the lock clicks so quiet you don't hear it until you're already inside."

**END CHAPTER 18A**
