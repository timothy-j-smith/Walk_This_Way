CHAPTER 13: MAXANNE — WHEN FRIENDSHIP STARTED DYING

Three hours left.

The demo cassette is on the console at my right hand. Maxell, ninety-minute, label hand-printed in ballpoint: *Demo — Nick/Jackie — un-Authorized — March 1979*. The plastic's warm. I keep picking it up and putting it down like I'm trying to decide if it's real.

The ON AIR sign blinks. Behind the glass, the kid they gave me for engineering — Bruins hoodie, laptop open, Red Bull sweating on the table — thinks music starts when somebody opens a DAW. He doesn't know he's working my last shift. They drew straws for tonight and I drew short. Everybody else gave their speeches this week. What you're hearing now is the one nobody wanted.

Two fingers of Heaven Hill in a paper cup. I've earned it.

On the turntable behind me, the station's farewell record is cued up and I haven't dropped the needle. Pink Floyd. *Shine On You Crazy Diamond.* The corporate playbook says that's how WBCN goes out. Graceful. Elegiac. The kind of ending you can put in a press release.

I reach for the needle. I don't touch it.

---

Thirty-nine years in rooms like this, and I still don't know what to say at a funeral.

So I'm going to tell you about two kids.

Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh. 1979. Sudbury, Massachusetts — a town with a soft-rock name and a geography like a knife. Route 20 splits it in half. North side: Tudors, horse fences, driveways long enough to teach a kid to drive. South side: ranches where the truck sits on blocks with the hood up and somebody's always halfway under it. Hop Brook runs between. There's a wooden bridge over that brook, and we'll get to the bridge in a minute. Not yet.

I met those boys through Maxell tape. Demo I wasn't supposed to have, the way demos come. I've discovered bands in worse ways. I broke Aerosmith out of a dive in Allston. I put The Cars on air when the labels still thought Ric Ocasek was a weirdo with cheekbones. Everybody at this station has a wall full of Christmas cards from people who went on to fill arenas. Mine's on the north wall. You can see it from here.

Nick and Jackie should be on that wall.

They're not.

---

November 14th, 1977.

Everything runs from that date, so I put it down first.

Dan Johnson died on a construction site off State Street. Crane cable snapped. Twenty-three years of a man who built things with his hands, gone in the time it takes to breathe in. The Globe gave him one paragraph on page six, wedged between a fender-bender on Route 9 and a three-alarm in Framingham. They misspelled the last name.

That's what a working man's death is worth in print. A paragraph and a misspelled last name.

Nick was fifteen.

Grace Johnson went back to Friendly's the next morning. Not because she was tough. Because the mortgage doesn't give a damn how you feel, and when you don't have a cushion, grief is a luxury you can't afford. You carry your dead to work with you and you smile when the table asks for more coffee.

---

Nick stopped talking.

The guitar spoke for him. The Dutton Road garage became his whole vocabulary. Sawdust and motor oil, the concrete slab cold enough through his socks to make your teeth ache, Dan's tools still on the pegboards in the exact order Dan left them — Phillips-heads in descending size, the coffee can of sixteen-penny nails, a wrench set nobody had moved since November. Nick played till his fingers bled through the calluses and then he played some more. He wasn't practicing. He was bleeding off pressure the only way he knew.

I can see Jackie standing in that garage doorway. Clean jeans, one hand on the jamb, trying to find a sentence that won't make it worse.

I know what Nick said. Jackie told me six years later, in a booth at the Paradise, and he said it like a man reading off a charge sheet.

*I don't need your pity.*

Four words. That's how friendships end. Not in a fight. In a sentence you can't take back, because it was true enough to hurt.

---

Two hours and twelve minutes left.

You want a villain for this part of the story? I can't give you one. Jackie didn't do anything wrong. He showed up. He showed up with homework and with offers to just sit in the garage and not say anything. What Jackie couldn't know — his father was still at the kitchen table every morning — was that if Nick accepted help from the kid on the other side of Hop Brook, Nick was admitting the bridge existed. And the bridge was the thing he couldn't afford to see.

So Nick sent him home.

Then Jackie sent himself home. And the day after that, and the day after that.

By spring of '78 they were nodding at each other in the Lincoln-Sudbury hallway like guys who'd met once at a party. That school kept making musicians — Mike Gordon was two doors down in the band room, bass case under the chair, nobody knew yet. The air at L-S was thick with kids who were going to be somebody. Nick and Jackie walked past each other in it like weather.

The cafeteria told on them. Nick sat with the kids whose fathers' names came up in the police log. Jackie sat with the kids whose fathers' names came up in the alumni magazine. Same room. Different zip code.

---

Here's the thing nobody warns you about this job.

Sometimes you hear the tape and you know. You know the way you know a storm is coming an hour before the sky cracks. I heard that Maxell in my kitchen on a Sunday afternoon in April of '79, and I stood up from the table with a coffee going cold in my hand, and I said out loud, to nobody in particular — *oh.*

I was looking for the next Neighborhoods. Upton boys, Rat regulars, the band WBCN's brand-new Rumble was going to put on the map that summer. I spent every weekend in Kenmore Square basements with beer on my boots, hunting for the next one. I had a type. Rough edges. Two voices that shouldn't work together but do.

I was looking for the next Neighborhoods, and they were on a cassette on my kitchen table.

Two kids. One in a garage that still smelled like his dead father. One in a bedroom lined with sheet music and a father who told him hands like his were for surgery. I put the tape down on the Formica and I thought, *I can save these boys.*

I have been wrong in this business before. I don't know that I've ever been wronger.

---

The thing about John Lennon is, he told us. Late in 1980, a few months before he went. About Bruce. *God help Bruce Springsteen when they decide he's no longer God.*

I've watched that prophecy come true in slow motion for thirty years. Not just Bruce. All of them. The machinery that lifts kids up is the same machinery that grinds them down, and the hand at the lever does not change.

Nick and Jackie never even got lifted. The machinery went to work on them before anyone outside that garage heard a note.

---

The Pink Floyd LP is still sitting there.

*Shine On You Crazy Diamond.* I could drop the needle right now and let Gilmour take us out. That's what the memo says. That's what the listeners expect. That's the ending somebody wrote on a clipboard in a glass tower downtown and forgot about ten minutes later.

I look at the record.

I look at the cassette.

I've been picking this tape up and putting it down for thirty years and the weight of it never changes. It's the weight of something that should have flown. Holding a bird's hollow bones and being told this thing used to fly.

One hour fifty-seven minutes left.

Plenty of time to get it wrong one last time. Plenty of time to get it right.

---

Before I tell you what I'm going to do with that tape, you need to hear the rest of the story.

You need to stand on a piece of wood across Hop Brook in October of 1978, late afternoon, oak leaves in the water, and watch two boys try to have a conversation.

That's where it really ended. Not in the hallway at Lincoln-Sudbury. Not in the cafeteria. On a wooden bridge with the brook running low under it — April you can't hear yourself think down there, but by October the water's a whisper, and every word lands.

Two kids went up onto that bridge.

I'll tell you in a minute what walked back down.

Stay with me.

**END CHAPTER 13**
