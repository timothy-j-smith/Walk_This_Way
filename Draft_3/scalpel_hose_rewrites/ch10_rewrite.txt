CHAPTER 10: CAMP ELBANOBSCOT

The bus smelled like diesel and bug spray and the vinyl seats were hot enough to brand the backs of your legs if you sat down wrong. Nick had the window. Jackie had the aisle. Their guitars rode between them, necks pointed toward the ceiling like they knew where they were going even if the boys didn't.

Camp Elbanobscot sat on forty acres of Sudbury conservation land between Route 20 and the reservoir, an outdoor camp that for some odd reason specialized in music. Not archery, not sailing, not the kind of camp where rich kids learned tennis and poor kids learned they couldn't afford to learn tennis. Music. Guitars and keyboards and voices carrying through white pines that had been dropping sap on everything since before the Revolution—your shoes, your sleeping bag, the fretboard of your guitar if you left it leaning against the wrong tree. The sap got into your fingertips and stayed there, tacky and sweet-smelling, so that for weeks after camp ended you'd catch a whiff of pine resin on your hands and feel the ghost of those two weeks rise up through your chest like a chord you'd forgotten you knew.

The cabins were screen-walled, which meant the mosquitoes had open invitations and the music had no walls. You could lie in your bunk at eleven o'clock and hear someone three cabins over working through a chord change, the notes drifting across the compound with the pine smell and the lake damp, tangling with the peepers and the occasional bang of a screen door that never learned how to close quietly. By morning the grass between the cabins was silver with dew and your sneakers were soaked before you reached the dining hall, where the coffee was for counselors only but the smell of it mixed with woodsmoke from the breakfast fire pit and made you feel older than thirteen.

They arrived on a Sunday in early July. Nick and Jackie claimed bunks on the same wall of Cabin 7, guitars propped against the pine planks, and by dinnertime they'd already mapped the place. The lake was a ten-minute walk through birch trees, down a path worn to bare dirt by twenty summers of campers. The music building was a converted barn with a piano that stayed almost in tune and acoustics that made everything sound better than it was. The practice meadow sat on a slope above the water, and if you played there at sunset the sound carried down across the lake and came back to you a half-second later, softened, like the water had heard your song and was singing it back.

That first night, lying in their bunks while the cabin settled into the sounds of six boys breathing and one boy quietly fingering chord shapes on an unplugged electric, Nick said, "This is different."

Jackie didn't ask what he meant. He knew. At Elbanobscot, nobody's last name meant anything. Nobody knew that Nick's house had a mortgage three months behind or that Jackie's father had opinions about rock and roll that could freeze a room. Here the only currency was whether you could play, and both of them were rich.

"Yeah," Jackie said. "It is."

The next morning they met Patty Lally.

She walked into the dining hall during breakfast and the room shifted. Not dramatically—nobody dropped a fork or stopped talking. But the air moved. Later, Nick would understand that some people carried their own weather, and Patty Lally was one of them. She was fourteen, tall and lanky, with straight wheat-colored hair that hung past her shoulders and moved when she moved, like it was listening to music nobody else could hear. She was barefoot. Her feet were brown with dirt and she didn't seem to notice or care, and she carried a canvas bag slung across one shoulder with a songbook sticking out of it, pages flagged with torn strips of newspaper.

She smelled like something. Not perfume—the opposite of perfume. Something faintly animal and earthy that the other kids couldn't quite place, a smell that lived in her jacket and her hair and the leather strap of her bag. It was pig farm. Cavicchio's pig farm, southeast Sudbury, where her family's property backed up against the hog operation and the smell got into everything—your curtains, your school notebooks, the seats of your car. Patty had grown up breathing it. She never apologized for it because she'd never been taught that the place you came from was something to apologize for.

She slid her tray onto the table across from Nick and Jackie and sat down like she'd been sitting there all summer.

"You two are in the intensive, right?" she said. Not a question, really. A confirmation.

Nick nodded. "Guitar. I'm Nick. This is Jackie."

"Piano and guitar," Jackie added.

"I know who you are." She bit into an apple, chewed, kept talking. "Talent show. 'Dream On' and 'Somebody to Love.' You made Karen Bowers cry in the bathroom afterward. That was the best thing I've ever seen at Curtis."

"You go to Curtis?" Nick said, and felt immediately stupid, because of course she did. She'd been there the whole time. She'd been in the hallways, in the cafeteria, at assemblies, a presence he'd walked past a hundred times without registering. But that was before. Before this summer. Before whatever was happening to his brain that made girls suddenly visible in a way they hadn't been six months ago, as if someone had adjusted the frequency on a radio he didn't know he owned and a new signal was coming in, strong and clear and impossible to ignore.

"Curtis," Patty said. "Same as you. We had bio together last semester. I sat two rows behind you." She looked at Nick with a level gaze that had no accusation in it, just amusement. "You never turned around once."

Nick felt heat climb his neck. Jackie was very quiet beside him, holding his milk carton with both hands.

"I play piano," Patty said. "And I sing. And I hum constantly, which drives everyone insane." As if to prove it, she started humming something—a melody that moved in a way Nick couldn't quite track, rising and turning back on itself, restless and certain at the same time. He didn't recognize the song. It sounded like it belonged to her, like she'd invented it, though years later he'd hear "Go Your Own Way" on the radio and feel a jolt of recognition so sharp it would stop him in the middle of whatever he was doing, because that was it, that was the melody she'd been carrying around inside her at thirteen, humming it into existence before the rest of the world had caught up.

She stopped humming and looked at both of them. "So what are we working on?"

That was Patty. No preamble, no negotiation. We. As if the three of them had already agreed on something and were just getting started.

Over the next five days they became the thing the other campers talked about. Not because they were the best musicians at Elbanobscot—there was a cellist from Newton who could make you cry and a drummer from Framingham who played like he had six arms—but because something happened when the three of them played together that didn't happen with anyone else.

They found it on the third afternoon, in the practice meadow above the lake.

Nick was noodling on his guitar, working through a progression he'd stolen from Joe Perry and bent into something his own fingers preferred. Jackie sat at the portable keyboard the camp kept under a tarp, adding chords underneath Nick's line—not copying it, not competing with it, but building a floor for it to stand on, the way he always did, his classical training turning raw rock into architecture. They'd done this before, the two of them. It was good. It was always good.

Then Patty started singing.

She didn't ask what key they were in. She didn't wait for a verse or a chorus. She just opened her mouth and a voice came out that was lower than you'd expect from a girl that thin, with a grain in it like fine sandpaper, a texture that caught on the melody and held it. She sang without words at first—just vowel sounds, aahs and oohs that wove between Nick's guitar and Jackie's piano like a third rope braiding into a cord. Her voice found the spaces the instruments left open and filled them without crowding, and when she finally landed on a harmony it locked into place with a physical click, like a door latch falling home.

Nick stopped playing. Jackie stopped playing. The silence that followed had a different shape than the silence before—fuller, charged, like the air after lightning.

"Don't stop," Patty said. "Play it again."

They played it again. And this time all three of them leaned into it—Nick driving the rhythm harder, Jackie pushing the chords into unexpected territory, Patty's voice climbing above both of them and then diving underneath, finding notes that shouldn't have worked but did because her ear was tuned to something the other two couldn't hear on their own. The sound carried down the slope and across the lake, and the lake sent it back, and for a few minutes the meadow held all three of them inside a single resonance that felt less like performing and more like breathing.

The sun was going down. The lake had turned the color of copper, and the tree line on the far shore was black against a sky that was moving from orange to violet in bands so distinct they looked painted. The light caught Patty's hair and turned it gold, and Nick felt something tighten behind his ribs that had nothing to do with music and everything to do with the way she tilted her head when she sang, eyes half-closed, like she was listening to a version of the song that existed somewhere ahead of them in time.

He looked at Jackie. Jackie was watching Patty too.

Not the way you watch a musician. The way you watch a person who has just changed the terms of your life without asking permission. Jackie's hands were still on the keys but he wasn't playing. He was just looking at her, and his face held an expression Nick had never seen on him before—open, unguarded, the careful Marsh composure cracked just enough to let something raw show through.

Nick looked away. He looked at the lake, at the sky, at his own hands on the guitar strings. He understood, in the way you understand things at thirteen—not with words but with the whole stupid machinery of your body—that something had just happened between the three of them that couldn't be unhappened. That the duo had become a trio, and the trio had introduced a new geometry, and geometry meant angles, and angles meant that sooner or later someone would be on the outside looking in.

But not yet. Not tonight.

"Again," Patty said, and grinned, and her grin was the kind that made you believe that this moment was the only moment that mattered and that it would last as long as you needed it to.

They played until the mosquitoes drove them inside. They played in the music building after dinner, working through songs they all knew—Patty leading them into Fleetwood Mac, Nick pulling them toward Aerosmith, Jackie insisting on Queen, and the arguments becoming their own kind of music, a call-and-response that generated more heat than friction. They played on the last night of camp, sitting on the dock with their feet in the black water, Patty humming that same restless melody while Nick picked a quiet line on his acoustic and Jackie added soft chords on a guitar he'd borrowed from the camp supply closet because the keyboard wouldn't survive the dew.

Three voices. Three instruments. The sound moving out across the water in the dark, mixing with the loon calls and the lap of small waves against the dock pilings, and if you were lying in your bunk in Cabin 4 or Cabin 9, half-asleep, you would have heard it and thought it was one voice, one instrument, one song—because that's what it sounded like when they got it right. Not three things combined. One thing that required three people to exist.

The last morning, waiting for the bus, Patty wrote her phone number on the back of a camp schedule and tore it in half. She gave one half to Nick and one half to Jackie.

"You'll have to talk to each other to get the whole number," she said.

Nick laughed. Jackie laughed. The bus came. Patty climbed on first, bare feet on the rubber-matted steps, her canvas bag over her shoulder, humming.

Nick watched her go. He was thirteen years old, and the world had just gotten larger and more complicated in ways he didn't have the vocabulary for yet. The music was bigger now. The friendship was bigger. And the space between what he wanted and what he could name had opened up like the lake at dusk—vast and dark and full of things moving beneath the surface.

He climbed on the bus. Jackie climbed on behind him. They sat in the same seats they'd taken on the way up, guitars between them, and neither of them mentioned Patty for the entire ride home.

They didn't need to. She was in every silence between them now. She would be for a long time.
