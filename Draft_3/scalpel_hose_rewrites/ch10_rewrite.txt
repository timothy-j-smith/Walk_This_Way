CHAPTER 10: CAMP ELBANOBSCOT

The bus smelled like diesel and bug spray and the vinyl seats were hot enough to brand the backs of your legs if you sat down wrong. Nick had the window. Jackie had the aisle. Their guitars rode between them, necks pointed toward the ceiling like they knew where they were going even if the boys didn't.

Camp Elbanobscot sat on forty acres of Sudbury conservation land between Route 20 and the reservoir, an outdoor camp that for some odd reason specialized in music. Not archery, not sailing, not the kind of camp where rich kids learned tennis and poor kids learned they couldn't afford to learn tennis. Music. Guitars and keyboards and voices carrying through white pines that had been dropping sap on everything since before the Revolution—your shoes, your sleeping bag, the fretboard of your guitar if you left it leaning against the wrong tree. The sap got into your fingertips and stayed there, tacky and sweet-smelling, so that for weeks after camp ended you'd catch a whiff of pine resin on your hands and feel the ghost of those two weeks rise up through your chest like a chord you'd forgotten you knew.

The cabins were screen-walled, which meant the mosquitoes had open invitations and the music had no walls. You could lie in your bunk at eleven and hear someone three cabins over working through a chord change, the notes drifting across the compound with the pine smell and the lake damp, tangling with the peepers and the occasional bang of a screen door. By morning the grass was silver with dew and your sneakers were soaked before you reached the dining hall, where the coffee was for counselors only but the smell of it mixed with woodsmoke from the breakfast fire pit and made you feel older than thirteen.

They arrived on a Sunday in late July. Nick and Jackie claimed bunks on the same wall of Cabin 7, guitars propped against the pine planks, and by dinnertime they'd mapped the place. The lake was a ten-minute walk through birch trees, down a path worn to bare dirt by twenty summers of campers. The music building was a converted barn with a piano that stayed almost in tune and acoustics that made everything sound better than it was. The practice meadow sat on a slope above the water, and if you played there at sunset the sound carried down across the lake and came back to you a half-second later, softened.

That first night, lying in their bunks while the cabin settled into the sounds of eight boys breathing and one boy quietly fingering chord shapes on an unplugged electric, Nick said, "This is different."

Jackie didn't ask what he meant. He knew. At Elbanobscot, nobody's last name meant anything. Nobody knew that Nick's house had a mortgage three months behind or that Jackie's father had opinions about rock and roll that could freeze a room. Here the only currency was whether you could play, and both of them were rich.

"Yeah," Jackie said. "It is."

The next morning they met Patty Lally.

She walked into the dining hall during breakfast and the room shifted. Not dramatically—nobody dropped a fork or stopped talking. But the air moved. She was fourteen, tall and lanky, with straight wheat-colored hair that hung past her shoulders. She was barefoot. Her feet were brown with dirt and she didn't seem to notice or care, and she carried a canvas bag slung across one shoulder with a songbook sticking out of it, pages flagged with torn strips of newspaper.

She smelled like something. Not perfume—the opposite of perfume. Something faintly animal and earthy that the other kids couldn't quite place, a smell that lived in her jacket and her hair and the leather strap of her bag. Pig farm. Cavicchio's, southeast Sudbury, where her family's property backed up against the hog operation and the smell got into everything—curtains, school notebooks, the seats of the car. Patty had grown up breathing it. She never apologized for it because she'd never been taught that the place you came from was something to apologize for.

She slid her tray onto the table across from Nick and Jackie and sat down like she'd been sitting there all summer.

"You two are in the intensive, right?" she said. Not a question, really. A confirmation.

Nick nodded. "Guitar. I'm Nick. This is Jackie."

"Piano and guitar," Jackie added.

"I know who you are." She bit into an apple, chewed, kept talking. Her hands on the apple were small and strong and the nails were cut straight across, and at the base of her thumb there was a faint permanent stain the color of weak tea, and a white scar crossed one knuckle like a crease ironed in; Nick noticed and didn't ask. "Talent show. 'Dream On' and 'Somebody to Love.' You made Karen Bowers cry in the bathroom afterward. That was the best thing I've ever seen at Curtis."

"You go to Curtis?" Nick said, and felt immediately stupid, because of course she did. She'd been in the hallways, in the cafeteria, at assemblies, a presence he'd walked past a hundred times without registering. But that was before. Before this summer. Before whatever was happening to his brain that made girls suddenly visible in a way they hadn't been six months ago.

"Curtis," Patty said. "Same as you. We had bio together last semester. I sat two rows behind you." She looked at Nick with a level gaze that had no accusation in it, just amusement. "You never turned around once."

Nick felt heat climb his neck. Jackie was very quiet beside him, holding his milk carton with both hands.

"I play piano," Patty said. "And I sing. And I hum constantly, which drives everyone insane." As if to prove it, she started humming something—a melody that moved in a way Nick couldn't quite track, rising and turning back on itself, restless and certain at the same time. He didn't recognize it. It sounded like it belonged to her, like she'd carried it out of a song the rest of the world hadn't written yet.

She stopped humming and looked at both of them. "So what are we working on?"

We. As if the three of them had already agreed on something.

---

Over the next five days they became the thing the other campers talked about. Not because they were the best musicians at Elbanobscot—there was a cellist from Newton who could make you cry and a drummer from Framingham who played like he had six arms—but because something happened when the three of them played together.

They found it on the third afternoon, in the practice meadow above the lake.

It had been ninety-two degrees at noon. The thunderheads had built up over the reservoir around three and spent themselves in a hard twenty-minute rain that knocked the pollen down and dropped the air ten degrees, and by four-thirty the sky was washed blue and the grass was steaming and everything smelled like wet pine and electricity.

Nick was noodling on his guitar, working through a progression he'd stolen from Joe Perry and bent into something his own fingers preferred. Jackie sat at the portable keyboard the camp kept under a tarp, adding chords underneath—not copying, not competing, but building a floor for Nick's line to stand on, the way he always did. They'd done this before, the two of them. It was good. It was always good.

Then Patty started singing.

She didn't ask what key they were in. She didn't wait for a verse or a chorus. She just opened her mouth and a voice came out that was lower than you'd expect from a girl that thin, with a grain in it like fine sandpaper, a texture that caught on the melody and held it. She sang without words at first—just vowel sounds, aahs and oohs that wove between Nick's guitar and Jackie's piano like a third rope braiding into a cord. Her voice found the spaces the instruments left open and filled them without crowding, and when she finally landed on a harmony it locked into place with a physical click, like a door latch falling home.

Nick stopped playing. Jackie stopped playing. The silence that followed had a different shape than the silence before—fuller, charged, like the air after lightning.

"Don't stop," Patty said. "Play it again."

They played it again. But before the instruments came back in, she took a breath and nodded at them both, and what they found on the second pass, when all three of them opened their mouths on the same held breath, was a plain C-major triad a cappella for one beat: Nick's root underneath, Jackie's G above, Patty's E clean and unshowy on top, and she laughed.

The laugh was short and surprised and belonged to no one. It belonged to the chord.

Then the guitar and the keyboard came back under it and this time all three of them leaned into the song—Nick driving the rhythm harder, Jackie pushing the chords into unexpected territory, Patty's voice climbing above both of them and then diving underneath, finding notes that shouldn't have worked but did because her ear was tuned to something the other two couldn't hear on their own. The sound carried down the slope and across the lake, and the lake sent it back, and for a few minutes the meadow held all three of them inside a single resonance that felt less like performing and more like breathing.

The sun was going down. The lake had turned the color of copper, and the tree line on the far shore was black against a sky that was moving from orange to violet in bands so distinct they looked painted. The light caught Patty's hair and turned it gold, and Nick felt something tighten behind his ribs that had nothing to do with music and everything to do with the way she tilted her head when she sang, eyes half-closed, like she was listening to a version of the song that existed somewhere ahead of them in time.

He looked at Jackie. Jackie was watching Patty too.

Not the way you watch a musician. The way you watch a person who has just changed the terms of your life without asking permission. Jackie's hands were still on the keys but he wasn't playing. He was just looking at her, and his face held an expression Nick had never seen on him before—open, unguarded, the careful Marsh composure cracked just enough to let something raw show through.

Nick looked away. He looked at the lake, at the sky, at his own hands on the guitar strings.

"Again," Patty said, and grinned.

They played until the mosquitoes drove them inside. They played in the music building after dinner, working through songs they all knew—Patty leading them into Fleetwood Mac, Nick pulling them toward Aerosmith, Jackie insisting on Queen, and the arguments becoming their own kind of music, a call-and-response that generated more heat than friction.

---

The night they found the chord, Nick couldn't sleep.

Cabin 7 had gone quiet by eleven the way it always did—eight other boys in their bunks, breath settling into the loose unison of camp fatigue, the pine-board walls ticking as the day's heat let go of the metal roof. Somewhere out past the reservoir a whip-poor-will was calling its own name on repeat, patient and mechanical, the way it does for two hours at a stretch until you stop noticing and then start noticing again. A bullfrog answered from the reedy end of the lake. Someone—Cabin 9 or Cabin 10, too far to tell—was playing a guitar under a blanket, the chords muffled and thumb-shaped, a kid trying to finger-pick "Blackbird" and getting most of it right. Outside the screen, fireflies worked the tall grass in slow, distracted pulses.

The boy two bunks down from Nick snored like a tractor. Not like a snore. Like a tractor—the low hitch and diesel catch of an engine trying to turn over in cold weather, once, twice, then the long roll.

"You awake?" Jackie's voice came down from the bunk across the aisle, pitched so it wouldn't carry over the tractor.

"Yeah."

A pause. Then Jackie said, "There's sheet music in our attic."

Nick rolled onto his side so he was facing the voice. "Yours?"

"My father's. Boxes of it. Piano." The word landed with the weight of a thing Jackie had not said out loud before. "I found it last year looking for skis. He doesn't know I saw."

"He played?"

"He played." Jackie was quiet a minute. "He doesn't anymore. The Baldwin in the den hasn't been tuned since before I was born."

Nick thought about that. About Dr. Marsh coming home from the hospital at seven and reading a journal in the wing chair and not looking at the piano that sat in the same room with him. About the sheet music in its boxes, upstairs, getting colder every winter.

"My dad's got a guitar in the garage," Nick said. "In his toolbox. I'm not supposed to know."

"You opened it?"

"No."

The tractor-boy snored. The whip-poor-will kept its count.

"I don't know what I'm going to do after high school," Nick said. It came out before he'd decided to say it, which was how most of the true things he'd ever said had come out. "I don't know if I can do anything."

"You can play."

"Yeah."

"That's something."

"Is it?"

Jackie didn't answer right away. The kid with the blanket guitar had moved on to something Nick didn't know—a slow descending line, almost a lullaby, the low E string buzzing a little where it needed a new set.

"It's what we've got," Jackie said finally.

Somewhere out beyond Cabin 7 the lake did whatever lakes do at midnight. The screen door of the counselors' cabin banged once and didn't bang again. Nick lay on his back and listened to eight other boys breathing and the whip-poor-will counting and his best friend not sleeping.

He thought about Patty's E on top of the triad. He thought about her hands.

He was asleep before he could think about anything else.

---

They played on the last night of camp, sitting on the dock with their feet in the black water, Patty humming that same restless melody while Nick picked a quiet line on his acoustic and Jackie added soft chords on a guitar he'd borrowed from the camp supply closet because the keyboard wouldn't survive the dew.

Three voices. Three instruments. The sound moving out across the water in the dark, mixing with the loon calls and the lap of small waves against the dock pilings, and if you were lying in your bunk in Cabin 4 or Cabin 9, half-asleep, you would have heard it and thought it was one voice, one instrument, one song.

The last morning, waiting for the bus, Patty wrote her phone number on the back of a camp schedule and tore it in half. She gave one half to Nick and one half to Jackie.

"You'll have to talk to each other to get the whole number," she said.

Nick laughed. Jackie laughed. The bus came. Patty climbed on first, bare feet on the rubber-matted steps, her canvas bag over her shoulder, humming.

Nick watched her go.

He climbed on the bus. Jackie climbed on behind him. They sat in the same seats they'd taken on the way up, guitars between them, and neither of them mentioned Patty for the entire ride home.
