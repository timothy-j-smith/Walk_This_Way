CHAPTER 20: TEACHERS WHO FANNED THE FLAMES

Tuesday afternoon, May 1979, ten days before the Battle. Bob Wentworth had the jazz band in a horseshoe — seventeen kids on folding chairs in the cinder-block room off the east wing, ballast buzz in the fluorescents, dust motes turning in the late light that came slantwise through the one high window. The posters on the wall were a small argument he had been having with himself since 1962. Ellington at the Blue Note. Miles in shadow, the Harmon mute at his lips. Bernstein with his baton down. A tasteful cross-section, or that was what he had told his wife when he tacked them up. A man's divided loyalties in frames.

The Yamaha upright stood against the long wall, lid open, middle C pitted where ten years of middle-school hands had found it first. Nick's Marshall practice amp sat on a chair by the piano, not plugged in yet, the cord coiled on top with the precision of a boy who owned one cord. Music stands at seventeen different heights. A broken maraca on the shelf above the tuba case, the handle snapped off at the bead, which Wentworth kept for reasons he could not articulate. The room smelled of old valve oil and floor wax.

He had the photograph in his drawer. He had been thinking about it all period.

"Put the scales away," he said. He set a stapled two-page chart on each of their music stands. "Brubeck. 'Take Five.' Nick, Jackie — just you two. The rest of you listen."

Seventeen heads came up.

Nick was by the amp, not looking at the piano. Jackie was already on the bench, not looking at the amp. They had not spoken, Wentworth knew, since October. Two Aprils of careful air between them in a building with two hundred and forty feet of corridor.

"Mr. Wentworth," Nick said. He had not picked up the guitar. "I don't think —"

"Five-four," Wentworth said. "One, two, three, four, five. The off-balance is the point. You lean into it. Just see."

Jackie put his hands on the keys. Nick, because there was nowhere else for his hands to go, plugged in.

---

The left hand came first. D, E-flat, F — ostinato, walking — the Brubeck bass figure the way the record had it, Jackie's fingers finding the shape clean on the first pass because classical training had given him the 5/4 the way it had given him everything, for free. The ostinato settled into the room like a heartbeat that had decided to come in a pattern nobody's body expected. One, two, three, four, five. One, two, three, four, five.

Nick listened for four bars. He found the crack between the four and the five and he put a bent blue note in it. Low on the neck. Not loud. The string was slightly flat and he let it be flat. Another bar of listening, and then he threaded the second phrase, longer, letting the time signature lean him sideways and catching himself on a chord, and the chord was not a Brubeck chord, it was an Aerosmith chord, and it sat down in Jackie's ostinato like it had been waiting there.

Jackie's right hand came in above it. Not flourish — restraint. A single high note held, then a run, then held again. He was leaving Nick room.

Nick stepped into the room Jackie left him.

For thirty seconds it was a working thing. The off-balance found its center the way a bicycle finds its center, not by stopping the wobble but by leaning into it. In the horseshoe one kid — Brian Castellano, second chair trumpet — stopped chewing his gum and his jaw hung half-open and he did not remember to close it. In the corner, on flute, Patty Lally from Sudbury Southeast had been humming something of her own under her breath the whole period, a Fleetwood Mac melody she didn't know she was humming, and the hum was going now, quiet, underneath the 5/4 like a third voice the room had not been asked to provide. She lowered the flute to her lap. She did not lift it again.

Wentworth did not move. He was not sure he was breathing. The ballast buzz was the only thing in the building that did not know what was happening in the room.

---

The door whooshed on its hydraulic hinge.

Don Gould came in the way Don Gould came in — Northeastern coffee mug in the left hand, jeans, sleeves rolled to the elbow, wool tie loose at the collar, the AP American History grin he wore on days he thought he was going to teach somebody something. He stopped in the doorway long enough to register the music, which was still happening, the ostinato walking under him, and then he smiled wider.

Patty Lally's hum stopped.

"You know what this is?" Gould said, loud, over the music that was still going. "This is the Lincoln-Douglas debates, right here."

Nick's hand came off the strings.

Jackie's ostinato carried one more bar on its own, alone, the D-E-flat-F figure with nothing on top of it, and then Jackie's left hand stopped too, the last note hanging and dying into the ballast buzz.

---

Nick set his pick down on the amp. He set it down carefully, because if he had not set it down carefully he would have thrown it.

"We were playing music," he said.

Gould was still smiling. "Right, but — the tension, Nick, the tension between —"

"Between the kids who matter and the kids who don't." Nick stood. The strap caught on the music stand; the stand went over; the Brubeck chart went under a chair. He didn't pick it up. "Between the kids whose parents donate the new risers and the kids whose parents die in a trailer the company blames on the guy who was inside it."

Gould's smile did a thing. It did not go away. It got confused at the edges.

"You want a lesson?" Nick was unplugging the cord now. His hands knew the cord. His hands did not need his face to do it. "Fine. Here's your lesson. Jackie's going to Juilliard or Yale or wherever. I'm going to work construction like my dad. Maybe die quick like him too. That American enough?"

In the horseshoe, Patty Lally looked down at the silver keys of the flute in her lap. Her thumb moved on the G key and stopped. Something in her face went somewhere it had not been before.

"Nick." Jackie's voice was small. He had not stood up. His hands had come off the keyboard and were flat on his thighs.

"What."

"Don't."

"Don't what. Don't say it in front of people? Your dad said it in front of people. *These boys don't understand boundaries.* On the bridge. In front of my friends. In front of me."

Jackie's hands came off his thighs and went back to the keys and did not press anything. The fluorescent buzzed. One of the tubes up near the door was going; it ticked twice, a small electric tick, and then buzzed again.

"I was trying to avoid this." Jackie's voice had gone quieter, not louder. The precision was starting to crack at the edges the way it did when he was tired. "I thought maybe we could get through one week without —"

"Without what. Without you stepping aside like a good little prince on a wooden bridge. Without your Queen covers being you slumming with real people."

"Slumming." Jackie said the word like it was a small heavy object somebody had handed him. He did not look up. His breath came in once and did not come out evenly.

He stood. The piano bench scraped on the tile, the short ugly sound of oak on waxed linoleum, and the sound was loud because the room had gone so quiet around it. He shut the fallboard. He did not slam it. He set it down the way his mother set a china cup down.

"See you Friday," Jackie said.

That was all. He walked out. The door went on its hinge and the hinge whooshed and closed on the soft seal.

Nick was still holding the cord.

---

Wentworth got them out of the room. He did not remember, afterward, what he said to do it — some sentence with the word *dismissed* in it, probably — but the seventeen chairs emptied, folders under arms, and the last kid out was Patty Lally, who paused at the door with the flute case under her elbow and looked back once at the piano bench and then was gone.

The door closed on its seal.

The ballast buzz was still the only sound.

Wentworth walked past the Yamaha, past Nick's amp with its coiled cord, past the Brubeck chart under the chair, and went around behind the piano to the stand where the trumpet had been since September, in its black case, and had not been out of its case in fourteen months. He opened the case. The felt lining had gone bald at the bell end. The valve oil bottle on the inside of the lid was three-quarters down, where he had left it.

He took the horn out. He set the mouthpiece in. The mouthpiece was cold. He wet his lips. He raised the horn. The ring on the bell said *Bach Stradivarius 37* in the same small script it had said it in at Jordan Hall in March of 1958.

He blew one note.

It was not a good note. It was an F, and the F sagged under his breath because the embouchure had gone and the lip had gone and the air had gone, and the note came out of the bell and went nowhere. Nobody heard it. The room ate it.

He set the horn back in the case. He closed the case.

He sat down on the piano bench Jackie had left.
