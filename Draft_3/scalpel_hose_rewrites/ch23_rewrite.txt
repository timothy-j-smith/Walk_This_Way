CHAPTER 23: MAXANNE — THE EVE OF BATTLE

Three hours into the broadcast. One hour left.

The Heaven Hill paper cup has gone soft at the rim. I refill it. My hands on the console are steady now. Guilt doesn't shake you forever. It just sits down beside you and waits for the cue.

The ON AIR sign holds its red like an open wound. Behind the glass, the kid finally put the phone down. Quit scrolling about an hour ago. He's watching me the way you watch an old drunk at a bar who's about to say something true.

The Neve 8068 under my fingers was old when I left this station the first time. Faders worn smooth at the tips from a thousand hands. Coffee ring from '86 still there, exactly where I left it. Studio B still smells like the forty years of cigarette smoke nobody bothered to shampoo out of the walls, even after the state banned it in 2003. Some of that smoke is mine. Pall Malls, '73 to '76. I quit the year I quit WBCN. One vice at a time.

On the wall above the console, the old NAB cart rack — steel frame, thirty slots — is empty. Just the dust shadows where the brown-and-cream carts used to sit. Little gray rectangles. Ghosts of rotation. In '75 I could put my hand on any one of those carts in the dark and know the song by the weight of the tape. Now it's a laptop and a playlist somebody in Texas printed out. The kid runs the laptop. The kid does what the laptop tells him. Nobody in this building has their hand on a song anymore.

The Pink Floyd LP is still cued on the turntable, needle up. Waiting. To my right, the demo cassette: *Demo — Nick/Jackie — un-Authorized — March 1979*. I picked it up an hour ago and I haven't put it down.

---

The Battle of the Bands was my idea.

I want that on the record before we go any further. Not Tom Andrews. Tom ran it. Tom executed. The seed was mine, planted in a pitch meeting in the fall of '76 — regional high school contest, WBCN sponsorship, a recording session for the winners. I sold it to management the way you sell anything to management: find the next Aerosmith before the next Aerosmith needs us. Drive the fifteen-to-twenty-fives. Attract the sponsors who wanted the kids.

Six months later, in April of '77, I left.

Elektra had offered me A&R. More money. More reach. I'd spent seven years in this building breaking bands — Aerosmith out of a dive in Allston, the Cars when every label thought Ric Ocasek was a weirdo with cheekbones. I told myself A&R was the next floor up. Same work, better budget. I was still going to find the next Neighborhoods. I'd just find them with a checkbook in my hand.

The Battle went off without me. Spring of '79. Tom called and asked if I'd sit the judges' panel. Elektra said yes before I did. Scout the room, sign somebody. The industry doesn't care which end of the microphone you hold. It only cares that you keep the line moving.

So I came back.

Not as WBCN. As Elektra. In a blazer I wouldn't have owned two years before.

---

May 15, 1979. Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School auditorium. Four hundred and forty-three seats, every one of them filled, every one of them already decided. I sat on the judges' panel between two people whose names I have let myself forget, clipboard on my knees, pencil in my hand.

One concrete image I've kept for thirty years.

Nick Johnson's hands, before Nick played a note. Thumb flat-picking the low E to tune. Knuckles still raw from a week of rehearsal with no stopping. *Dan Johnson's hands*, I thought. *Nick has his father's hands.*

Across the stage, Jackie Marsh tuning too. Jackie's hands were clean. Long fingers. Surgeon's hands the way the father wanted them. The kind of hands that know what a metronome is.

They did not look at each other. Not during tune. Not during the set. Not once.

Grace was in the third row. Still in the polyester. Name tag *Grace* pinned on the apron she hadn't bothered to take off because she'd come straight from a double at Friendly's. The seat next to her was empty. Dan should have been in it. Everybody in the room who knew pretended not to see the empty seat. I had never met Grace. Never have. I knew her from two rows up the way you know weather.

I wrote the contest rules myself. Three months before the event, in my Elektra office with my name on the door, I drafted the tie-breaker clause. Small-print paragraph most panels never read. *In the event of equal scores, the panel may elect to declare co-winners.* I told myself I was being fair. I told myself I was hedging for quality.

What I was doing was leaving a door open.

The clipboard on my knees that night had a scoring rubric the other two judges treated like a church. Technical execution. Arrangement. Stage presence. Originality. Little boxes. Little numbers. Neither side was going to win cleanly on that rubric, and some part of me already knew it. Some part of me had already rigged the ending and was waiting for the other two to catch up.

That's the thing nobody warns you about this job. Sometimes you set a trap months before you meet the people who will walk into it.

---

I didn't create their division.

Hop Brook did that. The cafeteria did that. Dan Johnson's crane cable in November of '77 did that. Dr. Marsh did that, at his kitchen table, in sentences about surgery and sensible futures. Class did that. America did that.

But I took a slow, private death and I put a ticket price on it. I saw two boys drifting and said, let's bring them back together onstage. For content. For sponsors. For a photograph in the Globe.

I didn't kill their friendship. But I gave class warfare a microphone. I sold tickets to watch innocence die. I turned tragedy into entertainment.

And then I called it opportunity.

---

Eleven forty-seven. Thirteen minutes.

Freddie Mercury's voice comes through the monitors from somewhere deeper than analog. He's been gone eighteen years. Thirty years of medicine too late.

I used to tell myself I left this station before I had to compromise. That leaving in '77 was the clean choice. A&R was just a different angle on the same work. I was still looking for the next Neighborhoods. I still had the ear.

Elektra was a cage with better carpet.

I signed the safe ones. I passed on the dangerous ones. I learned to say *demographic* with a straight face. I helped turn the kind of band I used to champion into the kind of band I used to warn kids about. The rebel DJ who wouldn't shut up became a woman in a blazer who filled out quarterlies and measured risk and told herself this was still music.

I went quiet. For thirty-two years.

The station brought me back tonight because nobody else wanted it. I drew short straw. Walked in at nine o'clock to play eulogy for my own life. On paper I'm the guest of honor. In the actual room I'm a mirror.

Four decades of rock radio. Forty-one years, give or take. The brochure says *fifty-six* because somebody in corporate couldn't count and nobody stopped them. Monday morning, 98.5 starts playing contemporary Christian out of this same transmitter. Jesus on the frequency we used to give Patti Smith. I don't know if that's irony or just America.

---

The demo cassette is warm in my palm. *un-Authorized.* Still unplayed. I haven't put it down since the ON AIR light came on.

The Pink Floyd LP waits. *Shine On You Crazy Diamond.* The memo says that's how WBCN goes out. Graceful. Elegiac. Ending by committee.

The clock gives me twelve minutes now.

Before I choose, there are two rooms you need to see. The night before the Battle. May 14, 1979.

Dutton Road first. Nick's garage. His dead father's tools still on the pegboard in the exact order Dan left them — Phillips-heads in descending size, the coffee can of sixteen-penny nails, the wrench set nobody had moved since November of '77. Overhead bulb. Concrete cold through a pair of gym socks. An acoustic guitar worn raw at the twelfth fret because he won't stop playing it even when his fingers leave prints of blood on the strings.

Then across the brook, under the Marsh house. A finished basement. Carpet. Acoustic panels on the walls the color of a manila folder. A Fender Rhodes on a stand. Sheet music in a binder with tabs. A boy whose father is paying for the next fifteen years of his life in advance and expects a receipt.

Two kids. Same hour. Four miles of road and one wooden bridge between them.

Neither of them knowing they'd already lost.

My hands are steady. The engineer behind the glass isn't blinking. I thumb the bourbon cup empty in one swallow, set it down next to the demo tape, and I lean into the mic.

Stay with me.

**END CHAPTER 23**
