CHAPTER 15: THE POSTER

Monday morning, seven-thirty, and the music room at Lincoln-Sudbury still smelled of Friday's varnish — the janitor had refinished the risers over the weekend and the air hung sharp with turpentine. Cinder block walls the color of canned peas. Fluorescents with one tube stuttering. A faded Boston Symphony poster curling off its thumbtacks near the door. And beside it, something brand new.

The poster was a photocopy. You could tell by the fuzz on the letters — that soft Xerox black where the ink had spread a hair into the paper, so the edges of the words looked like they had been breathed on. Legal-sized, 8.5 by 14, and whoever had run the stack had run it hot; a faint gray bar ghosted down the left margin where the drum had smudged. The Lincoln-Sudbury crest sat slightly off-center in the upper corner, the shield canted maybe two degrees off true, as if the kid on the copier had been in a hurry. Four push-pin holes, one at each corner, punched straight through. The paper had a grain you could feel with a thumbnail.

BATTLE OF THE BANDS
May 15, 1979
Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School
Grand Prize: $500 — Professional Recording Session
Sponsored by WBCN — The Rock of Boston

The WBCN letters were set in that unmistakable seventies sans-serif — round, confident, the kind of typeface that belonged on a concert tee. Whoever had pinned the poster had pinned it at a height where tall kids had to stoop slightly to read and short kids had to look up. Nick Johnson came in through the west door at 7:31, on his way to first-period shop. He was reading it by 7:32. Jackie Marsh came in through the east door at 7:33, having left his sheet music in the practice room the night before, and saw Nick before he saw the poster. He read the poster anyway. They stood a foot apart at the bulletin board and their shoulders did not quite touch.

Five hundred dollars. Grace Johnson made two sixty-five an hour at the Friendly's on Route 20, plus tips on a good Sunday. A month of shifts would not close that number. Three months of groceries would. It was also the amount Jackie's father spent on a weekend in Chatham without remarking on it, which meant the figure read one way to the boy coming in through the west door and another way entirely to the boy coming in through the east — a Friday shift on one side, a receipt on the other.

From his desk, Bob Wentworth watched the two boys read. He did not stand. He kept the red pencil in his hand and kept it moving over the scale sheets in front of him, as if the check marks he was making were the reason he was looking up between them. He had seen students read a poster before. He had never seen two students read one the way Nick and Jackie were reading this one — as if the paper might, if looked at long enough, tell them what to do about each other.

"Gentlemen," he said. "I see you've noticed the announcement."

Neither boy moved.

"Band names due Friday. Eight slots."

Jackie spoke first, to the poster. "Queen."

Nick spoke to the poster. "Aerosmith."

Wentworth set the pencil down. "The competition is meant to celebrate —"

"It's about the music," Nick said.

"The music," Jackie said. "Not the story around it."

"Right."

"Just the sound."

"Just what you can do."

Nick turned his head a half-inch. Jackie turned his. For a beat the two of them stood with their faces angled toward each other without quite facing each other, the way two dogs stand before they decide whether they know one another. Wentworth opened the top drawer of his desk.

"Wait," he said. "Hold on a minute."

Under a stack of school-issue staff paper there was a manila envelope that had been in that drawer since September. In the envelope was a photograph. His fingers found it the way they used to find the mouthpiece of the trumpet in the dark. He slid it out and laid it on the blotter and flattened it with the heel of his hand.

Curtis Middle School Talent Show, May 1975. Two boys standing on a gymnasium stage that had been draped in crepe paper. Thirteen years old, one guitar each, matching too-big sport coats borrowed from fathers. Nick's hair a little longer then. Jackie's a little shorter. They had their free arms around each other's shoulders and they were grinning the kind of grin that gets you photographed — a grin that had not yet learned to be careful about anything.

"I was there," Wentworth said. "You boys played 'Jailhouse Rock' and an arrangement of 'Yesterday' that had no business working. It worked."

He turned the photograph toward them.

The music room clock, which lost forty seconds a day, ticked.

Nick's face did something — a small collapse at the jaw, a flicker above the eyes — that neither Wentworth nor Jackie could have put into a word, but Jackie saw it, and Jackie's hand came halfway out of his pocket and stopped and went back in. The fluorescent in the corner buzzed. Down the hall a locker slammed.

Then Nick's eyes came off the photograph and went back to the poster, and Jackie's followed a half-second behind.

"That was middle school," Nick said.

"We're different."

"Yeah."

The bell rang. Thirty seconds from a corridor of bodies.

"May the best band win," Nick said, because the other sentences did not have a way to be said.

"Count on it."

They left through the doors they had come in by. Wentworth sat with the photograph on the blotter in front of him, and did not put it away.

---

He did not put it away for forty minutes.

The first-period kids filed in past him and he took attendance by ear, counting the thumps of trumpet cases on the tile without looking up. The photograph lay where he had set it down, two boys with their arms around each other's shoulders, and beside it, because he had opened the drawer far enough, lay the other objects that had been in it for twenty years.

A dried-out reed in a cardboard slip, last damp in 1963.

A program, folded twice along old creases, from Jordan Hall, March 8, 1958: New England Conservatory Undergraduate Showcase, Robert A. Wentworth, trumpet, Hindemith Sonata, second movement. The paper had gone the color of weak tea at the folds.

A metronome that did not work.

He had not played the Hindemith since the year his second child was born. He had taken the music teacher's job at Lincoln-Sudbury the spring after that, and he had not unpacked the Hindemith from the bottom of the upright case in the hall closet, and he had not, in all the years since, explained to anyone — wife, children, department chair — what had gone out of him between the 8th of March, 1958 and the Tuesday afternoon in September of 1962 when he signed the W-2 for the Lincoln-Sudbury Regional School District. It was a gap a man could live in. He had lived in it.

He looked at the photograph of Nick and Jackie and felt the gap open a half-inch wider.

The call letters on the poster outside his office — WBCN — still carried Maxanne Sartori's signature even though Maxanne had been gone from the station two Aprils now, over at Elektra in A&R. She was the one who had played the Neighborhoods out of Upton when nobody else would. The lettering on the poster was the shape of her, even if her voice wasn't in it anymore. Wentworth had the thought and then put it down, the way he put most thoughts down, and closed the drawer without locking it.

---

By second period the poster was in the library. By third there was one in the cafeteria above the milk coolers, one on the glass of the gym door, one taped — against policy — to the inside of the smokers' corridor, where the light came through dirty windows the color of weak tea and nobody took it down. By fourth, five copies had gone up school-wide, and the Xerox toner on all of them smelled faintly of ozone because the machine in the main office had been run thirty times in an hour.

The old four-way map of Lincoln-Sudbury — Rampers, Rats, Regs, Rollers — had been the only map anyone needed since before any of them started high school. By Monday lunch it had a new seam cutting diagonal across all four. A Ramper and a Roller could sit at the same table for the whole period arguing whether "Dream On" was a better song than "Somebody to Love," and the argument would be the only thing the table talked about.

By lunch someone had drawn a small careful skull in blue Bic next to the word Queen. By seventh period someone else had drawn a longer-fanged skull next to Aerosmith. By the end of the day the margin of the cafeteria poster carried, in black Sharpie, in the handwriting of somebody who had been to Kenmore Square, the words THE RAT, with an arrow pointing at nothing in particular.

The poster by the gym door was read fifty times before sixth period. You could tell by the thumbprints on the lower right corner where kids planted a finger while they leaned in to check the date a second time. The corners of the paper had already begun to soften.

In the hall outside the music room, Patty Lally stood with her books on her hip and read the poster the way she read everything, which was twice. Under her breath, without knowing she was doing it, she was humming a Fleetwood Mac song that had come out two years ago and had not left her.

Mr. Ogden, in sixth-period calculus, wrote the words harmonic and structure in the margin of a problem set and did not immediately remember why. Mrs. Williams in home ec caught herself humming "Dream On" while folding pie dough, stopped, embarrassed, and started again. The janitor, who had been at L-S longer than most of the teachers, fixed the cooling duct in the cafeteria ceiling and said nothing about anything, because he had seen this kind of weather come up before and knew it ran its course either way.

That night, Nick stayed in the garage on Dutton Road past midnight with the tube amp humming and his left hand cramping around the neck of the Les Paul copy and the opening of "Dream On" under his fingers for the fortieth time — the way you grind a thing down until it either gives up its secret or breaks your hand. The concrete was cold through his sneakers. The pegboard on the wall was his father's. He did not stop.

In the music room at L-S, at five-past-six that same evening, Jackie Marsh held the high note in "Somebody to Love" until his throat gave out, then drank from the tap over the utility sink and tried it again. The fluorescent stuttered. His voice, when it came back, was raw at the top. He went again.
