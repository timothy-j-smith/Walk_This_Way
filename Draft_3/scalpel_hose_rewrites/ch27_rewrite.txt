CHAPTER 27: JACKIE'S PERFORMANCE

Backstage at 8:15, Jackie Marsh sat on a metal folding chair with the Martin D-28 across his thighs and tuned the low E by ear.

The guitar was there because it kept his hands busy. He had decided a week ago that he would not carry it out. The D-28 would stay backstage. He would walk on and go to the school's Yamaha, and the school's Yamaha would be the only instrument the 800 of them saw him touch. He had spent a month deciding this. His father had said nothing about it either way, which had been its own kind of pressure.

His fingers knew the D without looking. The A he tuned off the D. The E off the A. He went through the strings the way he had been taught at eight years old, one at a time, patient, the spring in the tuning peg resisting just so before it gave.

Elizabeth stood beside a road case playing G-major scales into her chest, the bow moving, her eyes on nothing, the small pink callus on the left of her neck above the cardigan collar. She played four octaves up and four octaves down and did not stop between. Max Wesslen sat on an Ampeg head with the Precision flat across his lap, plucked the open A, watched the tuner's needle, and moved the peg a sixteenth of a turn. Ace Callahan ran the tip of one stick around the rim of a practice pad he was holding in his left hand, tick tick tick tick, the dry small clock of a drummer's nerves.

From the stage side someone called three minutes and nobody said anything back.

Jackie set the Martin down on its stand. He flexed his left hand open and closed twice. His palms were dry. Cold air in through the nose, warm out through the mouth — his father's trick for the pre-concert body. He had been doing it for eleven years.

"Hey," he said.

The three of them looked up.

"We're playing Queen because we love Queen. That's it."

Ace said, "What if we don't win."

"Then we don't win."

Max said, "A-flat on the second."

"A-flat on the second."

Elizabeth closed the bow against her skirt, tightened the horsehair a quarter turn, and did not say anything.

Someone called one minute. Ace stood up and went through the blacks toward his kit. Max followed with the P-bass. Elizabeth followed with the violin at her side. Jackie did not look at his Martin again. He walked past it.

---

The house was bigger from the stage than it had looked from the audience floor. The lights cleaned his depth and left him a hot white wall — he could see the first three rows as faces, maybe four, and past that the dark went to a charcoal haze where the house rose. He walked to the Yamaha. The grand was half-open on the short stick. He knew this piano. He had played it for the spring recital in March and for the district assembly in April. It was the piano his father had helped the school buy in 1976. He had never thought of it as his before.

He sat. He touched middle C without playing it. The key was cool, and the key-dip was maybe two millimeters deeper than the Steinway upstairs at home, and the hammer action on the Yamaha leaned a hair to the treble side — felted badly, the school's maintenance man filing the hammers once a year with a piece of sandpaper whose grit nobody asked about. The asymmetry was a real thing he could feel with his knuckles before he played a note, and it steadied him because it was a fact, the way a bad floor under a bar stool is a fact, and you sit on it anyway.

He found the front row by its silhouettes. He could not read his father's face in the third row. The lights made Dr. Richard Marsh a shape in a good suit, dark on dark, a seated body under the spill. His hands on his knees or in his lap, impossible to tell. Jackie would not know anything about what his father's face did tonight. That would have to be all right.

Elizabeth raised the violin. Ace clicked the sticks four.

---

"Killer Queen" was a piece of furniture. Mercury had written it in an afternoon in the Trident Studios kitchen in 1974 — Jackie had read that in a Melody Maker interview six months ago and had held onto it — and it came out of the Yamaha clean, the sixteenths in the right hand, the music-box clockwork he had gotten clean in rehearsal twenty times. He let the Yamaha's treble lean work for him and kept the right-hand figures bright and closer to the fallboard where the hammers spoke easier. Max walked the roots under Ace's brushes. Elizabeth threaded the countermelody an octave above where the horn would have lived on the record.

The southwest-section kids made the sound a crowd makes when what it expected is being delivered. A small front-of-chest murmur. Assent. Jackie heard it and did not look up. He hit the last chord and let the pedal hang a breath longer than it needed, and the hall did what a hall does with a grand piano when the pedal hangs — it kept the note, and then it did not. The applause came in as the note went.

---

He did not stand up.

He gave Elizabeth the nod for A-flat.

Max found the root on the E string fifth fret and held it under his palm, not yet plucked. Ace brought the sticks down to brushes. Elizabeth tucked the violin closer. Jackie put his left hand on the A-flat in the second inversion and played the intro chord and sang the first line with his mouth close to the cardioid at the shell of the piano. His voice sat where it should. The floor monitor at his feet had been tilted too far back by somebody in the crew and he had to lean to catch his own return, and he leaned, and that was fine too.

"Somebody to Love" was not "Killer Queen." "Killer Queen" was jewelry. "Somebody to Love" was a church — Aretha's church, Mercury building a gospel piano song in A-flat because the black keys let the voice ride — and Jackie had been alone with it at the Steinway upstairs for three years, at one in the morning, the keyboard lid up and the pedal down and the house asleep. He played it now the way he had played it then. The left hand went into the big octave roll under the verse, the right hand answering itself up the stacked thirds. Ace's brushes turned to sticks at the pre-chorus. Max came in under with a walking line that climbed on the off-beats. Elizabeth did not double the melody. She had asked in rehearsal and he had said no. She played a long bowed descant on the second chorus, up in fifth position, a single line held across eight bars that narrowed the harmonic field to a spotlight and widened it again when she cut out — a piece of musicianship above his skill, and he felt the southwest section go quiet through it and did not understand why until he was most of the way through the bridge, when he realized they were listening.

The climb into the last chorus is the climb into the last chorus. It is the reason people cover this song and the reason most people covering it should not. The voice has to go up and it has to break a little or it is a lie.

He hit the note on *find me somebody to love* and his voice cracked on the second syllable.

He heard it crack.

His left hand did not adjust. The note held anyway.

He came down off the line and into the vamp. Max went up a fifth with him. Ace moved to the ride. Elizabeth's long bowed line came back in from under, and the four of them rode the last twelve bars the way they had ridden them in the basement on Tuesday. On the final cut Jackie lifted the sustain pedal on the downbeat and killed the Yamaha clean, and Ace caught the last hit on the crash and let it ring open, and Elizabeth pulled the bow off the string, and the cymbal decayed alone for four seconds before the room knew it could clap.

---

The hall did not come up all at once. A section near the back started it. Then the front. Then the middle filled in. Not thunder. A wide, uneven sound, a sound of 800 separate decisions. Jackie sat at the bench and his hands were shaking on his thighs and the sweat under his collar had come forward to his neck. His throat was hot from the high note. The A-flat key under his left thumb was still warm from his thumb.

He stood. Elizabeth was already walking off stage-right. Ace had lifted the snare and was carrying it. Max slung the Precision. Jackie followed them through the wing curtain and felt the change in the air where the stage light ended and the backstage light began, a few degrees cooler, and the Martin D-28 was where he had left it on its stand, and he walked past it without looking at it, because it had done its job, which was to be the thing he did not pick up.

**END CHAPTER 27**
