CHAPTER 33A: THE STATION'S FINAL COMPROMISE

August 11, 2009. Seven-fourteen PM. I pull into the WBCN parking lot and kill the engine and sit.

The building looks the same from outside. Squat brick, the kind of architecture that says *functional*, a place where things get made, not displayed. The antenna rises off the roof like a finger pointing at something nobody wants to look at. The logo on the side is still backlit. Someone has already taken the promotional banner down from the front entrance. The glass doors throw the lot back at me — my car, the August sky, the heat-shimmer coming off the asphalt in slow animal waves.

I check my purse. It's there. It's always there.

The cassette sits in the zippered pocket next to a pack of tissues, a lipstick I haven't worn in months, a parking stub from a restaurant I don't remember. Thirty years it's traveled with me. Brookline to New York to LA to Nashville to wherever the corporate music machine needed a woman who could identify talent and keep her mouth shut about what talent was actually worth.

I take it out. Hold it against the windshield. The label has gone the color of weak tea. My block letters blurred by three decades of body heat and pocket friction but still legible. *Demo — Nick/Jackie — un-Authorized — March 1979*. A hairline crack along one edge I noticed in '93 and Scotch-taped and have checked every few months since, the way a nurse checks vitals on a patient who isn't changing.

Under my arm, in a padded mailer I have not opened since I drove out of my own driveway in Cambridge this afternoon, is the reel. Seven-inch, cardboard sleeve, the master Mike cut in Studio C the night Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh stopped being enemies for four minutes and forty-three seconds. The cassette is the talisman. The reel is the weapon. I have carried one for thirty years and the other for thirty years and tonight is the first night they have been in the same room since 1979.

I get out of the car.

---

The lobby is a labyrinth of cardboard.

I stop inside the entrance and let my eyes adjust. The reception desk — where for forty-one years some kid buzzed visitors through, took delivery of promo records, fielded calls from fans and artists and people who thought they were both — is stripped to its laminate shell. Computer gone. Phone gone. The chewed-pen-on-a-string gone. A box labeled RECEPTION — MISC sits with its flaps open, a tangle of phone cords and a chipped WBCN — THE ROCK OF BOSTON mug the movers couldn't be bothered with.

I pick up the mug. The ceramic is cold. I put it back.

The hallway past reception is stacked with boxes two and three high, labeled in black marker in the clinical shorthand of institutional death. STUDIO A — EQUIPMENT. ARCHIVES 1971-1980. PROMO — UNSORTED. AWARDS / PLAQUES. The Gold Records are gone from the walls. They left rectangular ghosts where the frames blocked forty years of nicotine and grime, clean patches on dirty plaster the exact shape of things that used to matter.

My shoes click on the linoleum. The building is quiet in a way I have never heard it. Not the productive quiet of between-shifts, not the focused quiet of a DJ deep in a set, but the quiet of a body that has stopped breathing. The hum is wrong. For forty-one years WBCN had a hum you could feel in the floor, electricity running through the board and signal bleeding up the cable into the tower, a heartbeat you could lean your hand on. I felt it in '73 when I was twenty-seven and running late for a shift in new boots that gave me blisters I didn't notice until April. I felt it in '76 when I pressed my hand to the corridor plaster during a Springsteen broadcast and the wall seemed to move under my fingers. That hum is gone.

Tonight's hum is mechanical. A thin synthesized wash bleeding through the closed door of what used to be Promotions. I stop. Listen. Christian contemporary test tones. Someone is in there running the automation playlist that will take this frequency at 12:01 AM. A synthesized choir rehearsing salvation to an empty room, the new tenant moving in before the old one's body is cold.

I keep walking.

---

The station manager's office is open. Lights on, nobody home.

I shouldn't go in. I've spent thirty years not doing things I should have done, so the small reverse crime of doing something I shouldn't seems about right.

The desk is cleared to three items. A telephone. A desk lamp. A document in a clear plastic sleeve, the kind you use for things that need protecting from coffee rings and casual handling. I read the header through the plastic without picking it up.

ASSET PURCHASE AGREEMENT

Between: WBCN Radio, Inc. (Seller)

And: Educational Media Foundation (Buyer)

I don't need to read further. Everybody knows the terms. The Globe reported them, the Herald reported them, the trades covered it the way medical journals cover a terminal prognosis. WBCN sold to EMF. The operators of K-LOVE and Air1. Effective August 12, 2009, 12:01 AM, the frequency that carried Aerosmith and the Cars and Boston and J. Geils and Springsteen becomes Christian contemporary.

Below the header, in a line I have not seen before, somebody — the station manager, or a lawyer with a human moment — has written in blue ballpoint:

*41 years. Thank you.*

I stand there looking at it longer than I should. Four words, and whoever wrote them didn't even bother with complete sentences. *As if the building could hear.* I almost say it out loud. As if the frequency would remember.

The phone rings. I watch it ring three times, four, five. Nobody here to answer. The phone quits and the office settles back into its quiet, except for the Christian contemporary bleeding down the corridor, the test tones louder now, confident, a synthesized choir finding its pitch.

I leave the document where it is. I leave the mug where it is. I leave the lamp on.

---

STUDIO C, the door says, in stenciled letters older than anything the new tenant is planning to do with this frequency. The door is open. Four boxes are stacked in the hall beside it, labeled STUDIO C — EQUIPMENT in the same black marker that labeled everything else, as if the movers couldn't be bothered to distinguish a room from a room.

I have not been inside this room since May 16, 1979. Three twenty-five AM, give or take. I left it with a cardboard-sleeved reel under my arm and Grace Johnson idling in a station wagon in the lot and the two kids who made the tape standing in the doorway where I am standing now.

I stop. I do not go in.

Nine by twelve. I measured it once. Nine by twelve feet exactly, the smaller of the two demo studios in 1979 and a storage closet since the mid-nineties, which is what anybody will tell you if you ask. Nobody will tell you it was anything else.

The acoustic panels are piled in the far corner. Someone has unscrewed them from the walls for the move-out and stacked them the way you'd stack old mattresses, the quilted fabric going grey-brown where thirty years of dust have settled into the weave. On the walls they left behind is the original 1968 plaster, the color of old butter, and on the plaster are the clean shadow-rectangles where each panel used to sit. Clean patches on dirty plaster, the shapes of things that used to matter.

The Yamaha U3 is gone. Sold at auction in '91 or '92 — an engineer told me once at a wrap party, offhand, the way you mention the weather. On the linoleum where it sat there are four small rectangular marks in the floor, one at each leg, pressed into the tile by the piano's own weight over twenty-three years. Four small rectangles in a pattern I could draw in my sleep.

I look at them.

I do not cry. I do not make a sound. I close my eyes for two beats and open them, and the room stays what it is — a storage closet with its storage stripped, a piano-shaped ghost on the floor, a folding chair nobody put away.

The chair sits facing the doorway. Aluminum, the kind the custodian used to drag in when a third person came to a session. Somebody took a break here and did not come back for it.

The boom stands are gone. The DI box off the floor is gone. The control-room glass past the panels is still there, smudged, the control room beyond it stripped to bare conduit.

For three seconds I let myself have it. The D minor chord Jackie's hands found on the Yamaha at two-fourteen AM, thirty years and seventeen weeks ago. Just the one chord. Not the whole song. My memory has always let me have it and has never let me have more.

A janitor passes me in the hall with a mop bucket, younger than my daughter would have been if I'd had one, the casters squeaking on the linoleum. "You doing okay, ma'am." He does not stop.

"Fine. Thank you."

He does not stop.

I stand another beat in the doorway. One step and I am across the sill. I do not take it. Crossing the sill tonight would break something I am not prepared to break. The broadcast is still to come.

I keep walking.

---

Studio B.

I push through the door and here it is. My coffin, my cathedral, my confessional. The room where I played *Dream On* for the first time. The room where Springsteen walked in wet from a Storrow Drive rainstorm and leaned into the mic and said HI MOM.

The equipment is still here. The Wheatstone board glows its familiar amber, more solder than original circuitry now. The turntables sit dusty on their platforms, needles pulled and probably packed in one of the boxes in the hall. The cart machines are empty except for one slot. Someone has left the final song.

I walk to the cart machine. A single cartridge, strip of masking tape across the top, Sharpie in someone's tidy block letters:

SHINE ON YOU CRAZY DIAMOND — PINK FLOYD — FINAL TRACK — DO NOT REMOVE

I pick it up.

The cart is warm from the machine's standby heat. I turn it over in my hand. Twenty-six minutes of Pink Floyd. The whole nine-part suite. *Wish You Were Here*, 1975. I remember the album came in to the station in a shrink wrap that smelled like the pressing plant, and I played "Shine On" in full on a Sunday night in September at 11 PM and got six phone calls during the fade and one of them was a man crying and he could not say why.

The song is for Syd Barrett. Everyone who knows Pink Floyd knows that. The founder. The crazy diamond. The kid from Cambridge with the kaleidoscope eyes who built the band and then burned out inside it, acid and schizophrenia and whatever the exact mixture was, and the remaining four wrote an entire album trying to explain to themselves why he wasn't in the room anymore. Syd died in 2006. Three years ago. In Cambridge, in a modest house, painting and gardening and not speaking to the music press. The tape in my purse is older than his entire time in Pink Floyd. He was in the band for three years. I have carried this cassette for thirty.

Someone in a conference room in North Carolina opened a brochure of Pink Floyd tracks and picked the one that's about Syd Barrett. They had no idea what they were doing. The corporate machine, accidentally honest for the first time in thirty years.

It's the right song. That's the thing I am standing here trying to swallow. It's the right goddamn song.

It's an elegy for the one who couldn't survive the industry. It's an elegy for the one who went first. It's an elegy for Syd, and for Nick — Nick who played Aerosmith covers in a garage full of a dead father's tools and then chose his mother and put the guitar in a case and went to build things with his hands — and for Jackie, who did survive, who went to Juilliard and came out whole-looking, which is a different kind of burning out that doesn't get a suite written for it. And for me. The one who left and called it ambition.

I set the cart down on the console. Gentle, the way you put down a thing you respect.

---

The engineering booth door opens and the kid comes in. Mid-twenties, Bruins hoodie, phone in hand before the door is closed. Same kid from my last shift here. They drew him short straw twice.

"Ms. Sartori. Kevin. I'll be your engineer tonight."

"Kevin. You got any bourbon?"

He blinks. "I've got Red Bull."

"That'll have to do."

He retreats to his booth. Through the glass I watch him settle in and immediately check his phone. Born in '84. He has never lived in a country where a DJ chose the music. He has never felt the electricity of a booth when someone drops a needle on a track nobody has heard and a city leans in.

John Lennon said it late in 1980, a few months before he went. About Bruce. *God help Bruce Springsteen when they decide he's no longer God.* I have watched that prophecy come true in slow motion for thirty years. Not just Bruce. All of them. The machinery that lifts kids up is the same machinery that grinds them down, and the hand at the lever does not change. Tonight the hand at the lever is a kid from North Carolina with a Pink Floyd brochure.

I reach into my bag. There is a flask in there too. Old habit. Tonight it's Heaven Hill — I switched off Pappy a long time ago, once I started paying for my own bottles and not letting the labels do it. Heaven Hill is cheaper and more honest and more me. Pappy was for rooms with A&R guys in them. Heaven Hill is for a Tuesday at the kitchen table.

I find the paper cups in the second drawer where they have always been. Pour two fingers.

I lift the cup a half inch in the direction of the cart machine. *For Syd.* I drink it.

I pour another. *For Dan Johnson.* For Grace's husband who died on a crane cable in 1977 and started all of this. Drink it.

I pour another. I hold it over the console and I don't drink it for a second, because Jackie Marsh is not dead. Jackie is at Berklee teaching music theory. Which is a different kind of not-alive but not one that takes a toast. I drink it anyway. For the Jackie who isn't teaching. For the Jackie who would have played Carnegie and didn't.

I don't pour one for Nick. Nick is in Sudbury and alive and I will not pretend otherwise tonight. I am tired of pretending.

---

I take the demo cassette out of my purse. Set it on the console, to the right of the Wheatstone, about four inches from the Pink Floyd cart. Not a plan. A memorial arrangement. The cart and the cassette side by side, the way you'd set two photographs on a mantel, the way Grace probably set Dan's watch and his wedding ring on the nightstand the first night.

Tonight it's Pink Floyd.

I can say that to myself and mean it. The corporate overlords accidentally wrote the right song. "Shine On You Crazy Diamond" is a real elegy — twenty-six minutes of Gilmour's guitar reaching for a man who wasn't coming back, saxophone in the middle like a funeral procession, Gilmour's voice finally breaking on *you reached for the secret too soon, you cried for the moon*. That's what you play when a rock and roll station dies. Not a demo tape with a hairline crack down the shell. Not two kids from Sudbury nobody knows. Not a private grudge against a labyrinth I helped build.

You play Pink Floyd. You let Gilmour take them out. You go home.

The cassette sits there next to the cart. My talisman. Thirty years of pocket heat, warm through the plastic shell. I run my thumb along the taped crack. I do not pick it up.

---

The ON AIR sign is dark. It lights at eight.

I sit down in the broadcast chair. The leather creaks the same way it creaked in 1974. I put the MDR-7506s around my neck, the old Sonys with the three black rings of electrical tape where the headband cracked in 1998, the only piece of equipment in this room older than my regrets.

Through the glass Kevin's phone lights his face pale blue.

Down the hall the Christian contemporary swells into some kind of chorus. Synthesized voices on a synthesized rising third, hopeful, ascending, praise music designed to lift you without asking you to lift yourself. The future of 104.1, rehearsing five hours early. I press my palm flat against the wall beside the console and feel the faint foreign vibration, the frequency of a building learning to speak a language it was never built for.

The padded mailer is on the console beside the cassette. I haven't opened it. I won't yet.

Forty-six minutes until I open the mic.

I crack my knuckles. One hand, then the other. I lean back in the chair. The console glows its amber the way it has glowed for thirty-five years, little lights going about their small electrical business, waiting for somebody to tell them what to do.

I wait.

**END CHAPTER 33A**
