CHAPTER 21: NICK'S REHEARSAL

The garage bay door stood up to the chest-high stop, lifted halfway for the air — May eighth in Sudbury, sixty-five degrees if it was a degree, the kind of spring night that felt warm only because April had set the bar so low. Crickets had started in the grass by the oil stain. Behind the Cavicchio fence a spring peeper was going in the wet low place, one note at a time, not fast. A Chevy with a bad belt whined down Dutton Road and faded toward the Horse Pond light.

Inside the garage the air was forty watts worse than outside. One bare bulb on a length of exposed Romex swung a finger's width when a kid walked under it. The Marshall practice amp on the milk crate was warm enough to put a palm on. The guitar case was open on the concrete, cables heaped in it like rope in a dinghy.

Nick stood in the middle of it with the twenty-five-dollar acoustic he'd walked out of a pawn shop in Framingham with the summer he was fourteen — four springs ago, Dan still around for one more year, the price Dan had paid because he'd said *don't tell your mother* and then told her at dinner that night himself. The guitar had a crack in the upper bout Nick had packed with wood glue the summer he was fifteen. His fingertips knew every raised grain on the neck.

Stephen Murphy had his Univox Hi-Flyer plugged into a little Kustom 2x12 borrowed off his cousin in Hudson. Univox was the cheap copy of the Mosrite the real players in Boston clubs used, and Stephen had saved for it out of a paper route and three-fifty an hour at the Sunoco on 126. It was the color of a Ford brake light. He'd tuned it four times already.

David Cassel sat on a crate behind a Ludwig four-piece his uncle had handed down, one cracked crash rivetted back together with a brass washer his father had drilled out on the basement bench. Mark Pianka had a Rhodes on loan from Mr. Wentworth — a Stage Piano out of the music-room closet that buzzed on the B below middle C if you leaned on it. Mark had figured out not to lean on it, mostly.

"One more time," Nick said.

"We've done it four times," David said.

"One more."

They ran "Dream On." Stephen's bass locked in under Mark's keyboard figure. David's snare hit was quieter tonight than it would be tomorrow — the neighbors closer at a milk crate's remove than they'd be at the back of an auditorium. Nick sang.

When they finished, Stephen laid the Univox flat on his thigh.

"Nick."

"Yeah."

"Whatever happens tomorrow. We're with you. Not because of your mom at Friendly's. You're the reason any of us started playing."

Mark was wiping Rhodes-sweat off the F key. "Freshman year you were the only one who didn't laugh when I came in with my grandmother's Casio. You said *can you play it.*"

David twirled a stick between his fingers and watched it. "You taught me the grip. Three lunches in a row, the music room."

"So tomorrow," Stephen said, "we play. Screw the rest."

Nick nodded once. He did not trust his voice yet.

"One more," he said, when he did. "Then go."

They ran it one more time. Then the Kustom cut and the Rhodes cut, and David laid the sticks crossed on the snare head the way a carpenter lays a level down, and they were packing up.

At the curb Stephen's father's '74 Nova sat idling with the headlights aimed at the garage. Mark's brother was five minutes out. David lived a quarter mile down Dutton and was walking. The three of them went out under the half-raised bay door with their cases and their cords, and at the end of the driveway Stephen said *Nick,* once, and Nick lifted two fingers off the neck of the acoustic, and Stephen got in the Nova and the Nova pulled away down Dutton and the headlights took the last of themselves with them.

The crickets came back up.

---

Grace stood on the back step in her Friendly's uniform with her hand on the storm-door handle and did not open it.

The laundry line ran from the porch post to the maple in the side yard and the line was empty — she had brought the sheets in at six the way she had brought them in every Thursday for twenty-three years. Across Dutton Road on the Connolly porch the orange cat sat on the railing with its tail hanging over the mailbox side. Up by the mill the Chevy with the bad belt passed again, coming back. The same one. The belt did the same thing.

From the open garage she could hear him finishing out. The kids were gone. It was just the one guitar now.

She was afraid of what he would do if he lost. She was afraid of what he would do if he won. Both fears were the same fear, and both fears had the same hand on the same chrome storm-door handle of the same house she had grown up in at twelve, in a different life, on a step she had been standing on when the first policeman came up the walk in October of '41 to tell her mother about her father. She did not open the door. She stood with her hand on the handle until the chrome warmed. Then she took her hand off and went in.

The storm door shut behind her on its own hinge.

---

In the garage Nick stood alone in the forty-watt yellow.

His left hand hurt. The pads of his fingers had gone past sting to something that was not quite numb. The high E bit him a little every time he landed on it, and he landed on it a lot, because "Dream On" started there and the bite got worse as the night went. His grip on the neck tightened and he made himself open the hand twice and close it.

He bent and picked up a pick off the concrete — Fender medium, the logo worn white down one side where his thumb had been. He turned it over. The other side still had the tortoise pattern readable. He set it on the Marshall next to the one already there and stood up.

The toolbox sat where it had sat for eighteen months, under the 1975 April calendar, red paint chipped at the corners, latch shut. On the Maxwell House lid beside it, the 9/16ths socket. Nick glanced at the latch once. Still shut. He did not go near it.

He sat on the milk crate. He put the acoustic across his lap. He worked his left hand open and shut twice, the way Dan had taught him to do when the tendon got tight — *shake it out, Nicky, don't fight it, shake it out —* and then he laid his fingers on the neck and played the piano intro to "Dream On" on six strings of gut and wire.

On the record there was a piano on the intro — Tyler himself, one finger at a time, the way Nick had read it in Circus Magazine two summers ago on the floor of the garage. Nick played the piano part on the twenty-five-dollar acoustic because there was no piano in the garage and because it was the only way he could play it alone. His right thumb picked the melody low on the neck. His left hand fretted slow. When he came to the bend at the end of the first phrase his fingertip bit and he breathed in through his nose and did not stop.

Outside, the crickets had gone quiet.

He did not notice at first. He was in the third phrase before he noticed. The peeper had stopped too. The Chevy with the belt was gone up the road. The night past the half-opened bay was holding still around what was coming out of the garage.

*You got the gift, Nicky.*

The Battle was eighteen hours away.

He played the passage through, alone, the acoustic bright and thin against the pegboard and the bare joists and the concrete, the way Dan had bent over him one Sunday afternoon in the summer he was fourteen and guided his hand on the neck until the fingering came right. When he stopped the crickets started back, first one, then two, then the whole yard, and the peeper came in behind the Cavicchio fence on its slow cadence, and the bare sixty-watt bulb on the Romex swung the width of a finger because a moth had hit it.

Nick set the guitar down in the case on the tangle of cables. He closed the lid. He did not latch it. He reached up and pulled the bay door down to the concrete with both hands, slow, the way Dan had taught him, easing the springs the last six inches so the counterweight did not slam.

On his way to the side door he did not look at the toolbox.
