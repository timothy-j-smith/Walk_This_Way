CHAPTER 34: THE LAST VOICE OF BOSTON ROCK — PART 2

Eleven-thirty. Half an hour left.

I've been talking for four hours. The battle. The tie. The 3 AM in Studio C. Dan's crane cable in '77. Grace in the third row in her good navy dress. The tie-clause I wrote in the fall of '76 in a pitch meeting before I ever left this building. I have given WBCN the longest uninterrupted monologue in its forty-one years on the rock dial and WBCN has not interrupted me, because WBCN is already gone. Only the signal is left, and the signal is waiting.

The phones have been ringing all night. I have let them ring. The board lights up in constellations, dies, lights up again. Somewhere down the hall a program director has given up and gone home. The kid through the glass — Kevin, Bruins hoodie, twenty-five, raised on Spotify — has put his phone down. He's watching me the way you watch a fire nobody lit on purpose.

Two objects on the console.

On the left, a cart. Brown plastic shell, cream label, Sharpie in somebody else's hand: SHINE ON YOU CRAZY DIAMOND — PINK FLOYD — FINAL TRACK — DO NOT REMOVE. Someone pulled it off the LP sleeve of *Wish You Were Here* this afternoon and loaded it into the NAB machine for me. Corporate's picked ending. Cued. Ready. All I have to do is punch it.

On the right, a seven-inch open-reel in a cardboard sleeve. Mike's handwriting, faded to the color of weak tea: *Nick/Jackie — un-Authorized — March 1979*. The sleeve is soft at the edges from thirty years of hands. The reel's been in my Cambridge study in a locked drawer the first of every month for three decades. I check it. I don't play it. I know what's on it. I carry the cassette of it in my purse the way a soldier carries a bullet that missed him — not as a souvenir, as proof.

The cassette sits on the console between the cart and the reel. Talisman. Witness. Unused tonight.

The ON AIR sign holds its red. Twenty-eight minutes until it doesn't.

WBCN signed on in 1968. I was twenty-four when I got here, in 1970. I'm sixty-three now. The subtraction is not simple.

---

I lean into the pop filter.

"I want to talk to you about Pink Floyd," I say. "Specifically about 'Shine On You Crazy Diamond,' which is on the cart machine in front of me, which somebody in corporate in North Carolina decided three weeks ago was how this station should end. I want to tell you why they're right."

The phones go off again. I don't look.

"The song is from *Wish You Were Here*. 1975. Gilmour wrote the opening four-note guitar phrase and when Roger Waters heard it he stopped and said, this is him. He meant Syd Barrett. Syd was Pink Floyd's founder. He named the band — took two old Carolina bluesmen, Pink Anderson and Floyd Council, and made them one word. Everything Pink Floyd became was built on top of Syd, and by the time they sat down to make *Wish You Were Here* he was already gone. Not dead. Gone. Acid, mostly. A head that couldn't come back from where he'd taken it. He was twenty-four."

I pick up the cart. Weigh it in my palm. The plastic is warm from the lamp.

"They recorded the record and one afternoon a fat bald man in a raincoat wandered into the control room at Abbey Road, and for ten minutes the band didn't recognize him. It was Syd. He listened to the song they'd written about him and he smiled politely and he went home. He died in 2006. Three years ago, July. He was sixty."

My voice is bourbon-low. Heaven Hill paper cup on the console, gone soft at the rim.

"'Shine On You Crazy Diamond' is about burning so bright at the beginning that the rest of your life is a long dim afterward. It's about the one who couldn't make the compromise and didn't survive not making it. It's about a band that built a cathedral on the bones of a friend they couldn't save. It's a nine-part suite. It opens a record and it closes a record. You can't put it on in the car. You have to sit down for it."

Kevin through the glass has his hand on the fader. Waiting for my cue.

"Corporate picked this song because it sounds like grief without committing to any. Because it's long enough to run out the clock. Because the Christian broadcasting people taking this frequency at one minute past midnight needed something that would end the old story quietly so the new story could begin. And I will say this, because I have to say it on the air before I do anything else tonight — corporate got it right. Accidentally. For the first time in thirty years of corporate getting everything wrong, somebody in North Carolina picked the correct song for a rock station going dark. Syd Barrett deserves it. Pink Floyd deserves it. The people who spent four decades listening to this frequency deserve it. 'Shine On' is the elegy. I mean that. I'm ready to play it."

I am. I mean it all the way down to the hand on the cart.

The VU meters idle. The reel to my right waits in its sleeve. The ON AIR holds red.

---

And then — nothing dramatic, just a small thing in the chest — I look at the reel.

Syd Barrett has Pink Floyd. Syd Barrett has a cathedral and a nine-part suite and a record that sold six million copies and a fat bald afternoon at Abbey Road that's been told so many times it's its own myth. Syd Barrett is fine. Syd Barrett does not need my airtime.

Nick Johnson has four minutes and forty-three seconds in a drawer in my house.

"Sorry, Syd," I say into the mic.

I set the cart down. I pick up the reel.

My hands know this motion. They've known it since I was twenty-four. The reel fits the spindle like a key in a lock that was cut for it. I thread the tape across the capstan, past the heads, onto the take-up. The leader is the color of old butter.

---

"Okay," I say. "New plan. I'll get in trouble for this. It doesn't matter who with.

"May 15, 1979. Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School auditorium. Battle of the Bands, WBCN-sponsored, four hundred and forty-three seats, every seat full. Two bands. The Crown Jewels — a kid named Jackie Marsh on the school's Yamaha grand, and three others. Walking the Line — a kid named Nick Johnson on a borrowed Telecaster, and three others. These two boys had grown up on the same brook, played on the same mini-bike trails, traded baseball cards in the same middle school cafeteria, and by the spring of '79 they weren't speaking. The town had done its work. Class did its work. A crane cable in November of '77 did its work. They tied. I made sure they could. I wrote the tie-breaker clause in the fall of '76 in a pitch meeting at this station with Tom Andrews, and I left the trap baited for three years before it closed.

"They played that tie for the room. Then at 2 AM Grace Johnson drove her son, and somebody's mother or father drove Jackie, to this building. I'd pulled a session engineer out of bed. Studio C. Two microphones four feet apart. No amp. Nick through a DI box, Jackie on a Yamaha upright. One take. No overdubs. 'Sweet Emotion' folded into 'Bohemian Rhapsody,' Aerosmith bleeding into Queen, a bar of 'Walk This Way' strutting through the middle just to see if it could. Four minutes and forty-three seconds. A stumble at the twelfth fret that Jackie caught on a crushed piano note and turned into the best sound in the recording.

"Then they shook hands in the hallway and went home to the people who'd already chosen them. Nick to the Army. Jackie to Juilliard. Me back to the label. I put the reel in a drawer. I've checked it the first of every month since Reagan. I have never played it. I am going to play it now. For you. Full volume. The way I used to, before I learned to ask permission."

The kid behind the glass has taken his hand off the fader. Something has happened to his face I will think about later.

"This is how rock was supposed to crash the silence," I say. "One time only. Stay with me."

I press play.

---

The VU meters slam red. My signature. The engineers used to hate it. The listeners never did.

D minor. Jackie's Yamaha. The chord fills the booth, fills the building, fills 104.1 FM from the Cape to the New Hampshire border — fifty thousand watts, for the last time tonight. The chord hangs in the air like a held breath the room has been waiting for since 1968.

Then Nick's guitar enters. Sweet Emotion stripped down to its bones. Clean, exposed, a boy playing at 2 AM in a room where anger did not help and he'd finally found that out. I've heard this tape two hundred and forty times in my head. I have never heard it out loud. The speakers give it dimensions my memory couldn't — the hum of the Studio C walls, the breath before a phrase, the slight hiss of Mylar wound on a plastic spool that cost forty cents wholesale in '79.

The two songs find each other. Jackie's left hand doubles Nick's riff an octave high, then lets go, and the Queen melody rises up out of the piano the way a river carries a leaf. The stumble at the twelfth fret. The crushed note. The crack that lets the light in. The hard rock section — both of them digging in, thirty seconds of controlled fury, the kind of fury that is actually tenderness with nowhere else to go. Two beats of silence.

The final chord. D minor. Sustain pedal down. The note rings and rings and fades.

Four minutes and forty-three seconds. The reel turns on into leader. I stop the machine. The spindle clicks.

The booth is quiet in a way no booth has ever been quiet in this building.

---

I lean into the mic one more time.

"This is Maxanne Sartori, signing off."

A beat. The clock on the wall reads 11:59.

"The music was real. Goodnight, Boston."

The ON AIR sign dies at midnight. Not a second off. Forty-one years of red light and then no red light. The booth drops to emergency fluorescent, the color of old newsprint, and the absence of the red is louder than anything I ever played through this microphone.

Transmitter silence. Not quiet. Empty. The space where something used to be.

Twelve-oh-one. Static, then smooth. A man's voice mixed too hot, processed through reverb, the audio equivalent of a smile with nothing behind it.

*You're listening to JOY FM, bringing you the good news in music and ministry.*

A synthesizer choir fills the booth where Studio B used to live. I laugh once, sharp, half a sob, and the acoustic foam eats it.

---

Kevin opens the booth door. He stands in it. He does not speak. I understand we are not going to speak.

I leave the reel on the playback machine. Someone from JOY FM can inherit it with the rest of the ghosts. I pick up the cassette off the console and put it back in my purse where it has lived. I leave the Pink Floyd cart where it sits, honor guard for a suite that did not get its airtime.

The hallway's moving boxes have not moved. The lobby is darker than when I came in. Outside, August in Boston, asphalt and harbor, the antenna tower dark against the sky for the first time in forty-one years.

My Volvo wagon is the only car in the lot. Twelve years old. I get in and close the door and the silence wraps around me the way Hop Brook wraps around a stone.

My phone buzzes.

A text from Sophie. My niece. Nineteen. Emerson. Thinks radio is what old people did before podcasts.

*Aunt Max did you play that song?? Someone recorded it someone put it on YouTube.*

A second text, already landing.

*It is BLOWING UP.*

I open the laptop on the passenger seat. The WBCN WiFi still works because nobody's thought to kill it yet. YouTube loads in the blue glow.

TWO GUYS WHO SHOULD HAVE BEEN FAMOUS — WBCN FINAL BROADCAST. Posted fourteen minutes ago by BostonRockRIP. Thumbnail of the antenna tower in Impact font.

50,000 views.

I refresh. 78,000.

The comments scroll past the way the request line used to scroll at eleven on a Friday night.

*1979??? This sounds like it could be today.*

*Who are these kids and how do I find them.*

*Sitting in my car in the parking lot of a CVS in Quincy crying.*

*Aerosmith plus Queen = this. How is this not a record.*

*Whoever played this on her last broadcast is a legend.*

*The Neighborhoods opened for the Cars at the Rat in '79 and these guys never even made the card. This is criminal.*

*RIP WBCN. Thank you Maxanne.*

I refresh. 140,000. 212,000. Twelve-thirty, three hundred thousand. The number climbs like something alive.

My phone rings. 212. New York. I don't answer. It rings again. 617. I don't answer. 310. Los Angeles. The calls stack up the way planes stack over Logan in weather. A voicemail from a producer at NPR. A voicemail from a writer at Pitchfork. A text from Carter Alan: *Maxanne what did you do.* A text from a number I don't know: *do you know where they are.*

One million views at 1:14 AM.

I am crying. Not sadness, not happiness. Some older thing my face knew before I did.

I close the laptop. The car goes black. I sit there in the Volvo in the empty lot with my hands on the wheel and I let myself think what I have not let myself think for thirty years.

---

Somewhere in a practice room on Mass Ave, on a Wednesday afternoon a month from now, a man in his late forties will be sitting at a Steinway Model B. Not his own Steinway. His office feels wrong today and he has been drifting through practice rooms since noon, the way he has been drifting through them more and more often this year without quite admitting to himself that he is. The teaching day is mostly over. His last two students canceled — one by email, one by not showing up. The room is provisionally his, the way an academic room is provisionally anyone's: forty minutes, then somebody else's. The lid is open on the full stick. He is not playing. He has not played seriously for himself in years. He plays *for* students now — demonstrates voice leading at the keyboard, voices a chord to make a point, closes the lid at the hour.

He is sitting with his hands resting on his thighs. His back is straight. His feet are flat under the pedals. It is the shape his body settled into one February afternoon in a practice room on West 66th Street thirty years ago, and it is a shape his body has been returning to more and more these last years, Wednesdays like this one, hands quiet on the wool of his slacks, the piano warm and unplayed in front of him. He has not yet noticed. He will notice when he is seventy, maybe, if he gets to seventy.

Someone will come to the door with a laptop. A colleague, maybe, or a kid from the theory class — somebody young enough to have found it first. They will say, you have to hear this. He will not move at first. He will listen all the way through, four minutes and forty-three seconds, with his hands in his lap and his eyes closed. And when it ends his right hand will lift from his thigh. It will not lift because he lifts it. It will lift the way a hand lifts from a table when someone calls you from the next room and you are already rising before you have decided. It will move across the air over the keys and come down on the D above middle C without pressing — the pad of the finger resting on the ivory, the key not moving, the string not sounding. A beat. Two beats. Then his left hand will come up to meet it. The weight will come down. The D minor chord will sound, just the chord, just once — and he will nod, very slightly, the way people nod when they finally hear somebody say out loud the thing they had decided they were alone with.

I don't know him. I have never met him. I am making him up in a Volvo in an empty parking lot at one-twenty in the morning because I need to. He might be out there. He might not. The imagining is what I have.

I turn the key. The Volvo catches on the second try. I drive out onto the Pike heading west, toward Cambridge, toward home, and I leave the radio off.

**END CHAPTER 34**
