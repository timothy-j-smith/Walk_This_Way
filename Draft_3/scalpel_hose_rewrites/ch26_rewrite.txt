CHAPTER 26: THE BATTLE BEGINS

May 15, 1979. Seven o'clock on a Tuesday night. The Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School auditorium had been built in 1958 and smelled like it still — wood floor wax, the slow mineral breath of cast iron, four hundred and forty-three folding seats bolted into a concrete rake that groaned as kids sat down and groaned again when kids stood back up to wave at other kids. The fluorescent cove lights above the proscenium threw the whole room into the color of a grocery store. Behind the stage hung the painted backdrop every school play had used for a decade: a Concord Road maple tree in October, the red gone brown at the edges where the scrim had rolled. In the lobby the JV booster club had set up a popcorn machine and the smell of commercial butter and Aqua Velva and girls' perfume had come in with the crowd and was now layered, impossibly, on top of the baseline adolescent sweat of an April-warm room in May. The balcony was already full. Seniors.

Grace Johnson took a seat in the third row on the left. She had left Friendly's at four, changed at home into the good dress — the navy one with the buttons, the dress she'd worn to Dan's funeral and would not wear again after tonight — and brought her winter coat folded over one arm because she had nothing else to carry. The programs she'd picked up at the door were folded once in her lap. Her purse sat on top. She put her hands on top of the purse and then on top of the programs and then back on top of the purse.

Two seats to her right, Mary Murphy from the old Saint Michael's Tuesday group was settling in without looking at her. Mary had come. Mary did not say anything.

Three rows behind Grace, Dr. Richard Marsh took his seat on the aisle. Gray suit, pressed shirt, no tie loosened. His hands rested flat on his knees, palms down, thumbs in line with the seam of his trousers. He sat the way he sat at department meetings.

On the stage, the judges' table was draped in a black cloth, four folding chairs behind it. Maxanne Sartori had flown up from the Elektra office at noon and had come to the high school straight from the Holiday Inn in Natick — leather jacket over a plain shirt, jeans, boots, a styrofoam cup of WBCN coffee in front of her with the red logo still on it. A clipboard. A pencil. The pint of Heaven Hill in her bag for later was nobody's business. Beside her Carter Alan was twenty-six and looked nineteen, earnest as a Boy Scout, taking his own notes. Bob Walker from Strawberries had thick forearms laid across a second clipboard as if selecting the draft. Chuck Morrison, WBCN morning MC, stood at the lip of the stage with the mic already hot.

He tapped it once. The tap came back through the house PA like a door closing.

"Good evening, Lincoln-Sudbury." A good-natured roar. "First ever WBCN Battle of the Bands, brought to you by the Rock of Boston and by Strawberries Records and Tapes, where for seven ninety-nine you can take home an album that will change your life or your sister's." He let the laugh build and cut it off. "Eight bands. Five hundred dollars. Recording time on Boylston Street. Let's go."

He introduced Maxanne and got the warmest hand of the night because half the room had heard her nights on 104.1 before she'd gone to Elektra. Carter got applause. Bob Walker got the loudest — studio time, album contract, the kind of future a Sudbury kid could carry out of a gymnasium. Chuck held up the lineup card.

"The Jesters. Detroit Rock City. The Dead Heads. Talking Heads Talking. Walking the Line. Crown Jewels. Rumours. The Regulators. In that order. God help us."

He read the first four again and waved the first four on.

The Jesters were Lincoln freshmen too young to be self-conscious. They opened with "Just What I Needed" and the bass player missed the turnaround twice and they played it sweet anyway; the room clapped because it was a start. Detroit Rock City came on in greasepaint and platform boots from Acton-Boxborough, a blood packet gone off early in the first verse, a fire-breather reduced to steam, and the kids in the first five rows loved them for trying. The Dead Heads — Wayland, patchouli, Birkenstocks — opened "Truckin'" warm and rolled it toward "Dark Star" and lost the back half of the balcony around minute seven; Chuck flicked the house lights twice and they landed it into applause that was kind and exhausted. Talking Heads Talking from Boston Latin came on in matching thrift-store suits and played "Psycho Killer" with such clinical tightness that the older brothers in the crowd recognized it before the younger kids did, and a whole row of Rats in the back threw their hands up, delighted.

By the end of the fourth band, kids had started to drift — to the lobby for popcorn, to the smoking corner outside the fire doors, to the bathroom in little groups so they could go together. The house mood wasn't electric. It was waiting.

Along the rake, the waiting arranged itself. The quieter kids held the middle. A cluster in leather and denim colonized the back rows with boots on the armrests. Boys with grease still under their nails leaned against the side wall near the shop-corridor door because sitting in the folding seats took the ache out of the wrong part of their backs. Up front, the study-hall kids had the first two rows in neat sections, pens out as if they would be quizzed. Nobody had planned it. It was just where bodies went.

---

Backstage stage-right, Nick Johnson stood at the gap in the heavy velvet curtain where the hem didn't quite reach the floor, and watched the house through the slit.

His guitar hung from the strap Dan had bought him at E.U. Wurlitzer in the fall of 1976, the leather gone dark and soft in the curve where his thumb rode. The strap was not the thing. The thing was the body of the guitar against the front of his ribs, warm from the stage lights even through the curtain, and the pick in the pocket of his jeans where he could feel it with two fingers without looking. His throat was dry the way it had gone dry the first time he'd stood up in English class and lost the shape of a sentence halfway through. He swallowed and it tasted of pennies. That was adrenaline. Stephen had told him that once. The body made a metal when it was afraid.

On the pad of his right thumb a blister had come up late the night before, a little puffed dome at the edge of the nail where the pick had ridden on the third hour of "Sweet Emotion," and he had drained it at midnight with a sewing needle and his mother's rubbing alcohol and wrapped it in a square of white first-aid tape and then peeled the tape off before he left the house because he could not feel the pick through the tape. The blister had opened again somewhere between the parking lot and here. He did not look at it.

Through the curtain gap he found his mother in the third row and then he made himself look away, because it hurt to look at her from behind and not be able to fix anything.

He slid his eye along the stage. At the lip of the grand piano on the far side of the wings, a boy in a white shirt was running his fingers over the keys without sound. Jackie. Nick saw the collar, the posture, the way the shoulder blades sat. He did not see the face. He did not need to. For perhaps a second the two of them looked up at the same time and their eyes caught through the velvet slit and through the length of the stage and the grip of lighting-kid's black tape along the boards, and then one of them — neither could have said which — looked away.

"Walking the Line." The stage manager's whisper, hot and close. "Three minutes."

Stephen Callahan was at Nick's shoulder. Mark had his bass already strapped. David was tightening the high hat for the third time in five minutes. Nick looked at them. They were kids from Lincoln. They were his kids for fifteen minutes.

"Yeah," Nick said. That was all he said.

He put his hand flat on the curtain like you put a hand on a horse's neck before you got up.

---

On the other side of the backstage, by the school's Steinway, Jackie Marsh took his hand off the keyboard and folded his fingers once in his lap to stop them from doing anything else.

Elizabeth was tuning the violin against a pitch pipe without needing to. Ace was up on a crate with a stick in each hand, walking paradiddles on his thigh. Max had squatted behind his bass amp and was watching a meter that didn't need watching. Four kids who had been practicing since they were small enough to need a phone book on the piano bench.

Footsteps on the wing stairs. A measured tread. Jackie knew the tread before he turned.

"Jackie."

Dr. Marsh stood in the curtain of the stage-left wing. He did not come all the way in. He stopped at the Steinway, and his right hand — without Jackie seeing — came down and rested for half a second on the polished wood of the fallboard over the keys, the wood warm from the lights, the hand wide and long-fingered and steady, the hand that had not played since before Jackie was born. He took it off again before he spoke.

"Fine?"

"Fine," Jackie said. "Just nervous."

"Good." Dr. Marsh let the silence do the next thing for him. Then he said, very quiet, "Play it the way you hear it," and he turned, and he went back down the stairs to the house.

Jackie stood where he was for a moment after the footsteps were gone. His father had never said that sentence before. His father did not know he had said it now. Jackie did not look at the fallboard; his father had not touched anything there.

He laid his own hand flat on the wood where the hand had been. It was warm. He took it away.

Out in the house, the Regulators from Framingham were three minutes into a version of "Whole Lotta Love" that had the balcony on its feet already, and the drummer hit the bottom of a fill and the room met him, and Chuck Morrison's voice rolled down from the PA: "Ladies and gentlemen. All the way from Sudbury, Massachusetts. Walking the Line."

---

In the third row, Grace Johnson's hand closed on the folded programs in her lap and did not let go.

Backstage, Nick looked once at Stephen and Mark and David, and then he walked through the gap in the curtain where his hand had been, and the stage lights hit him full in the face, and a stage monitor at the downstage-right edge crackled once, a small hot pop, and the crowd came up off its seats, and the strap took the weight of the guitar, and Nick's left hand found the neck.

**END CHAPTER 26**
