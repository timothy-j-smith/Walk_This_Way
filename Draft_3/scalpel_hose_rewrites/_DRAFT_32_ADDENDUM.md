## Canon change 1 — Dr. Marsh is a pianist, not a guitarist

The prior draft established Dr. Richard Marsh as a man who had given up the guitar for his academic career, a mirror of Dan Johnson (who played guitar secretly in the garage). The author has corrected this: Dr. Marsh is a **former classical pianist** who abandoned performance for psychology. The mirror is sharper this way — Dan hid his guitar in the garage; Dr. Marsh closed the piano lid and walked away — *same regret, different instrument, opposite class expression.*

Consequences:

- Jackie learning piano is his father's abandoned instrument inherited as a projected dream.
- Nick learning guitar is his father's alive gift passed hand to hand.
- Jackie and Nick share *music in their bones* across different instruments. The mirror is at the level of parental sacrifice, not instrument.
- The existing "Dr. Marsh's hand on the Baldwin's middle C" (Ch 22) and "his hand on the Yamaha fallboard backstage" (Ch 26) are PERFECT for a pianist and need no change.
- The existing "Dr. Marsh's fingers closing a chord shape on the wine-glass stem" (Ch 17) can stay — a pianist forms chord shapes too; the three-finger triad reads equally as piano.
- The existing "Dr. Marsh glancing at Nick's callus pattern" (Ch 14) must be reframed. A pianist would NOT know a guitarist's specific fretting callus. But he would recognize a musician's hands — the unconscious committed posture, the callus at all, the small ghost of a wound-G scar. The crack becomes: he sees a kid committed to an instrument and looks away because his own commitment is buried. Still a crack. Not intimate-same-instrument recognition; committed-musician recognition.
- The existing "phantom twelfth fret" pinky flex (Ch 30) must change. A pianist doesn't reach for a fret. Change to a phantom-piano reach — the pinky uncurling once for a low octave his thumb hasn't touched in thirty years, or similar.
- The "gave up the guitar" references in Ch 32 (Jackie's bankbook monologue) become "gave up the piano." Associated verbs change — a piano isn't packed in a case and put in an attic; it was abandoned, the lid closed, the sheet music boxed.

This retcon is being applied to Draft 3.1 **before** Wave 1 begins.

## Canon change 2 — Grace's father, the scandal, the suicide

New backstory (subtle, not heavy-handed — the author's directive holds):

- **Arthur Halloran** (Grace's father — placeholder name; author may rename). Sudbury-based financier / broker. Big house on Dutton Road. "The wealthy influential guy in town."
- **Fall 1953:** Halloran is caught in a **Boston-area securities scandal** (broker fraud, commingling client funds, possibly a land-deal Ponzi — the precise legal mechanism is deliberately vague; fraud of the era). Federal indictment lands. Twenty-two-month sentence looming.
- **Late 1953 (late fall):** Halloran commits suicide at home to avoid prison and disgrace. Sudbury gossip is immediate and unkind.
- **Grace is 16, a junior at Lincoln-Sudbury in the class of 1955.** She witnesses the full collapse: the big house sold, the family moved into a rented duplex near Route 20, her social standing dropped from Ramper-tier to Reg overnight. Friends she'd known since first grade treat her differently. Some stay loyal. Most don't.
- Grace finishes her junior and senior years in the working-class geography of the town. This is where she first sees Friendly's from the *behind the counter* side of the class line — though she doesn't start working there until '56.
- **1955:** Graduates L-S.
- **1956:** Marries Dan Johnson (ironworker, working-class, steady, quiet). Takes the Friendly's waitress job at 19. Dan and Grace settle in.
- **1956–~1975:** Halloran's widow (Grace's mother) dies sometime in the late 1960s of stroke or illness — the family home has by now been sold, repurchased, or remained in a sibling's hands; the author can dramatize or leave implicit.
- **By 1977:** Dan and Grace are established Sudbury working-class. They have reclaimed (or rented, or inherited) the old Halloran house on Dutton Road. Per Ch 12 canon: "*this had been her parents' house and the world had been a different shape.*" This is now load-bearing — the house she lives in is her childhood home, the place where her father died.

### Author directive on Grace

> Grace and Dan are that Sudbury couple that never leaves town. They grew up in Sudbury and never left. Many real Sudbury lifers fit this description.
> The backstory should mostly NOT be shown. It's context. The reader feels it in Grace, doesn't learn it in a flashback.

Rendering rules:

- **No flashback scene** to 1953. Not a single sentence of direct dramatization.
- **Subtle surface hints only.** The chrome-handle flash in Ch 19 (existing) is perfect. The library-varnish-she-knew-as-a-girl in Ch 18 (existing) is perfect. The banister worn thin *under her father's hand and her own* in Ch 18 (existing) is perfect. Wave 2 may deepen these by exactly ONE concrete image — a framed family photograph that isn't on a wall anymore, a particular recipe card from her mother in a kitchen drawer, a specific wedding-china plate. One image. No paragraph. No exposition.
- Grace does **not** speak about her father. Not to Nick, not to Mary Murphy, not to anyone. His name may appear once in the full novel, or not at all.
- Dan knew the story but didn't press. He married her after, not before, knowing.
- One consequence the text can show: Grace's wariness around wealth. She never covets it. She's been on both sides.

### Fix required in Ch 21 rewrite

The Ch 21 rewrite currently contains the line:

> "the same chrome storm-door handle of the same house she had grown up in at twelve, in a different life, on a step she had been standing on when the first policeman came up the walk in October of '41 to tell her mother about her father."

This is wrong. Grace was four in October 1941 and her father's death was in 1953. **Fix to:**

(Or similar — keep the rhythm but correct the date and the specific authority. "Policeman" becomes "men from the DA's office" because it's financial crime, not ordinary crime. If the author prefers "policeman," use one patrol officer accompanying the DA men.)

## Canon change 3 — Jackie 2009: pre-depressive decline

Per the author, Jackie in 2009 is:

- Divorced.
- No kids.
- Teaching music theory at Berklee.
- **Unhappy.** Beginning the slow decline into full depression. Not yet housebound. Not yet broken. But the first internal coordinates of the disease are taking root.

Rendering rules:

- The man at the Berklee Steinway (Ch 34 closing imagination) must carry this. When he listens to the tape and plays the D minor chord, he is not a contented academic having a wistful moment. He is a man who has been alone with the wrong version of himself long enough that solitude has become architecture. The chord is not nostalgia; it is a small outward signal that the frequency still reaches him — that he has not gone entirely silent yet.
- Ch 33 Section II (Jackie / Manhattan August 1979) can be retrofitted in Wave 2 with one concrete physical beat that foreshadows this: a moment in the practice room when Debussy is done and Jackie sits still for longer than he should, not practicing, not resting, the piano warm under his hands and his hands not moving.
- **No diagnosis.** No word "depression." No clinical framing. Physical truth only.

This is the part of the book that is *for the real Jackie* — housebound in 2026, depression as his daily weather. The last image must say *you are seen* without saying it.

## Canon change 4 — Friendly's as ironic quadrant melting-pot

Per the author:

- Friendly's on Route 20 is where all four quadrants of Sudbury brush shoulders. The other genuine cross-quadrant space is The Dump on Saturday mornings. Friendly's is The Dump with ice cream.
- The irony of the name ("Friendly's") is the recurring Lehane-note: the town is not always friendly, especially not across quadrant lines. But Friendly's is literally the one restaurant where the Rampers' dad, the Rollers' dad, the Rats' older sister home from college, and the Regs' mom all eat at the counter at the same time on a Saturday.
- Grace working the Friendly's counter for 23 years means she has poured coffee for every adult in Sudbury at least once. She knows everyone's order. Everyone knows her name. None of them know her family story.
- This texture belongs in **Ch 11A** (new cliques chapter) — a scene where the four cliques converge at Friendly's after a school dance or a Saturday, and the social dynamics show. It can also be lightly woven into **Ch 19** (Grace Loses Her Grace).
