CHAPTER 31: THE GARAGE, MIDNIGHT

Stephen Murphy's truck smelled like wet retriever and gear oil. Nick sat in the passenger seat with his window cracked two inches. May air, fifty-something, damp — the kind of late-spring New England night that put fog on the inside of the glass. Sodium lights made the wet asphalt of the Lincoln-Sudbury lot look like a record under a needle.

Grace had taken the wagon home twenty minutes ago. Navy dress, Friday double at Friendly's, exhausted. She'd said *come home when you come home* and touched his elbow and gone.

Stephen was inside helping David load the Ludwig. Nick had said he'd wait. He did not want to walk back through the auditorium. Did not want the handshakes, the *great job* from people who meant it and the *great job* from people who didn't.

A tie. He rolled the word against his teeth. Two hundred and fifty dollars. The '67 wagon's transmission had been slipping in second since March. The money would delay it, not fix it.

He clenched his jaw.

Knuckles on the passenger window.

Nick's hand went to the door lock before he saw who it was. Jackie Marsh stood outside the truck in his father's peacoat, collar turned up, breath ghosting. The coat Dr. Marsh had worn on a hundred Saturday mornings back when they were twelve and the world was simple.

Nick rolled the window down another three inches.

Jackie held up the contract. The paper was already softening at the folds. "We have to decide about the studio time."

"You take your half."

"Doesn't work that way." Jackie turned the contract toward the light. "Says *shared*. Together or forfeit."

"Then we forfeit."

"A hundred hours. Professional studio. Engineer."

"I know what it is."

"Do you know what it's worth?"

Nick looked at him through the gap. Jackie's face was half-lit, half-shadow, split down the middle.

"What do you want me to say?" Nick asked.

"I want you to get out of the truck."

Stephen came out of the side door carrying the kick drum by its lug.

"Stevie. Run us to my house?"

Stephen looked at Jackie, looked at Nick. He was a big kid who did not ask questions you didn't invite. "Yeah. Lemme get the floor tom."

---

Stephen drove them up Route 20 past the Store 24 with the Ludwig rattling under a moving blanket. Nobody spoke. The dash light made Jackie's cheekbone the color of weak tea. At Horse Pond Road the Chevy with the bad belt was out again, and Stephen lifted two fingers off the wheel in recognition.

He turned up Dutton Road. The Johnson house sat where it always sat, white paint going gray on the north side, bay window dark, kitchen light on. Stephen put it in park.

"Call me tomorrow."

"Yeah."

Jackie got out first. Nick followed, carrying his guitar case by the handle Dan had re-riveted the summer he was fifteen. The pickup reversed out of the driveway and took its tail lights down Dutton toward Stephen's own kitchen and his own night.

---

Nick put his shoulder to the bay door.

The springs groaned at eleven forty-five the way they had groaned at eleven forty-five for twenty years, a two-tone complaint that belonged to the house the way a throat-clearing belongs to a man. The door rose. The smell hit them both.

Sawdust and motor oil. WD-40 and cold concrete. Dan Johnson's workshop, preserved past the point where anyone had decided to preserve it. The tools hung on pegboard in the Sharpie outlines Grace had drawn so Dan would put them back right — a system Dan had ignored and Grace had enforced, each outline a ghost of the tool it held.

The bare sixty-watt bulb on exposed Romex. The 1975 April Aerosmith calendar above the workbench, frozen on Joe Perry in a white scarf. On the Maxwell House lid beside it, Dan's 9/16ths socket, set there the week after the funeral and not moved since.

The red toolbox sat under the calendar. Latch shut. Paint chipped at the corners. Nick did not look at it.

Jackie stopped in the doorway.

He hadn't been here since the summer before eighth grade. The Brubeck summer. Three weeks on "Take Five" because Jackie said the time signature was impossible and Nick said impossible was just a word for things that hadn't been done yet. Dan had brought them lemonade in Mason jars with too much sugar and sat on the workbench with his boots swinging, listening like a man watching a magic trick he could not explain.

"It's the same," Jackie said.

"Yeah." Nick plugged in the amplifier. The tubes warmed to a low hum that settled in under the bulb's own hum a whole tone lower. "Sit down or go home."

Jackie sat. He pulled a folding chair to the middle of the floor and looked around for a keyboard, and his eyes went — once, and then away — to the red toolbox under the calendar, and then back.

"Behind the salt," Nick said.

Leaning against the workbench, behind a bag of Morton's road salt Dan had never put out, was the Wurlitzer 200A Jackie had left there the spring of '76 — spring of the mashup — its power cord coiled on top like a sleeping snake. Jackie pulled it out. Three keys stuck. Middle C had a hairline crack that bent the timbre toward a small broken bell. He pressed it twice, listening.

Nick strapped on the Stratocaster. '72, tobacco sunburst, paper-route money and a loan from a dead man he was still paying back into silence. He hit an E. The walls shivered.

"What do you want to play?" Jackie said.

"'Walk This Way.'"

"Fine."

Nick counted off — one, two, three, four — too fast. Anger tempo. His right hand attacked the riff, all downstrokes, the way Joe Perry never played it, because Joe Perry had groove and Nick had fury. The garage filled with sound. Too much. The notes piled up like cars in a chain collision.

Jackie came in on the Wurlitzer too controlled. He flattened the melody into something clean and mathematical, every note accountable, no room for accident or breath.

They crashed into each other at the chorus. Nick pushed faster. Jackie pulled tighter.

Nick stopped. The last chord rang out and died against the concrete.

"That was garbage."

"Your tempo —"

"My tempo was fine. You played it like sheet music."

"You played it like a bar fight."

Six feet of oil-stained concrete between them. The amplifier hummed.

"'Bohemian Rhapsody,'" Jackie said.

"No."

"You picked first."

Nick exhaled through his teeth. "Fine."

Jackie counted it off. Proper tempo. The opening piano chords, his hands moving without instruction, the way a tongue knows its own mouth. He played it too respectfully. Like something he was afraid of breaking.

Nick came in on guitar for the operatic section and overplayed. The notes came out strained and sharp.

It fell apart at the hard rock section. Nick heavy. Jackie theatrical. Two different bands in the same room.

Silence.

"We can't do this," Jackie said.

"No."

"We sound like strangers."

"We *are* strangers."

The amplifier hummed. A car passed on Dutton Road, headlights sweeping across the small window above the workbench and vanishing. Somewhere in the house a faucet ran and stopped.

The side door opened.

Grace stood in the frame, backlit by the kitchen. She had changed out of the dress into the Friendly's sweatshirt she wore in the house, and the white Bradlees sneakers with the new insoles. Her hair was down. Her face was tired the way it was always tired now, a tiredness that went past sleep into something structural.

She looked at Nick. She looked at Jackie. She looked at the Wurlitzer plugged in on the folding chair.

"Your father would've liked that," she said. "Both of you winning."

She went back inside. The door closed with a click.

---

The silence after was different from the silences before. Those had been walls. This one was a window.

Nick sat on the edge of the workbench. He put the guitar in his lap and muted the strings with the heel of his hand. Jackie stared at the closed door where Grace had been.

"Don't," Nick said.

"He used to bring us lemonade."

"I said —"

"In Mason jars. With too much sugar. He'd sit right where you're sitting."

Nick's throat closed. He swallowed against it. The bench was solid under him, real — Dan had built it from reclaimed oak, sanded it smooth enough to run a palm across. He could feel the grain through his jeans.

"Remember the Brubeck?" Jackie said.

Nick's hands loosened on the strings. A harmonic escaped — thin, bright, gone.

"Yeah."

"'Take Five.' You said the time signature was impossible."

"You said that."

"Did I?"

"You sat right there and said *five-four is impossible, nobody plays in five-four*, and I said *Brubeck does*, and you said *Brubeck is an alien.*"

Something moved in Jackie's face. Not a smile — too much scar tissue for that — but the muscles around his eyes loosened and for a second he was the kid with lemonade in a Mason jar who believed music was the only honest language.

"Want to try?" Jackie said.

"Try what?"

"You know what."

Nick did know. Summer of '76. "Sweet Emotion" into "Bohemian Rhapsody." Three afternoons bridging them — the Aerosmith riff transposed into D minor because D minor was the key center of the Queen ballad section. It should not have worked. It worked.

Nick counted off slower this time. Not angry. Not controlled. Just honest.

His guitar found the "Sweet Emotion" bass line clean, no distortion, letting the notes breathe the way Dan used to say you let wood breathe before you sealed it. Once. Twice. Finding the groove.

Jackie played a chord. Just one. D minor. He let it hang. Then he played it again and added a ninth, and the chord opened up like a door into a room that had been locked for two years.

Nick heard it. His hands responded before his brain could object. Same pattern, different color. The Aerosmith groove sat inside the Queen harmony and the two songs began to breathe the same air.

Jackie added the melody. Tentative. *Is this the real life* over the bass groove of "Sweet Emotion." Nick looked at him once — Jackie had his eyes closed, his right foot tapping quarter notes on the concrete, the heel of his loafer catching grit — and looked back at the fretboard.

The Stratocaster started to sing the way it only sang when Nick forgot to be angry, when his hands stopped fighting the instrument and started talking to it. Fragments of "Walk This Way" crept in as texture, as color, not a separate song but a thread in the weave.

The tools vibrated on the pegboard. A screwdriver walked to the edge of its hook and hung there, buzzing. A Phillips-head followed, chattering against the wood. The 9/16ths socket rolled a quarter-inch on the Maxwell House lid and stopped. The garage breathed — old wood expanding at a frequency neither instrument was playing, concrete humming under their feet, the bare joists overhead picking up a sympathetic drone that was not in either instrument but was somehow in both of them, the air itself thick with overtones no single string and no single key had produced alone. The red toolbox did not move. Jackie's eyes went to it once, the paint chipped at the corners, the latch shut, and then back to the broken middle C.

They played for eleven minutes. No structure. No arrangement. Two musicians remembering what their hands had always known and their lives had made them forget.

When they stopped, the silence rang.

Nick's fingers trembled on the fretboard. Adrenaline. Or something older.

"That's what we could have been," he said.

Jackie looked at him. Two years of silence. Two years of choosing sides and crossing streets. "We could still do it."

The phone rang.

Nick stared at the side door. Kitchen phone, audible through the frame Grace had left cracked. Four rings. Five. Grace's footsteps on the linoleum. Her voice, muffled by the wall, a long pause, then again.

The side door opened. Grace, one hand on the frame, the receiver stretched to the end of its curled cord.

"It's for both of you," she said. "The woman from the radio station."

Nick took the phone. Maxanne's voice came through clear and close, the particular urgency of someone who had been listening to something she wasn't supposed to hear.

"Get down to the station. Now."

"It's midnight."

"I know what time it is. Bring whatever you just played."

She hung up. Nick held the dead receiver and looked at Jackie.

"WBCN," he said. "She wants us there now."

Jackie looked at the Wurlitzer. At the tools on the pegboard, still faintly trembling. At the red toolbox that had not moved.

"Okay."

---

Nick pulled the bay door down to the concrete with both hands, slow, easing the springs the last six inches so the counterweight did not slam, the way Dan had taught him. He did not look at the toolbox on the way out.

Grace drove. She did not ask questions. She had put a windbreaker on over the sweatshirt and she had taken her wallet because she was Grace Johnson and she did not leave the house at one in the morning without her wallet. The '67 wagon pulled out of Dutton Road onto Route 20 east and found the center line and stayed there. The speedometer sat at forty-five. The dash light was the color of old butter. Jackie had the Wurlitzer on his lap in the back seat with the cord wrapped around his wrist.

Nobody spoke past the Wayside Inn. The wagon's timing was a half-beat off in third and Grace did not mention it, because mentioning a thing and fixing a thing were in different budgets and always had been.

They came into Boston at twelve fifty-eight AM, the skyline low and lit, the Prudential where Dan had stopped being alive rising thirty-seven stories to the north. Nick looked once at its blinking red beacon and then away. Grace did not look at it at all.
