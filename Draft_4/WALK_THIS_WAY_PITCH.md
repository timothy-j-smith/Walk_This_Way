# Walk This Way — The Pitch

## What Professor K Would Say to the Book Producer

---

In the spring of 1979, a woman named Maxanne Sartori sits in a radio booth with a bottle of bourbon and four hours to confess. WBCN — Boston's legendary rock station, the frequency that broke Aerosmith and gave Springsteen his first airtime — dies at midnight, and Maxanne drew the shift nobody wanted. She's fifty-nine, she's angry, and she's been carrying a cassette tape in her purse for thirty years. On it: the only recording of two seventeen-year-old boys from Sudbury, Massachusetts, playing together — a four-minute demo that proves harmony was possible between Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh, before Maxanne helped destroy it. The tape was never authorized. She never played it. Tonight, she will. But first, she tells the story. Nick grew up three blocks from the town dump, learned guitar from a father who died on a construction site when a crane cable snapped, and played Aerosmith in a garage that still smelled like his dead father's sawdust. Jackie grew up across Hop Brook in a house with a brass plaque by the mailbox that wasn't even his family's name, played Queen on a Steinway his father rented to impress BU colleagues, and hid an electric guitar under his bed like contraband. They met at twelve over belt buckles in a middle school cafeteria, bonded over hating disco, and for three years made music that scared every adult in Sudbury — because when a working-class kid and a professor's son harmonize, it threatens the architecture of a town built on keeping people in their proper lanes.

But America does what America does. Dan Johnson dies. Nick withdraws into grief and the garage becomes a shrine instead of a studio. Jackie's father — a psychiatrist who writes papers about childhood trauma while being blind to his own son's — tightens the leash toward Juilliard. The high school cafeteria sorts them into separate tribes. A Saturday confrontation on a bridge over Hop Brook, where Dr. Marsh calls Nick's friends "those boys," ends whatever was left. By senior year they're strangers. Then WBCN sponsors a Battle of the Bands, and Maxanne — who discovered both boys separately, who saw in each of them something she'd been searching for since she found Aerosmith in a bar in Allston in 1973 — engineers the contest rules to force a tie, believing she can manufacture reconciliation the way she once manufactured discovery. She can't. The battle becomes a proxy war for class, for family loyalty, for two irreconcilable versions of what it means to be authentic. Nick plays "Dream On" like he's eulogizing his father in public. Jackie plays "Bohemian Rhapsody" like he's escaping a burning building. A girl named Patty Lally — from the pig-farm side of town, the one who smelled like Cavicchio's and stopped apologizing for it freshman year — plays Fleetwood Mac like she's trying to remind everyone in that auditorium what music was for before they turned it into a weapon. The judges tie Nick and Jackie. Maxanne offers them studio time. At 2 AM in WBCN's Studio C, they record one song — a mashup of "Sweet Emotion" and "Bohemian Rhapsody" that proves, in four minutes, that everything they lost was recoverable. Then they walk away from each other. Not because anyone stops them. Because Nick can't abandon Grace, who's worked doubles at Friendly's since the funeral and never once complained. Because Jackie can't waste his father's investment in Juilliard. They choose family over dreams — both choices virtuous, both choices tragic, both choices exactly what the system was designed to produce.

Thirty years later, Maxanne plays the tape at 11:55 PM on WBCN's final broadcast. Full volume. Unauthorized. Her signature move — the same reckless defiance she showed in 1973 when she dropped "Dream On" on the air without permission. At midnight the station goes silent. Christian radio takes the frequency. But at 12:15 AM, her niece texts: someone recorded the broadcast and uploaded it to YouTube. The demo is going viral — fifty thousand views, a hundred thousand, a million. A new generation discovers Nick and Jackie's four-minute proof that harmony existed, even though the two boys who created it couldn't survive the world that produced them. The novel asks the question America keeps refusing to answer: what do we kill when we sort our children by zip code? And it answers: the individuals, always. But the music doesn't die. It waits for the next frequency. *Walk This Way* is a love letter to a dead friend and a bridge to a living one, disguised as a novel about rock and roll, class warfare, and the last night of Boston radio. It's *Mystic River* meets *Almost Famous*, told by a woman who helped break Aerosmith and broke two kids in the process.
