CHAPTER 3


Jackie Marsh woke at 6:47 — three minutes before the alarm, the way he always did. His body kept time the way his father expected: early, precise, prepared.

The bedroom was his mother's design. Dove gray walls. Bookshelves alphabetized, mostly read. A framed photo on the nightstand: Jackie at seven, hands positioned on piano keys, his mother standing behind him with that bright particular smile. The photo had won something — a parenting magazine feature, "Classical Excellence: Nurturing Your Child's Musical Gift." The photographer had made him hold the position for twenty minutes. Jackie remembered his hands going numb and his mother saying "just a little longer, sweetheart" without looking at him, looking at the photographer, making sure the light was right.

On the closet door, his tuxedo hung pressed and ready. Tonight was the recital. Chopin, Bach, Debussy, Rachmaninoff, and the Mozart to close. The program was printed on cream card stock, his name in italic script. His father had approved the selection in September. His teacher had approved it in October. Nobody had asked Jackie what he wanted to play.

Under his bed, in a case his father pretended didn't exist, was the guitar.

He showered, dressed, and went downstairs. Breakfast at the Marsh house ran on a schedule that didn't tolerate deviation. The dining room table was set with cloth napkins and the good china — not because it was a special occasion, but because every morning in this house was presented as one. Orange juice in crystal glasses. Toast cut into triangles. The Globe folded beside Dr. Marsh's plate, business section on top.

Dr. Richard Marsh sat at the head of the table reading about interest rates. He was tall and lean with the posture of a man who'd spent thirty years in front of classrooms and never once leaned against the chalkboard. His hair was gray at the temples, which he liked. He wore reading glasses he didn't need — his vision was fine, but the glasses made him look more like what he wanted people to see. His hands were soft. He'd never built anything with them. But if you watched closely, his right index finger tapped the table in rhythms that had nothing to do with interest rates. Four-four time. Steady. The unconscious habit of someone who'd once played an instrument and never entirely stopped hearing music.

Jackie sat across from him and ate his toast without speaking. Eileen Marsh — his mother — brought coffee to the table with the efficiency of someone who'd long ago traded her own preferences for the smooth operation of the household. She'd been beautiful once in a careless way; now she was beautiful in a careful way, which was different.

"The recital tonight," Dr. Marsh said, still reading. "Maxanne Sartori from WBCN will be attending. I called her personally."

Jackie's toast stopped halfway to his mouth. "You called a rock DJ to a classical recital?"

"She's a music industry professional. She has connections. And your name should be heard by the right people." Dr. Marsh turned a page. "You'll play the program as written. No surprises."

From upstairs: the sound of a violin. Elizabeth — thirteen, a freshman, Jackie's sister — warming up with something by Paganini. The notes came down through the floor like water through a crack, effortless and true. Elizabeth never had to be told to practice. Music came to her the way breathing came to everyone else. Their father didn't hover over her the way he hovered over Jackie, because Elizabeth's talent didn't need managing. It just was.

Jackie envied her with a pain he'd never named.

"No surprises," Jackie said.

Dr. Marsh looked up from the paper. Their eyes met. For a moment, Jackie saw something behind the reading glasses and the interest rates — a flicker of the man his father had been before he became Dr. Marsh. A younger man. A man who'd sold a Gibson to pay for his dissertation. A man who'd made a choice he called practical and spent thirty years listening to his son play the instrument he'd given up, tapping his finger on tables without knowing why.

"Your mother worked very hard on tonight," Dr. Marsh said. The flicker was gone. "Don't embarrass her."

---

By seven o'clock the living room was unrecognizable. Eileen Marsh had spent two days converting it into a concert space — folding chairs in precise rows, the rented Steinway positioned where the afternoon light would have caught it if this were an afternoon recital, which it wasn't. She'd had the piano tuner in that morning. She'd arranged lilies in three vases. The room smelled like furniture polish and flowers and nothing cooking, because dinner would be catered afterward by a woman from Wayland who made quiches for faculty events.

The audience arrived in wool and cashmere. BU department chairs and their wives. Two families from the New England Conservatory prep division who had children Jackie's age and would evaluate his playing the way you'd evaluate a rival's stock portfolio. A couple Jackie didn't recognize who turned out to be scouts from a Tanglewood summer program. His mother greeted each of them by name, took their coats, directed them to the good chairs, poured wine for the drinkers and sparkling water for the ones who were performing sobriety. She moved through the room the way she moved through life — a half-step behind her husband's ambition, making sure nothing wrinkled.

Maxanne Sartori arrived late, which she did on purpose. She took a chair in the back row, accepted a glass of white wine she didn't intend to drink, and crossed her ankles the way the other women had crossed theirs. She'd been in rooms like this. She knew how they worked. She knew these women had met her type before — the media person who didn't own the right shoes — and dismissed her before she'd sat down. That was fine. She wasn't here for them.

Three days ago she'd sat on a paint bucket in a freezing garage and watched a kid play "Dream On" like he was trying to bring his dead father back to life. Now she was sitting on a rented folding chair in a heated living room watching a kid adjust his cufflinks. She wanted to understand how the same town had produced both of them, the same twenty miles from Boston, the same high school, and she couldn't get there yet. So she watched.

Jackie entered the living room at 7:30 in the tuxedo that fit him the way tuxedos fit boys who've been measured for them since confirmation. He crossed to the Steinway, adjusted the bench a quarter inch, placed his hands on the keys, and began.

Chopin's Nocturne in E-flat major. Technically impeccable. Every phrase shaped by a teacher's careful hand, every dynamic exactly where the score demanded it. The audience nodded in the particular rhythm of people hearing what they expected to hear. Dr. Marsh sat in the front row with his hands on his knees. His right index finger tapped against his kneecap — keeping time, unable to stop, the one honest thing about him in that room.

Maxanne watched Jackie's face. He wasn't there. He was behind his own eyes somewhere, executing a program the way a good employee executes a task list. His fingers did the work. His face did nothing.

Bach. Then Debussy. Then Rachmaninoff. Each piece was correct. Each piece was a report filed on time. Maxanne had heard studio session players cut jingles for car dealerships with more feeling.

Between the Rachmaninoff and the closing Mozart, Jackie paused.

The pause went half a beat too long. Dr. Marsh cleared his throat from the front row — a sound Jackie had been hearing his whole life. It meant: stay on program. Don't deviate. You're being watched.

Jackie's hands moved to different keys.

The first notes of "Bohemian Rhapsody" came out of the Steinway.

Forty adults in formal clothes went still. Not the attentive stillness of an engaged audience. The paralysis of people who had paid for Chopin and were receiving Queen.

"Is this the real life?" Jackie sang. His voice had that classical foundation — the breath control, the placement, the training that money buys. But now the training was carrying something it hadn't been hired for. His left hand found bass notes Freddie Mercury never wrote for solo piano. His right hand ran the same precise scales he'd brought to Chopin, but the precision had a heartbeat now. It was serving him instead of the other way around.

Elizabeth, sitting in the second row with a violin across her lap, smiled. Not the polite smile of a girl at a faculty event. A real smile. The kind that meant: there you are.

Eileen Marsh's wine glass stopped halfway to her mouth. Her free hand found her pearls — not clutching, just holding. Needing something to hold.

Dr. Marsh's finger stopped tapping his knee.

Jackie played the full operatic section — Galileo, Figaro, the tempo shifts — on a solo Steinway, in a living room full of academics and their spouses, in a tuxedo that cost more than Nick Johnson's pawn-shop guitar and his amp and his mic and his drummer's entire kit combined. His right foot found the sustain pedal and held it, and the Steinway opened into a sound that room had never contained. The champagne glasses on the buffet table hummed with it.

He played the hard rock section — "So you think you can stone me and spit in my eye" — and his posture changed for the first time all night. His shoulders dropped. His head came forward. He wasn't sitting at a Steinway in his parents' living room anymore. He was somewhere else, somewhere the tuxedo couldn't reach.

Four minutes and fifty-five seconds. The last chord hung in the air, and Jackie's hands stayed on the keys, fingers trembling. Not nerves. Aftermath.

Silence.

Then Dr. Marsh: "That was unexpected."

His voice was controlled. His face was doing something complicated — pride and fury negotiating in real time, neither one winning. Maxanne recognized the look. She'd seen it on music executives who'd just heard something that threatened their business model. The look of a man who'd been right about everything except the thing that mattered most.

Maxanne stood. Not because she'd planned to. Because somebody had to.

"That was rock and roll," she said. "That was your son."

Jackie looked at her. His hands were still on the keys, still shaking. There was color in his face for the first time all evening. He looked like a person. Before this he'd looked like a program.

"You're Maxanne Sartori," he said. "From WBCN."

She walked to the piano and held out a business card. The same one she'd given Nick Johnson three days ago. The same card, the same station, the same offer extended to a kid from the other side of a stream you could jump in July but might as well be the Atlantic.

"Come by the station," she said. "Bring that."

Jackie took the card. He held it with both hands, turning it over like it was a ticket to somewhere he'd been told didn't exist.

"Nobody's ever told me that was real music," he said.

The words were so quiet that only Maxanne heard them. They'd been in Jackie Marsh's chest for seventeen years, pressed flat under programs and recitals and practice schedules and his father's finger tapping on tabletops. They were the truest thing she'd heard all night, and she'd just come from a garage where a kid played Aerosmith like prayer.

She left before the champagne was poured. In the driveway, sitting in the Karmann Ghia, she thought about two boys less than a mile apart. One played in a garage where his dead father's tools still hung in order on the wall and nobody plugged in the space heater. The other played in a living room where everything was polished and heated and arranged and no one could breathe.

She turned the key and drove west on Concord Road, past the Marsh house's perfect lawn and the stone wall that marked the property line and the oak tree that was older than the town. Past Hop Brook where the water was loud with snowmelt, running fast over flat stones. Past the turn for Dutton Road where, if she rolled her window down, she thought she could still hear power chords bleeding through the walls of a garage that held a dead man's dreams and a living boy's.

She drove toward Boston, and she thought: those two should be in a band together. And then she thought: that will never happen. And then she thought: but what if it could?

That was the thought that would ruin everything.
