CHAPTER 3: JACKIE'S WORLD — THE GILDED CAGE


Three days after the garage on Dutton Road, Maxanne drove across Hop Brook to hear the other kid.

Dr. Richard Marsh had called the station himself — didn't use a secretary, which told her something. His voice had that careful pronunciation of a man who'd worked hard to sound like he'd always talked this way. "Jackie trained at the New England Conservatory prep division. He's being considered for early admission to Juilliard."

Maxanne had said yes because something in the father's voice reminded her of Grace Johnson's. Different words, same fear. Grace said don't fill his head with dreams we can't afford. Dr. Marsh said you need to hear what my son can do. Both of them terrified of what music might cost their kid.

The Marsh house on Concord Road looked like it had been built to be photographed. White clapboard, black shutters, lawn still brown but you could tell it would be green and even by April. A brass plaque by the mailbox that said WHITMORE ESTATE, which wasn't the Marshes' name — they'd kept the old owners' sign. That told Maxanne something too.

Inside, the living room had been converted to a concert space. Folding chairs in rows. A rented Steinway grand with its lid propped open, the strings catching afternoon light. Champagne in silver buckets. White wine for the people who thought champagne was trying too hard. The room smelled like furniture polish and lilies and nothing cooking.

The audience arrived in wool and cashmere. BU faculty, prep school parents, a couple of doctors' wives Maxanne recognized from the Globe society pages. They sat with their ankles crossed and their programs held at the correct angle. Three days ago Maxanne had sat on a paint can in a garage that smelled like motor oil. She accepted a glass of white wine and found a chair in the back row.

Dr. Marsh worked the room. Tall, careful posture, handshake firm but not aggressive. He introduced himself as "Richard" to the men and "Dr. Marsh" to the women, which Maxanne filed away without knowing why. His wife Eileen moved behind him, directing coats and complimenting earrings and making sure nobody sat in the front-row seats she'd reserved for the department chairs.

At 7:30 PM, Jackie Marsh entered the living room in a tuxedo that fit him the way tuxedos fit boys who've been measured. Seventeen years old. Dark hair combed back. He moved to the piano with the posture of someone who'd been taught to enter a room, sat down, adjusted the bench by a quarter inch, and placed his hands on the keys.

He began with Chopin's Nocturne in E-flat major.

It was technically flawless. Every phrase shaped, every dynamic exactly where his teacher had placed it. The audience nodded along the way people do when they're hearing what they expected to hear. Dr. Marsh sat in the front row with his hands on his knees, and Maxanne noticed his right index finger tapping against his kneecap — keeping time without knowing it, the unconscious rhythm of a man who'd once played an instrument himself.

Jackie moved through Bach, then Debussy, then Rachmaninoff. Each piece was precise. Each piece was correct. Maxanne had heard session musicians play with more feeling while sight-reading jingles for car commercials.

She watched Jackie's face. He wasn't there. He was somewhere behind his own eyes, executing a program, and the distance between what his fingers were doing and what his face was saying was the saddest thing in the room.

Between the Rachmaninoff and the closing Mozart, Jackie paused. The pause went on a beat too long. Dr. Marsh cleared his throat from the front row. Eileen's hand found her pearls.

Jackie's fingers moved to different keys.

The opening notes of "Bohemian Rhapsody" came out of the Steinway.

"Is this the real life?" he sang. "Is this just fantasy?"

The room went still. Not the good kind of still. The kind where forty adults in formal clothes realize something is happening that wasn't on the program.

His voice was trained — you could hear the classical foundation in his breath control, in the way he placed vowels. But now the training was serving something it hadn't been hired for. His left hand found bass notes that Mercury never wrote for solo piano. His right hand moved up the keyboard with the same precision he'd brought to Chopin, except now the precision had a pulse.

He played the full operatic section. Galileo, Figaro, the tempo shifts. His sister Elizabeth, fourteen and sitting in the second row with a violin on her lap, started to smile. Mrs. Marsh's champagne glass stopped halfway to her mouth. Dr. Marsh's finger stopped tapping his knee.

When Jackie hit the hard rock section — "So you think you can stone me and spit in my eye" — his right foot found the sustain pedal and held it, and the Steinway opened up into something it had never been asked to do in this living room. The sound filled every corner. The champagne glasses hummed.

He played the whole song. Four minutes and fifty-five seconds. When the final chord died, his hands stayed on the keys.

Nobody moved.

Then Dr. Marsh spoke. "That was... unexpected."

Maxanne stood up. "That was rock and roll. That was your son."

Jackie looked at her. His hands were still shaking from what he'd just done — not from nerves but from the effort of hauling something that heavy out of hiding.

"You're Maxanne Sartori," he said. "From WBCN."

"Come by the station." She reached into her bag for a card. The same card she'd given Nick Johnson three days ago in a garage that smelled like sawdust. "Bring whatever it is you just played."

Jackie took the card. He looked at it, then at his father, then at the piano. Dr. Marsh's face was doing something complicated — Maxanne could see him trying to choose between pride and control, between the part of him that recognized what his son had just done and the part of him that knew it wasn't on the program.

"Thank you," Jackie said quietly. Then, to Maxanne and no one else: "Nobody's ever told me that was real music."

She left before the champagne was poured. In the driveway, she sat in the Karmann Ghia and thought about two boys less than a mile apart. One played in a garage full of a dead man's tools. The other played in a living room full of people who applauded with their fingertips. Both of them starving for someone to hear what they were actually saying.

She turned the key and drove back toward Boston, past Hop Brook where the water ran thin and cold over flat stones, past the turn for Dutton Road where she could still hear power chords if she rolled the window down, past the town line where Sudbury ended and the rest of the world began.
