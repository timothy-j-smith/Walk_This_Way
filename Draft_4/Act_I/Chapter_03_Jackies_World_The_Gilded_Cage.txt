CHAPTER 3


Jackie Marsh woke at 6:47. Three minutes before the alarm. His body did this every morning — surfaced just ahead of the clock, like some part of him needed to beat the system even in sleep.

His room was on the second floor of the house on Concord Road, the side that faced east, so the light came in early and landed on the bookshelf first. The bookshelf was alphabetized. Jackie had done it himself at twelve and never undone it. His desk was organized — a lamp, a pencil cup, his Juilliard acceptance letter in its envelope, a framed photograph he'd turned face-down three months ago. The photograph was from the Curtis Middle School talent show, May 1975. Two boys with guitars, arms around each other, grinning. He didn't look at it anymore but he hadn't thrown it away. He wasn't sure what that meant.

Under his bed, in a case his father pretended not to see, was a Fender Telecaster. Not expensive — Jackie had bought it used at Daddy's Junky Music in Cambridge with birthday money his aunt in London had sent. His aunt had seen Queen at Hammersmith Odeon and sent him the gold-script QUEEN belt buckle that he'd worn every day of seventh grade and now kept in his sock drawer like a medal from a war he'd lost.

He showered and dressed. Khakis, button-down, loafers. Not because he wanted to but because the house required it. The Marsh house on Concord Road didn't have a dress code the way a school has a dress code — written down, posted on a wall. It was understood. You dressed the way people dressed in houses where the living room had a Steinway and the bookshelves held first editions and the mailbox had a brass plaque that said WHITMORE ESTATE, which wasn't even the Marshes' name. The previous owners had put it up. Dr. Marsh left it there. That told you everything.

Downstairs, the dining room. The table seated twelve but held four: Dr. Richard Marsh at the head, Eileen Marsh at the far end, Jackie on one side, the empty chair where Elizabeth would sit when she came down, on the other. The table was set with cloth napkins and the china. Not the good china — the everyday china, which in this house was still better than what most families in Sudbury used for Thanksgiving. Orange juice in crystal glasses. Toast cut into triangles. The Globe folded beside Dr. Marsh's plate, business section facing up.

Dr. Richard Marsh was fifty-one years old, a psychiatrist at Boston University who specialized in trauma and published papers about what happens to children when the adults around them fail. He was tall, lean, silver at the temples. He wore reading glasses he didn't need — his vision was twenty-twenty, but the glasses completed the picture he'd spent thirty years building of himself. He sat with the posture of a man who'd never leaned against anything in his life. His hands were clean and soft. His nails were trimmed. And if you watched his right hand while he read the newspaper, you'd see something he didn't know he was doing: his index finger tapping the table. Tapping in rhythm. Four-four time, steady, the muscle memory of a man who'd once played guitar and sold it to pay for his dissertation and told himself it was the practical choice and spent the next twenty-five years tapping tables without knowing why.

Jackie sat down. Poured juice. Ate toast without speaking.

"Your mother tells me you've been practicing late again," Dr. Marsh said, not looking up from the Globe. "After eleven."

"I'll keep it down."

"The neighbors can hear the piano through the walls, Jackie."

"I wasn't playing piano."

A pause. The kind of pause that happens in houses where certain truths are managed through silence.

"The guitar," Dr. Marsh said. Not a question.

"Yes."

Dr. Marsh turned a page. "Juilliard doesn't have a rock program."

"I know."

"The Tanglewood faculty review your application materials in June. They'll expect the Chopin. The Bach. The Rachmaninoff. Those are the pieces that got you accepted."

"I know, Dad."

"Then I trust we understand each other."

Jackie ate another triangle of toast. Through the dining room window he could see the backyard — half an acre of lawn that would be green and perfect by May, edged with hydrangeas Eileen maintained with the focus of a battlefield surgeon. Beyond the lawn, past the stone wall that marked their property line, the land sloped down to a tree line. And beyond the trees, if you walked far enough, you'd reach Hop Brook.

He'd walked that route a thousand times as a kid. Through the trees, down the slope, across the shallow water — you could jump it in summer, wade it in spring — and up the other side into a different Sudbury. A Sudbury of smaller houses on smaller lots, of cars with rust on the quarter panels, of basketball hoops with no nets. If you kept walking north, you'd hit Dutton Road. Three blocks past the turn for the Dump, you'd find a house with peeling mustard paint and a detached garage where, even now, at seven-thirty in the morning, you might hear the sound of an electric guitar playing Aerosmith through a cheap amp to an audience of tools on a pegboard.

Jackie hadn't walked that route in two years. Hadn't crossed Hop Brook since the afternoon Dr. Marsh had found him on the bridge, facing down four kids on dirt bikes, and called them *those boys.* Since Jackie had moved aside and let Nick ride through and watched their friendship die in the sound of engines on old wood.

He didn't think about it. Not in words. But his body knew the distance — less than a mile from this table to that garage, close enough that if the wind was right and both of them were playing at the same time, the sound might meet somewhere over Hop Brook and blend for a moment before the water carried it away.

"Elizabeth!" Eileen called up the stairs. "Breakfast."

A door opened. Footsteps. Elizabeth Marsh came down the stairs the way thirteen-year-olds come down stairs — like gravity was a suggestion she was considering. She was small for her age, dark-haired, with their mother's face and their father's analytical eyes. She played violin the way other people breathed — without effort, without performance, without the bone-deep need to prove anything to anyone. Music came to her the way color comes to painters. She'd been principal violin of the youth symphony since she was eleven, and their father never hovered over her practicing the way he hovered over Jackie, because Elizabeth's talent didn't require management. It just existed.

Jackie envied her with a pain he'd never named.

She sat down, pulled her chair in, poured juice. Looked at Jackie. Looked at their father. Read the room the way she read sheet music — instantly, completely, with all the dynamics marked.

"Is this about the Battle of the Bands?" she asked.

Dr. Marsh set down the Globe. "We're not discussing that."

"Jackie's band is good, Dad. I've heard them practice."

"Elizabeth."

"The Queen stuff is actually really complex. The harmonies in 'Somebody to Love' are—"

"Elizabeth. That's enough."

She ate a triangle of toast. Chewed it slowly. Swallowed. Then, quietly, to Jackie and no one else: "You should play what makes you sound like you."

Dr. Marsh heard it. His jaw tightened. But he said nothing, because Elizabeth had a way of saying things that were impossible to argue with and equally impossible to punish. She was thirteen and she understood the house better than anyone in it.

After breakfast, Jackie went back upstairs. He sat on his bed and looked out the window at the yard, the stone wall, the trees, the invisible brook beyond them. Somewhere on the other side, Nick Johnson was in his dead father's garage, playing guitar to an audience of wrenches and socket sets. Somewhere in the southeast quadrant, past the Dump and the Cavicchio pig farm where the smell could peel paint on bad days, a girl named Patty Lally was probably humming "Go Your Own Way" without realizing she was doing it.

The Battle of the Bands was six weeks away. Jackie had his band — Elizabeth on violin, Ace Callahan on drums, Max Wesslen on bass. They called themselves Crown Jewels, which was corny but Elizabeth had picked the name and nobody argued with Elizabeth. They'd been rehearsing in the basement three nights a week, after Dr. Marsh went to bed, playing Queen covers with the amps turned low and the door closed. Jackie sang and played piano and sometimes picked up the Telecaster when he was sure his father couldn't hear it from upstairs.

He opened the sock drawer. The gold-script QUEEN belt buckle sat in the back corner, behind a rolled pair of black socks. He picked it up. Felt the weight of it. Remembered the cafeteria at Curtis Middle, September 1974 — sunlight catching a chrome AEROSMITH buckle across the room, a kid named Nick walking toward him with the confidence of someone who'd already decided they were going to be friends.

Five years ago. A different person. A different Sudbury — one where the lines between the quadrants barely existed, where a kid from Dutton Road and a kid from Concord Road could sit at the same table and argue about Queen versus Aerosmith and bond over hating disco and ride bikes through the conservation land together without anyone telling them they didn't belong in the same sentence.

He put the buckle back. Closed the drawer.

Downstairs, the Steinway waited in the living room, lid closed, bench pushed in. The piano his father had rented for recitals. The piano Jackie would play the Chopin on, and the Bach, and the Rachmaninoff, because those were the pieces that had gotten him into Juilliard and those were the pieces the Tanglewood faculty expected and those were the pieces that made his father tap his finger on the table in four-four time without knowing he was doing it.

Jackie went downstairs and opened the piano lid. Sat on the bench. Put his hands on the keys.

He played the Chopin. Perfectly. Every phrase shaped. Every dynamic where his teacher had placed it.

He hated every note.
