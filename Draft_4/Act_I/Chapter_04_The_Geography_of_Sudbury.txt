CHAPTER 4: THE GEOGRAPHY OF SUDBURY


You need to understand Sudbury to understand what happened to those boys.

I take a sip of bourbon and let the silence sit for a second. The ON AIR light blinks. Through the booth window, Boston is all glass and money. But twenty miles west, there's a town that tells you everything about how America sorts its children.

Hop Brook runs north to south through the center of Sudbury. Narrow enough to jump in summer. In March it's louder — snowmelt pushing through, water running cold over flat stones. Named for the hops that grew wild along its banks a century ago, back before the Sudbury Valley Trustees started preserving what was left of the land. The brook starts in Ashland and dies in the Sudbury River near the Wayside Inn, where Henry Ford tried to buy American authenticity wholesale.

Running east to west, bisecting the town at a right angle: the Dump. Twenty-three acres. The official name was Sudbury Sanitary Landfill. Nobody called it that.

Those two lines — water and waste — cut Sudbury into four pieces.

Northeast: working-class. Houses on streets named for trees that got cut down to build them. Fathers in construction, mothers counting coupons. Kids learning guitar because it was cheaper than expecting college. Nick Johnson lived here. Three blocks from the Dump. On hot days in July you could smell it from his front porch. His father Dan used to say they got a discount on the mortgage for the view. He wasn't joking.

Southwest: money. Colonials on lots measured in acres. Professors and doctors and their wives who ran the PTA like a military operation. Kids took lessons — piano, violin, French. Not because they wanted to but because musical literacy was assumed, like good posture. Jackie Marsh lived here. The Marsh house had a brass plaque that said WHITMORE ESTATE, which wasn't even their name.

Southeast: the Cavicchios. A hundred acres of pig farm up against the landfill, which sounds like a cosmic joke but was actually cosmic justice. Giuseppe Cavicchio refused to sell his water rights to Henry Ford in 1923. Ford wanted an auto parts factory. Giuseppe said no. Ford called it Italian stubbornness. Giuseppe called it understanding what land is worth. By 1979 the third generation was still there, pigs eating garbage, garbage feeding pigs. Patty Lally grew up on this side. Some days she smelled like pig. She stopped apologizing for it freshman year.

Northwest: old money pretending it wasn't money at all. Stone walls older than the Constitution. Revolutionary War land. "No Motorized Vehicles" signs on conservation trails that got torn down every weekend by kids from the northeast on mini-bikes.

Those mini-bikes. Honda 50s and Yamaha 60s, two-stroke engines screaming like angry hornets through the trees. Kids from the northeast side riding them because a mini-bike was the only freedom you could get when your parents worked doubles. Mud on your jeans, exhaust in your lungs, wind in your face going thirty miles an hour down a trail you carved yourself. The parents in the colonials wrote letters to the town council about noise and property values. The kids on the bikes couldn't hear them over the engines.

Saturday mornings at the Dump were the one time the lines didn't hold. Station wagons from the northwest, pickup trucks from the northeast, the Cavicchios' flatbed that always smelled like pig and carried twice as much as anyone else's. Everyone backed up to the pit. Everyone's garbage stank the same. Glass in one pile, paper in another, everything else into the ground. The kids sat in the cars while their fathers threw bags. For twenty minutes, Sudbury was a democracy.

Then everyone drove home to their own quadrant.

The thing about Sudbury — and this is what nobody who didn't grow up there will ever understand — is that the lines were invisible until they weren't. A kid from the northeast could ride his mini-bike through the conservation land and end up in the northwest without even knowing he'd crossed over. A kid from the southwest could walk to the Dump on a Saturday and stand next to a kid from the northeast and they'd both be watching the same bulldozer push the same dirt over the same garbage. In middle school, at Curtis, the lines barely existed. You sat where you sat. You liked who you liked. You wore your belt buckle and nobody thought twice about what it meant.

But by high school — by Lincoln-Sudbury, where the town of Lincoln added its own kids to the mix and the cafeteria divided into Rampers and Rats and Regs and Rollers — by then the lines were walls. And Hop Brook, that narrow little stream you could jump in July, might as well have been the Atlantic.

That's the town where Nick and Jackie met. Where they became best friends at thirteen because they both hated disco and loved rock and roll. Where they stopped being friends at sixteen because America doesn't let those friendships last.

I pick up the bourbon again. The glass is warm now. I've been holding it too long.

"So let me tell you about the day they met. Two kids. Two belt buckles. And a cafeteria where, for about five minutes, none of those lines mattered."
