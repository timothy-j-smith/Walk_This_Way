CHAPTER 2


The Karmann Ghia found every pothole on Dutton Road. Maxanne's coffee sloshed in the cup holder she'd wedged between the seats — a Dunkin' cup, large, regular, already cold. She'd been driving twenty minutes west from the station on a tip from a sound engineer named Paulie DeLuca who'd heard something at a VFW benefit in Sudbury.

"A kid," Paulie had said. "Sixteen, seventeen. Playing in a garage with some friends. I was there for the fish fry, and I could hear them from the parking lot. I walked over and stood in the driveway like an idiot for twenty minutes. Maxanne, this kid sounds like a young Tyler. Not a copy. The real thing."

Paulie had heard ten thousand bands. He got excited about maybe four. So Maxanne drove.

Dutton Road was the kind of street where the houses had been built in the forties, all at once, by someone who understood that returning veterans needed affordable shelter and not much else. Small capes, narrow lots, aluminum siding in colors that had once been choices — mustard yellow, olive green, a blue that might have been cheerful in 1947. The Johnson house sat near the end of the cul-de-sac. The paint was peeling in long strips, showing old white underneath. The front steps tilted. The gutter on the south side had given up and hung at an angle, a mouth with a broken jaw. But the yard was raked. The walk was shoveled. Somebody cared, even if caring couldn't fix everything.

She parked behind a station wagon with a Friendly's bumper sticker and a tailpipe wired on with a coat hanger. From the detached garage behind the house, she could hear it — distorted guitar, bass drum, the unmistakable stomp of "Walk This Way." Not clean. Not polished. Alive.

The front door opened before she knocked. Grace Johnson stood behind the storm door in her Friendly's uniform — polyester, beige and brown, a name tag pinned above the left breast pocket that read GRACE in that corporate font designed to make everyone look interchangeable. She had a ketchup stain near the pocket that was older than the conversation they were about to have. Her hands were rough. Not calloused — raw. The hands of someone who washed dishes at home because the dishwasher broke two years ago and there was never enough money to fix it.

Grace looked at Maxanne. Took in the bell-bottoms, the leather boots, the oversized sunglasses pushed up on her head, the keys to a foreign car that probably started on the first try every morning. Maxanne had been looked at this way before — by mothers who had already decided that whoever was standing on their step had money and therefore couldn't be trusted with the fragile thing inside.

"I'm Maxanne Sartori. From WBCN."

"I know who you are." Grace didn't open the storm door. "You broke Aerosmith."

"I played their record. They did the rest."

Grace studied her through the glass. Behind the house, the garage band kicked into "Sweet Emotion," the bass riff shaking something loose in the gutter. In the kitchen behind Grace, Maxanne could see a counter with bills fanned out next to a coffee mug and a calculator with a paper roll. The math of keeping the lights on.

"Nick's in the garage," Grace said. She still hadn't opened the door. "His friends are with him. They practice after school. Sometimes they practice instead of school, and I pretend I don't know."

"I'd like to listen. If that's all right."

Grace was quiet for a long moment. Something moved across her face — not hope exactly. Something more careful than hope. The way you'd pick up a piece of glass from the kitchen floor. You want to believe it's from a broken drinking glass and not something worse.

"Don't make him promises," Grace said. "He's had enough of those."

She opened the storm door and pointed toward the back of the house. Then she went back inside without another word.

Maxanne crossed the yard. The grass was frozen stiff, brown and dead, crunching under her boots. A basketball hoop with no net stood at the edge of the driveway, the pole rusted at the base. A stack of firewood was covered with a blue tarp that had come loose on one side. The garage door was up about eighteen inches, enough to let the music escape and the cold air in. She ducked under it.

The first thing she noticed was the smell. Sawdust and motor oil, old and deep, baked into the concrete over years. Not a rehearsal space that smelled like a workshop — a workshop that someone's kid had colonized without displacing anything. The tools were still on the pegboard above the bench, hung by size, each one outlined with a felt-tip marker so you'd know where it went back. Masking tape labels in careful block handwriting: SOCKET SET. TORQUE WRENCH. 3/8 DRIVE. The handwriting of a man who organized things because his work depended on finding them fast. Whoever had written those labels was dead. The tape was yellowing. Nobody had moved the tools.

Under the workbench, pushed against the wall, a guitar case. Not the one the kid was playing — something older, a hard case with airline stickers on it and a broken clasp held shut with a bungee cord. Dan Johnson's guitar. Nobody had told Maxanne that. She'd find out later.

A space heater sat in the corner, unplugged. It was maybe thirty-eight degrees in the garage. The boys played in their coats.

And they were playing.

Nick Johnson stood in the center, a pawn-shop Stratocaster with a cracked pickguard and electrical tape on the strap button. He was lean, not tall, with dark hair past his collar and the kind of concentration that made him look older than seventeen. He didn't perform. He didn't move around or close his eyes or tilt his head back the way kids do when they're imitating their heroes. He stood flat-footed and played, his left hand working the fretboard with a speed that didn't match the rest of him — everything else was still, but the hand moved like it had somewhere urgent to be.

Three other kids backed him. A bass player who watched Nick's fretting hand for changes. A drummer on a kit held together with duct tape and C-clamps, keeping solid time. A kid on a Fender Rhodes with half the keys discolored, who played the pads competently and stayed out of the way. They were his band the way a frame is a painting's band — they held him up and didn't try to be the picture.

They saw Maxanne and stopped. The garage went quiet in the wrong way, the way a room goes quiet when someone's been caught.

"Keep playing," Maxanne said. She found a five-gallon paint bucket, flipped it over, and sat down. "Pretend I'm not here."

They looked at Nick. Nick looked at her — not suspicious exactly, but measuring. Deciding how much weight this stranger could bear before he had to worry about what she wanted from him.

He turned back to the band and counted off. Four clicks of the drumsticks. And then "Dream On."

He sang it close to the mic, a cheap Shure SM58 running through a Peavey amp with a torn grille cloth. His voice broke on the high notes. Not from lack of control — from something underneath the control, a pressure that the throat couldn't hold. He was seventeen years old, singing Steven Tyler's words about aging and mirrors and time, and it should have been ridiculous. A teenager singing about a life he hadn't lived. But Nick Johnson had buried his father at fifteen, and when he sang "every time that I look in the mirror, all these lines on my face getting clearer," he wasn't performing someone else's grief. He was translating his own into the only language that still made sense to him.

During the bridge, his eyes went to the pegboard. The socket set. The space where the 9/16ths should have been — the one Dan had in his pocket the morning he went to the Prudential Tower site and didn't come back. Nick's voice thinned to almost nothing on the line about the past being the past, and then it came back full and raw and ragged, and Maxanne felt something she hadn't felt since the first time she'd heard Tyler in that bar in Allston. The hair on her arms stood up. Not because it was perfect. Because it wasn't. Because the cracks in this kid's voice were load-bearing.

When the song ended, nobody breathed for about five seconds. The last chord hummed in the tools on the wall — a low vibration she could feel in the paint bucket under her.

"You got a name?" she asked. "For the band."

Nick shrugged. "Not yet. We're just playing."

"You got a demo?"

He shook his head. "Can't afford studio time."

Maxanne reached into her bag and pulled out a business card. WBCN. 104.1. The Rock of Boston. She held it out. He didn't take it right away. His eyes went to the tools, then to the guitar case under the bench, then back to the card.

"Why?" he said. "Why would you come all the way out here?"

She thought about giving him the speech — rock and roll is for kids like you, garages are where it all starts, Tyler played a room this size. She'd given that speech before. It was true and it was useless and it made her feel like a recruiter.

"Because I've been listening to bands for six years," she said, "and most of them are trying to sound like someone. You're not trying to sound like Tyler. You sound like yourself. That's rarer than you think."

Nick took the card. Looked at it. Folded it once and put it in his back pocket.

"We practice Tuesdays and Thursdays," he said. "If you want to come back."

Maxanne ducked under the garage door and crossed the yard. Behind her, they started up again — "Sweet Emotion" this time, the bass line first, then Nick's guitar coming in over the top of it, raw and full, the sound pouring out through the gap in the garage door and into the cold street where the potholes never got fixed and the houses leaned and the mortgages were always late.

She sat in the Karmann Ghia and didn't turn the key. Through the gap at the bottom of the garage door she could see sneakers and the base of a mic stand and the space heater that nobody plugged in anymore. On the kitchen window of the house, she could see Grace's silhouette, standing at the sink, washing dishes by hand, not looking toward the garage. Letting him have this one thing without watching too hard.

Less than a mile east, across Hop Brook where the water ran cold over flat stones in March, there was a boy at a piano in a living room where nothing was broken and everything was exactly where it was supposed to be. Maxanne didn't know about him yet. She would. Dr. Richard Marsh would make sure of that.
