CHAPTER 2: NICK'S WORLD — THE GARAGE


Maxanne Sartori drove up to the Johnson house in her powder-blue Karmann Ghia, the car bottoming out twice on potholes the town never fixed on this side of Hop Brook.

The house sat back from Dutton Road behind a lawn that had given up in November. Peeling mustard paint. Gutters sagging on one side. Front steps with a lean to them, like the whole place was slowly deciding to sit down. But the bones were good — old New England bones, crown molding visible through the front window, the kind of detail that said this house had been something once, before it became what it was now.

From the detached garage: power chords. Loud enough to rattle the mailbox lid. Maxanne had been driving around Greater Boston listening to bands for six years, and she knew the difference between noise and need. This was need.

She knocked on the front door. Grace Johnson opened it in her Friendly's uniform — polyester, ketchup stain near the left pocket that had survived every wash cycle since October. The name tag said GRACE in that cheerful corporate font. Her hands were red at the knuckles. Dish soap, not lotion.

Grace looked Maxanne over. Bell-bottoms. Oversized sunglasses pushed up on her head. The kind of boots you buy at a store that doesn't have a clearance rack.

"You're that lady from WBCN. The one who broke Aerosmith."

"Guilty as charged."

Grace didn't move from the doorway. "Nick's in the garage." She paused. "Don't fill his head with dreams we can't afford."

"Mind if I listen?"

Grace studied her a beat longer, then stepped aside — not toward the garage, but back into the house. "He's been at it since school let out. Won't stop until dinner."

Maxanne crossed the yard toward the sound. The garage door was up about two feet, enough to let the cold March air in and the music out. She ducked under it.

Inside: one bare bulb, four boys, and the smell of sawdust and motor oil that no amount of playing had ever cleared out. Dan Johnson's garage. Dan Johnson's tools still on the pegboard, hung by size, masking tape labels in a dead man's careful handwriting — SOCKET SET, TORQUE WRENCH, 3/8 DRIVE. A space heater sat in the corner, unplugged. Milk crates for chairs. Against the back wall, a stack of records next to the workbench — Aerosmith on top, Dave Brubeck underneath, some old blues Maxanne couldn't read from where she stood. And beneath the workbench, pushed back against the wall, a guitar case that wasn't the one the kid was playing.

Nick Johnson stood in the center of it all, seventeen years old, playing a pawn-shop guitar with electrical tape holding the strap button on. Three other kids backed him — bass, drums, keyboards on a Fender Rhodes that had seen better decades. All of them playing like the concrete floor might open up if they stopped.

They saw her and the music quit.

"Don't stop on my account," Maxanne said. She found a paint can and sat. "Play it like you mean it."

They looked at each other. Then Nick hit the opening riff of "Dream On" and the rest followed.

Maxanne listened.

His voice cracked on the high notes. Not training — something else. The crack of a kid who'd been carrying more than a seventeen-year-old was built to carry. He sang with his eyes half-closed, chin tipped up, and twice during the bridge his gaze drifted to the tools on the pegboard. The socket set was missing the 9/16ths. That was in Dan Johnson's pocket the morning the crane cable snapped.

"Every time that I look in the mirror," he sang, "all these lines on my face getting clearer..."

The bass player watched Nick's hands for cues. The drummer kept solid time. The kid on the Rhodes was competent — nothing more, nothing less. But Nick was the reason she'd driven twenty miles on a Tuesday.

When they finished, nobody spoke. The last chord hummed through the tools on the wall, a low vibration Maxanne could feel in her sternum. Then quiet.

"What's your band called?" she asked.

Nick shrugged. "Don't have a name yet. We're just playing."

"You got a demo?"

He shook his head. "Can't afford studio time."

Maxanne reached into her bag and pulled out a business card. WBCN, 104.1 FM. The Rock of Boston. Her name under the logo in type smaller than it deserved. She held it out.

"Come by the station. We'll find some time for you to lay down some tracks."

Nick took the card but didn't look at it. He looked at the tools on the wall. Then at the guitar case under the workbench — his father's, Maxanne would learn later. Then back at the card.

"Why?" he said. "Why would you help us?"

Maxanne stood, knees cracking. "Because I've been listening to bands for six years and most of them are trying to sound like someone else. You're not trying to sound like Aerosmith. You sound like a kid who lost his father and picked up a guitar because it was the only thing in that garage that could talk back."

She hadn't meant to say that much. Nick's jaw tightened. The bass player looked at the floor.

"Come by the station," Maxanne said again, quieter this time. She ducked back under the garage door.

Walking to her car, she heard them start up again behind her. "Sweet Emotion" — the opening bass line first, then the guitar, then Nick's voice coming in raw and unfinished over the top of it. The sound carried across the cold yard and into the street where the potholes would never get fixed and the houses leaned and the mortgages were always late.

She sat in the Karmann Ghia for a moment before turning the key. Through the garage's gap she could see Nick's sneakers and the bottom of the mic stand and the unplugged space heater that had probably been warm the last time Dan Johnson was alive.

Less than a mile away, on the other side of Hop Brook, there was a piano recital happening in a living room where nothing was broken and everything cost more than it needed to.
