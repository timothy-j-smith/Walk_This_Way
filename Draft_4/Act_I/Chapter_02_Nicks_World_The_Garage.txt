CHAPTER 2


March 1979. Nick Johnson woke to the sound of his mother's car not starting.

He lay in bed and listened to the engine crank — that tired whine the Buick made every morning now, the sound of a starter motor begging to be replaced. Three cranks. Four. On the fifth, the engine caught, coughed, held. He heard the transmission drop into reverse, the crunch of frozen gravel under bald tires, and then the car pulling away down Dutton Road, headlights swinging across his ceiling and gone.

5:48 AM. Grace worked the opening shift at Friendly's. Had since Dan died. She left before six, came home after four if it was a single, after ten if she pulled a double, which was most days now. The house was Nick's from 5:48 until the afternoon. His and whatever was left of Dan's.

He got up. The room was cold — they kept the heat at fifty-eight overnight because oil cost money they didn't have — and he dressed fast. Jeans from the floor. A thermal shirt that had been Dan's, the neck stretched out, the cuffs frayed. Work boots he'd gotten at Building 19, the discount chain where everything was cheap and nothing was exactly the right size. He was seventeen. Five-ten, a hundred and forty pounds, with dark hair past his collar and hands that were calloused in places that didn't make sense for a kid who'd never worked construction. First finger, left hand. The pad of the thumb. The side of the ring finger where it pressed against the steel strings.

Downstairs, the kitchen held the evidence of Grace's morning. Coffee in the pot — she always made enough for him. A note on the counter in her handwriting, the same handwriting that had once been on the household bills and the mortgage checks and the Christmas cards they'd stopped sending after Dan: *Meatloaf in fridge. Don't forget calc homework. Love, Mom.*

Nick poured coffee and stood at the counter. The kitchen window looked out on the backyard, which was really just twenty feet of dead brown grass and then the garage. Dan's garage. The one he'd built himself in 1971, the year Nick was born, working weekends with lumber from a job site that was throwing away perfectly good two-by-sixes. He'd poured the concrete floor with a buddy from the union. Hung the door himself. Wired the single overhead light. Built the workbench out of maple he'd milled at a friend's shop in Framingham. The garage was the best-built thing on the property, better than the house, which had been Grace's childhood home and was showing every one of its thirty-five years.

That was one of the things nobody in Sudbury knew, or if they knew they'd forgotten: Grace had grown up in this house. Not as a working-class kid — as a wealthy one. Her father had owned the property when Dutton Road was still farmland, before the town subdivided it into small lots in the fifties. He'd killed himself in 1960, and the money had gone the way money goes when the person who made it stops breathing. Grace was nineteen when she married Dan Johnson, a carpenter with clean hands and a Gibson acoustic he played on the back porch after dinner. She'd chosen him. Chosen this. And she'd never said a word to Nick about the life she'd had before, the one with money and possibility and a father who was alive.

Nick didn't know any of that yet. He knew the house was cold. He knew the mortgage was late. He knew his mother worked too hard and never complained and left coffee in the pot every morning like a small prayer that the day would be better than the last one.

He rinsed his cup and went out the back door.

The yard was frozen. March in Sudbury meant the ground was hard as concrete until ten in the morning, and the air had that raw edge that made your lungs ache if you breathed too deep. You could smell the Dump from here on bad days — twenty-three acres of landfill half a mile east, where every Saturday morning the whole town lined up to throw away what they didn't need anymore. Today the wind was from the west, so instead of garbage he smelled pine from the conservation land and the faint mineral tang of Hop Brook, which ran behind the properties on the next street over. In spring, when the snow melted, you could hear the water from Nick's backyard — a steady rush over flat stones that sounded like applause from far away. Now, in March, it was somewhere between a trickle and a roar, the brook deciding how much of itself to give back.

He opened the garage door. It rolled up on tracks Dan had greased every spring, and Nick hadn't greased since. The door stuck at the three-foot mark, the way it always did, and he lifted it the rest of the way by hand.

Inside: forty degrees. The smell of sawdust and motor oil and cold concrete. Dan had been dead for two years and three months, and the garage still smelled like him. It would probably smell like him forever. The sawdust was in the grain of the workbench. The motor oil was in the concrete. The cold was just cold — the space heater sat in the corner where Dan had always plugged it in, but Nick didn't use it. Couldn't say why. Just didn't.

The tools were on the pegboard. Dan's tools, hung by size, each one outlined with a felt-tip marker on the pegboard so you'd know where it went back. Masking tape labels in his handwriting — careful block letters, the handwriting of a man who measured twice. SOCKET SET. TORQUE WRENCH. 3/8 DRIVE. CHANNEL LOCKS. The socket set was missing the 9/16ths. That was in Dan's pocket the morning he went to the Prudential Tower site and a crane cable snapped and four steel I-beams came down on the trailer where he was checking blueprints and eating the tuna sandwich Grace had made him that morning.

Nick had stopped counting the days. Counting implied it would stop hurting at some number, and it hadn't, and he'd decided it wasn't going to.

Under the workbench, pushed against the wall, was Dan's guitar case. A hard case, brown, with airline stickers from trips Dan had never taken — he'd bought it used at a yard sale in Framingham. Inside was a Gibson J-45, the acoustic Dan had played on the porch after dinner, after Grace went inside, after Nick was supposed to be asleep. Dan never played in front of anyone. Never talked about it. Just sat on the porch steps in the dark and played blues and Brubeck and sometimes Aerosmith, thinking nobody could hear him. Nick heard him every night through the bedroom window. Fell asleep to it. After Dan died, he'd opened the case and found sheet music folded into the pocket — Brubeck's "Take Five," handwritten chord charts for "Dream On," and a piece of paper with just three words in Dan's handwriting: *don't stop playing.*

Nick didn't know if it was a note to himself or a note to his son. It didn't matter. He'd taped it to the wall above the workbench, next to the Aerosmith calendar from 1975 that nobody had turned past October.

He plugged in his amp — a Peavey Backstage he'd bought for thirty dollars at the same pawn shop where he'd gotten the Stratocaster. The guitar was a '66 with a cracked pickguard and a strap button held on with electrical tape. It had cost twenty-five dollars. He'd paid for it with lawn-mowing money from the summer he was fifteen, the summer after Dan died, the summer he'd decided that if he couldn't have his father he could at least have his father's music.

He sat on a milk crate and played.

Not practicing. Not warming up. Just playing, the way you breathe — because you need to, because it's the thing that keeps you in the room when everything in you wants to leave. He played the opening riff of "Dream On" and listened to it bounce off the concrete walls and come back to him changed, the way sound does in small rooms, the reverb adding something the amp couldn't — a thickness, a weight, like the garage itself was playing along.

The tools on the pegboard hummed. Not a metaphor — physics. Certain frequencies vibrate certain objects. The wrench rattled against the 3/8 drive when Nick hit E minor. Eighty-two hertz. Dan used to know which chords did it and which didn't. Nick had figured it out on his own.

He played for an hour. Alone. The garage door up two feet, the sound pouring out into the frozen yard and the empty street and the neighborhood where the houses leaned and the mortgages were late and the fathers worked construction or didn't work at all. Dutton Road. Three blocks from the Dump. On the wrong side of Hop Brook, where the town never fixed the potholes because the people who lived here didn't go to the meetings where potholes got discussed.

At seven-thirty, he heard a screen door open three houses down. Stephen Murphy, bass player, coming out to get the Globe off the porch for his mother. Stephen waved. Nick waved back. Stephen would be here by four with his bass and David Cassel with his drums and Mark Pianka with his grandmother's Casio keyboard, and they'd play until Grace came home and turned off the porch light, which was her way of saying enough.

But that was later. Now it was just Nick. Just the guitar. Just his father's tools on the wall and his father's case under the bench and his father's note taped above the workbench where he could read it every time he looked up.

*Don't stop playing.*

He didn't.
