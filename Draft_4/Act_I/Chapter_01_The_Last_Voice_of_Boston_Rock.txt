CHAPTER 1


Four hours. That's what I've got left.

Four hours before WBCN becomes just another frequency in the graveyard of American radio. Four hours before the suits flip the switch and turn us into automated Christian contemporary — because apparently Jesus rocks harder than Freddie Mercury ever did. Four hours before fifty-six years of breaking bands, of late-night conversations with musicians who poured their souls into three-minute songs, of watching Boston transform from a city of steel and sweat into whatever the hell it's become now — four hours before all of that gets erased like a bad demo tape.

I'm Maxanne Sartori. Fifty-nine years old, sitting in Studio B with a bourbon that costs more than most people's car payments, watching the ON AIR sign blink red like a dying heartbeat. The station called and brought me back for this. One last time. I'm in the same studio where I watched Steven Tyler hear his own voice on the radio for the first time back in '73 — the look on his face when he realized that raw, untamed sound was actually going out to thousands of people, that it was real, that it mattered. The same turntables that spun the first singles from bands who went on to fill stadiums. The same microphone that carried my voice through the seventies, the years of chaos and rebellion and music that made parents afraid of what was coming.

"Maxanne?" The program director's voice crackles through the intercom. The Kid's twenty-eight, wears sneakers that cost more than my first apartment — a studio in the Back Bay I rented with my first WBCN paycheck — and thinks rock history started with Nirvana. "You've got four hours left. Make them count."

Make them count.

As if four hours could contain what WBCN meant to a city that lived and breathed rock and roll. As if the story I'm about to tell could be wrapped up with a lesson learned. It doesn't work that way.

But here's the thing about endings — they make liars of us all. You promise to go out with dignity, say something profound, make those final moments matter. And then you find yourself sitting in a radio booth with a bottle of Pappy Van Winkle that Steven Tyler himself sent when he heard about the station closing. "For the first lady who ever gave a damn," the note said. Even after all these years, even after becoming one of the biggest rock stars in the world, he still remembers.

I adjust the headphones — the same Sony MDR-7506s I've worn since 1971, held together now with electrical tape and pure stubbornness. The console lights pulse. Outside the booth windows, Boston glitters in the darkness — all glass towers and gentrification where dive bars used to breed bands, where the Rat used to host shows that would make your grandmother blush, where kids with nothing but dreams could still believe they had a shot at changing the world.

"This is Maxanne Sartori, and for the next four hours, you're listening to the final broadcast of WBCN, 104.1 on your FM dial. The Rock of Boston. The station that launched Aerosmith when they were just kids from New Hampshire with more attitude than money. The station that gave J. Geils his first shot, that broke The Cars when they were still a garage band, that made Boston believe it could rock as hard as anywhere else."

I take a sip of bourbon — another violation of modern radio protocol, but who's going to fire me? The warmth spreads through my chest. The good kind that makes you tell stories you've been carrying too long.

"But tonight isn't about the bands that made it. Tonight is about the ones that didn't. And tonight is about one I could never get out of my head — two kids from Sudbury who should have changed everything but got caught in the crossfire of America's class war. Two seventeen-year-old boys who should have been allies became rivals over a question that seems quaint now: What is authentic rock and roll? But it was deeper than rock. Much deeper. It always is."

The silence stretches. In the engineering booth, the kid they've assigned to babysit my final show looks up from his phone. Maybe something in my voice warns him this won't be the nostalgia trip he expected.

"March, 1979. Disco was gasping its last breath. The Bee Gees had spent three years convincing America that falsetto voices and dance floors were the future of music. Saturday Night Fever had turned every bar into a discotheque. But rock and roll was fighting back hard. The Clash were about to teach America that punk had something to say. MTV was three years away from making image matter more than sound. We were living in the last moments when two kids with nothing but talent and passion could matter as much as marketing budgets."

I spin my chair to face the wall of albums. The vinyl spines catch the booth's amber light. My fingers find the right cart without looking — muscle memory, my hands knowing these grooves better than my own face in the mirror.

"Nick Johnson lived in a house where the mortgage was late and dreams were luxuries you couldn't afford. His father had died in a construction accident two years earlier, crushed beneath a load of steel beams when a crane cable snapped — the kind of industrial tragedy that makes page three of the Metro section and gets forgotten by everyone except the families who eat silence for dinner afterward. His mother worked doubles at Friendly's, came home smelling like fryer oil and other people's happiness, and never once complained about her son spending grocery money on guitar strings."

The kid in the engineering booth is scrolling through his phone. He has no idea what a garage means to rock and roll. He grew up with GarageBand, thinking music could be manufactured in a bedroom with the right plugins. He never felt four walls, concrete floor, and kids who had nothing to lose.

"Jackie Marsh lived less than a mile away, but between them stretched Hop Brook — a stream that divided Sudbury like a scar, separating the haves from the have-nots. In winter, kids from both sides skated the same pond trying to emulate Bobby Orr. But when the ice melted, they returned to their proper territories. Jackie's father was a psychiatrist and a professor at Boston University. A social climber. He published papers about childhood trauma and couldn't see what was happening under his own roof."

I pause. Let the silence breathe. Radio silence used to terrify program directors, but I learned long ago that sometimes the most powerful thing you can do is shut up and let the music speak.

"These boys lived in the same town, went to the same school, walked the same streets. But America designs its suburbs to prevent these collisions — invisible lines drawn by property values, school districts, the careful architecture of keeping the classes in their proper lanes. Music has always been democracy's wild card. The frequency that cuts through all the bullshit boundaries we build."

Through the booth window, I can see the billboard across Boylston Street advertising luxury condos where the Rat used to host bands too raw for radio. Twenty-four hundred square feet starting at 2.2 million — you could buy the entire block of Lansdowne Street for that in '77. The Ramones played that club when Joey was so tall he had to duck under the pipes backstage, and the sound system was held together with duct tape and prayers.

"I met them both in the spring of 1979, about two months before the Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School Battle of the Bands. I was there as a judge, WBCN sponsoring the event, back when we could afford to care about local music. Back when Clear Channel hadn't swallowed half the FM dial and algorithms hadn't replaced intuition."

I reach for the cassette on my desk. The plastic case is warm from my grip, the label faded but legible after thirty years. "Demo - Nick/Jackie - un-Authorized - March 1979" in block letters. My letters. The tape has that old-cassette smell — plastic and magnetic oxide and the faint trace of cigarette smoke. I can still remember the night they came to this studio and proved that harmony was still possible, even after everything.

"This tape is the only recording that exists of Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh playing together. The shred of evidence that we chose division over music. The rest of the story? That's what the next four hours are for. So pour yourself something strong, turn off your phone, and let me tell you how the Battle of the Bands became a war — and about the band that never was."

The ON AIR sign pulses. Four hours to tell the truth.

I crack my knuckles and lean into the microphone.

"But first, we need to talk about a garage on Dutton Road, and why the ghost of a dead construction worker might have been the only adult who understood what was really at stake."

I close my eyes and let the memory take me back. March 1979. Cold enough that you could see your breath, but the kind of cold that felt alive, promising spring whether winter liked it or not. That's when I first drove out to Sudbury, following rumors about a kid who played guitar like he was trying to resurrect the dead.
