CHAPTER 4


September 1974. Twelve years old and everything smelled like cafeteria pizza.

The Ephraim Curtis Middle School sat on a hill between the northeast and southwest quadrants of Sudbury, which meant kids from both sides of Hop Brook ended up at the same tables whether anyone planned it that way or not. The building was brick, single-story, built in 1958 with the optimism of a country that believed public education could fix everything. By 1974 the optimism had faded but the building remained — long hallways, linoleum floors the color of old chewing gum, fluorescent lights that buzzed at a frequency only dogs and twelve-year-olds could hear.

The cafeteria was in the center of the building, which was either an architectural choice or a metaphor, depending on who you asked. It had the dimensions of a gymnasium and the acoustics of a swimming pool. Sound bounced off the cinder block walls and came back louder and slightly wrong, so that every conversation sounded like it was happening inside a tin can. The windows ran along one wall, floor to ceiling, and in September the afternoon sun came through them like a searchlight, heating the room to approximately the temperature of a low oven and catching anything reflective — belt buckles, for instance — and throwing it across the room like a signal.

Nick Johnson walked into the cafeteria at 11:40 on a Tuesday carrying a brown bag lunch his mother had packed before her shift. Peanut butter and jelly on white bread, an apple, a bag of chips she'd bought on sale at Star Market. He was twelve, small for his age but wiry, with dark hair that his mother kept trying to cut and he kept growing past his collar. He wore jeans and a black t-shirt and work boots that had been his father's — too big, stuffed with newspaper in the toes — because Dan Johnson believed a boy should have decent boots even if the rest of his wardrobe came from the Goodwill bin on Route 20.

And the belt. Black leather, chrome buckle: AEROSMITH in gothic letters. He'd bought it at Spencer's in the Natick Mall with birthday money — eight dollars, which was also the price of four gallons of milk or two boxes of cereal or one month of guitar strings, and he'd chosen the buckle without hesitating, because some decisions make themselves.

He found a table near the windows. The cafeteria had its territories — not fixed yet, not the way they'd harden in high school, but forming. The kids from the northeast side, the Dutton Road and Maple Street kids, tended to drift toward the windows. The kids from Concord Road and the southwest sat closer to the food line, as if proximity to the source of institutional pizza conferred status. Nobody enforced this. Nobody had to. The architecture of American childhood did the work.

Nick sat down and opened his lunch. Across the cafeteria, someone's transistor radio was playing ABBA. "Waterloo." The synthetic strings and falsetto vocals filled the room like a gas leak — you couldn't escape it, couldn't ignore it, could only wait for it to dissipate. A few kids sang along. Most just endured it the way you endure weather.

He was eating his sandwich and not thinking about anything in particular when the sun found something across the room and threw it into his eyes.

A flash. Gold. Coming from a table near the food line where a kid sat alone with a tray of cafeteria pizza and a carton of milk. The kid was new — Jackie Marsh, arrived from Ithaca two weeks ago, the son of the new BU professor who'd bought the house on Concord Road with the brass plaque that said WHITMORE ESTATE. Jackie was small and clean and sitting with the posture of someone who'd been told to sit up straight so many times the instruction had fused to his spine. He was eating pizza with a fork, which told you everything about where he'd come from and nothing about who he actually was.

The flash came from his belt. Brown leather, gold-script buckle: QUEEN. A gift from his aunt in London, who'd seen Queen at Hammersmith Odeon and thought her nephew should know that rock and roll could be sophisticated and dangerous at the same time.

Nick squinted against the glare. Then he saw the letters. He stopped chewing.

He stood up. Crossed the cafeteria. Walked past the invisible line between the window tables and the food-line tables without knowing it was there, which was the privilege of being twelve and not yet taught which territories belonged to whom.

He stopped at Jackie's table and pointed at the buckle.

"Queen?"

Jackie looked up. Defensive but not afraid — the expression of a new kid who'd been evaluated by every clique in the building and accepted by none of them. "What about it?"

"Nothing." Nick grinned. He turned his hips to show his own buckle. Chrome catching the fluorescent light. "Just — Aerosmith's better."

Jackie put down his fork. "Better? You're out of your mind."

"'Dream On.' Tell me one Queen song that touches 'Dream On.'"

Jackie's eyes went wide. Not with anger — with the shock of recognition. The shock of finding someone who spoke your language in a building full of people who spoke something else.

"'Bohemian Rhapsody,'" Jackie said. "It's six minutes long. It has an operatic section. It changes time signatures four times. It's the greatest rock song ever recorded."

"It's six minutes of a guy who can't decide what genre he's in."

"That's the POINT. Freddie Mercury can do anything. Steven Tyler just screams."

"Tyler doesn't scream. He bleeds."

They stared at each other. The ABBA song ended. "Kung Fu Fighting" by Carl Douglas started. Several kids began doing karate moves in their seats. Nick and Jackie ignored them completely.

"Can we at least agree," Jackie said, "that this—" he pointed at the radio "—is garbage?"

"Oh, God. Yes. What even IS that?"

"Disco. It's what happens when music gets a lobotomy."

Nick sat down. Not across from Jackie — next to him. The way you sit next to someone when you've already decided they're on your side.

"I'm Nick."

"Jackie." He paused. "My dad just moved us here from Ithaca."

"Ithaca. That's upstate New York?"

"Yeah. Cornell. He was teaching there before BU."

Nick didn't know what Cornell was and didn't ask. It didn't matter. What mattered was the buckle and the argument and the absolute, bone-deep certainty that this kid — this fork-using, posture-having kid from the wrong side of the cafeteria — understood something essential about music that nobody else in this building understood.

"So," Nick said. "Queen versus Aerosmith. We're doing this?"

Jackie looked at him. For the first time since he'd arrived in Sudbury, since his father had moved them to a town where the houses had names and the expectations had weight and every kid seemed to already know where they belonged — for the first time, he looked like he was exactly where he was supposed to be.

"We're doing this."

They argued through the rest of lunch. "Killer Queen" versus "Sweet Emotion." Freddie's range versus Tyler's raw power. Brian May's guitar versus Joe Perry's. They agreed on nothing except the things that mattered: that rock and roll was the only music that told the truth, that disco was an abomination, that adults who said music was a waste of time had probably never listened to anything worth hearing.

The bell rang. They walked out together, still arguing. Jackie's QUEEN buckle and Nick's AEROSMITH buckle catching the hallway fluorescents, throwing small reflections on the lockers as they passed.

Outside the building, the September sun was warm on the parking lot where the buses idled. Nick's bus went north, toward Dutton Road and the houses near the Dump where you could smell the landfill on hot days and hear the bulldozers on Saturday mornings. Jackie's mother was picking him up, a green Volvo parked in the circle, waiting to take him south across Hop Brook to Concord Road where the lawns were measured in acres and the pianos were Steinways.

"Same time tomorrow?" Nick said.

"Tomorrow," Jackie said.

He walked toward the Volvo. Nick walked toward the bus. Between them, the parking lot held the invisible line that ran through the center of Sudbury — the line drawn by Hop Brook and the Dump and thirty years of property values and school budgets and all the quiet machinery that sorted American children into their proper categories.

They'd crossed it without knowing it was there.

They'd cross it again tomorrow, and the day after that, and every day for the next year, until the day they stopped crossing it, which was the day the world finally taught them what the line was for.

But that was later. Today they were twelve years old and they'd found each other and disco was terrible and rock and roll was the only thing that mattered and nothing — not geography, not class, not the quiet architecture of American sorting — nothing could touch what had just started in a middle school cafeteria on a Tuesday in September when the sun caught a belt buckle and threw it across the room like a signal fire.
