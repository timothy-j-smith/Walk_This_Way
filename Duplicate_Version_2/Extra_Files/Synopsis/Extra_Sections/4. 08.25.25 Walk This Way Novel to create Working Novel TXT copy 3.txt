A Novel

Chapter 1: The Sound of Suburban Ghosts
The thing about suburban Boston in 1979 was that it always sounded like it was holding its breath. Nick Johnson would think about this years later, when the memories had crystallized into something harder and more painful than nostalgia. He'd remember how the sounds of Sudbury drifted through the air like musical notes on an invisible staff – the metallic thwack of hockey sticks against pavement, the mechanical groan of garage doors opening to reveal the secret lives of families, the distant collision of rock and roll bleeding from kitchen radios and basement stereos.
It was music, all of it. Even the silence between sounds had rhythm.
On this particular March afternoon, with winter finally loosening its grip on New England and the first hint of spring hiding in the shadows, Nick stood in the Johnson family garage and felt the weight of four years pressing down on him like a physical thing. The concrete floor beneath his feet was the same concrete that had witnessed the birth of something beautiful and its subsequent, inevitable death. The pegboard walls still held the same tools his father had hung there before the accident, before the drinking, before everything went to hell in ways that working-class families understood but never talked about.
But it was the corner where the magic had happened that drew his attention now. The corner where two milk crates had once faced each other like thrones in a kingdom only he and Jackie Marsh had ever seen.
Four years ago, he thought, and the number felt like a curse.
Chapter 2: When the Music Died (The First Time)
Four Years Earlier – 1975
The garage had been different then. Not physically – the same oil stains on the concrete, the same musty smell of old cardboard and motor oil, the same workbench that hadn't seen actual work since Nick's father had given up on fixing things. But it had been alive in a way that Nick, at twelve, hadn't understood was special. At twelve, you think magic is normal. You think the way your voice harmonizes with your best friend's voice is just something that happens, like gravity or the way ice cream melts on hot summer days.
Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh had been inseparable then, two kids who'd found each other in the peculiar way that outcasts do. Nick, the working-class kid with calloused fingers and a guitar his uncle had given him for Christmas. Jackie, the professor's son with a voice that could make angels weep and parents who treated music like a mathematical equation to be solved rather than a language to be spoken.
They'd sit on those milk crates facing each other, and something would happen. Something that Nick, seventeen now and angry at the world, could still feel echoing in his chest when he let himself remember.
"Is this the real life? Is this just fantasy?" Nick had started that day, his twelve-year-old fingers finding the chords to "Bohemian Rhapsody" with the kind of instinctive precision that couldn't be taught.
And Jackie had answered, his voice pure and clear as mountain water: "Caught in a landslide, no escape from reality..."
The garage had changed then. The walls had seemed to expand, creating cathedral-like acoustics that turned their voices into something larger than the sum of their parts. The sunlight streaming through the single window had pulsed with the rhythm of their harmonies, and the tools on the workbench had vibrated in sympathy with frequencies that existed somewhere between the material world and whatever place music comes from.
They'd been good together. Not just good – they'd been transcendent. Two kids from different worlds who'd found a language that belonged to both of them.
"Okay, okay," Nick had said, stopping mid-verse and grinning the way twelve-year-olds grin when they've discovered something adults don't understand. "But 'Walk This Way' is still better."
Jackie had laughed – really laughed, not the polite chuckle his parents had trained into him. "Dude, that's not even close! But fine... you can have second place."
They'd collapsed in giggles, but the magic had lingered. It always lingered, back then. The garage had held their music like a secret, and for two years, they'd been the keepers of something beautiful.
Until the day Jackie's parents had sat him down and explained, in their careful, academic way, that music was a fine hobby but not a suitable career path for a boy of his intelligence and breeding. Until the day Nick's father had died in a construction accident and music had become a luxury the Johnson family couldn't afford. Until the day they'd both realized that the world had expectations for them that didn't include harmonizing in a suburban garage.
The friendship had died slowly, the way important things usually do. Not with drama or confrontation, but with the quiet erosion of time and circumstance. Different high schools, different social circles, different dreams that led in different directions.
But the music – oh, the music had never really died. It had just... changed.

Rock and roll nonsense. Jackie felt something twist in his chest – a memory of sitting in a garage, harmonizing with a boy who understood that music wasn't about respectability or career paths. Something that would change everything.

Chapter 13: The Mysterious Rumours
While the other bands prepared with varying degrees of intensity and skill, the mysterious Rumours from Concord High School remained an enigma. But Rumours? Rumours was something else entirely.
Chapter 14: The Doubt Creeps In

During one late-night practice session in the Marsh family basement, doubt crept in like fog through a cracked window.
"Maybe we should add more guitar solos," suggested Mike Torres, his drumsticks tapping nervously against his leg. "Make it more... rock and roll."
"No," Jackie said, his voice carrying the kind of forced confidence that came from trying to convince himself as much as his bandmates. "We stick to the plan. Technical proficiency will win out over... whatever it is Nick's doing."
But even as he said it, Jackie wondered if he was right. He'd heard rumors about Nick's practices – stories of music so raw and powerful that it made people stop in the parking lot just to listen. Stories of a sound that came from someplace deeper than technique, someplace that Jackie wasn't sure he knew how to access.

Across town, Nick was battling his own demons. The garage felt smaller each night, more cramped, more like a prison than a sanctuary. The weight of expectation – his mother's quiet pride, his father's posthumous dreams, his own desperate need to prove that working-class kids could make it – was becoming almost unbearable.
"Maybe we should clean up the sound," suggested Tommy Russo during a particularly rough practice session. "Make it more... professional."
"No," Nick said, his voice carrying the kind of desperate determination that came from knowing this was his one shot at something bigger. "We play it the way we feel it. If they don't like it, that's their problem."
But even as he said it, Nick wondered if raw emotion would be enough. He'd heard stories about Jackie's practices – reports of vocal harmonies so precise they made people cry, of arrangements so sophisticated they sounded like they belonged on the radio. Stories of a sound that came from years of training and natural talent, things that Nick wasn't sure he could match.

In the space between doubt and determination, between technical perfection and raw emotion, between the dreams of two seventeen-year-old boys and the reality of a world that didn't always reward the best intentions, something was building. Something that would either bring them together or tear them apart forever.
The Battle of the Bands was still a week away, but the real war had already begun.
