But here I am, Maxanne Sartori, seventy-three years old, sitting in the same Studio C where I discovered Aerosmith in 1973, and all I can think about is two kids who never made it onto my playlist.
WBCN dies at midnight. After fifty-six years of being the Rock of Boston, we're flipping to automated Christian contemporary. The equipment around me—turntables I fought to keep when everything went digital, cart machines that hold the DNA of Boston rock, mixing boards that have channeled more raw talent than any studio in America—will be sold for scrap or shipped to museums where teenagers will stare at them like archaeological artifacts.
"Maxanne?" The program director's voice crackles through the intercom. He's twenty-eight, wears sneakers that cost more than my first car, and thinks rock history started with Nirvana. "Four hours left. Make them count."
Make them count. As if four hours could contain what WBCN meant to this city, to rock and roll, to the kids who found their voices through our signal. As if I could compress fifty-six years of discovering music into a neat little package tied with nostalgic ribbon.
