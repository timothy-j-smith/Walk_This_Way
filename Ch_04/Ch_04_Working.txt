Chapter 4: The Geography of Division

Picture Sudbury from above in March 1979, and you'll see America in miniature—a town carved into quarters like a medieval map of the humors, each section believing it represents the real America while the others are somehow less authentic.
Hop Brook runs north to south through the center, named for the hops that fed the old mill before progress declared agriculture inefficient. The brook starts in Ashland, gathers momentum through Sudbury, and dies in the Sudbury River near the Wayside Inn where Henry Ford tried to preserve American authenticity by buying it wholesale. Even our water has a sense of irony.
But Hop Brook is only half the story. Running east to west, bisecting the town at a perfect right angle, sits what everyone simply called "the Dump"—twenty-three acres of American excess where Saturday mornings taught democracy and disposal in equal measure. The official name was Sudbury Sanitary Landfill, but nobody used official names for places that smelled like truth.
These two lines—water and waste—created four distinct Sudburys, each with its own ZIP code anxiety, its own relationship to the American dream, its own soundtrack.
Northeast: The working-class quarter that bordered Lincoln—a town so separate that most Sudbury residents would never set foot there in their lifetime, though it shared the regional high school. Lincoln existed like a ghost town just a football toss away, known to Sudburyites only through the hyphen in "Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School." Row houses and triple-deckers lined streets named after trees that had been cut down to build them—Maple, Oak, Elm. This was where fathers worked construction and overtime, where mothers counted coupons and prayed the car would pass inspection. Kids here learned guitar because instruments were cheaper than therapy, and both were cheaper than expecting college.
Southeast: The rural quarter, where the Cavicchio pig farm sprawled against the landfill in what seemed like cosmic joke but was actually cosmic justice. Three hundred acres of pigs eating garbage, garbage feeding pigs, the circle of life with a Boston accent. The smell when the wind was wrong could peel paint off houses a mile away. Giuseppe Cavicchio had refused to sell his water rights to Henry Ford in 1923, blocking Ford's plan for an auto parts factory. Ford called it "Italian stubbornness." Giuseppe called it "understanding what land is worth."
Southwest: The forest quarter, protected by the Sudbury Valley Trustees and careful zoning that preserved both trees and property values. Colonial homes with original wide-plank floors sat on lots measured in acres rather than feet. This was where professors lived, where mothers ran the PTA with military efficiency, where kids learned piano because musical literacy was assumed, not earned.
Northwest: The money quarter, where the Revolutionary War seemed to have ended just yesterday and on favorable terms. Stone walls older than the Constitution marked boundaries between estates named after dead Indians and living WASPS. These houses had names, not just addresses. The kids here didn't learn instruments—they took lessons, with the assumption that talent could be purchased like everything else worth having.
Between these four quarters ran the transportation lines that proved connection wasn't the same as unity. The Massachusetts Central Railroad ran east to west, carrying freight that never stopped in Sudbury because Sudbury had nothing to export except tax revenue. The Framingham and Lowell Railroad ran north to south, parallel to Hop Brook, carrying commuters to Boston jobs that paid for Sudbury dreams.
Where these lines crossed at South Sudbury station, engineers called it a "diamond crossing"—four tracks meeting at perfect angles, each train required to stop and wait for the other to pass. It was the perfect metaphor for a town where paths crossed but never really connected.
I adjust the microphone stand with the precision I've maintained since my first Boston broadcast, understanding that even equipment positioning matters when you're trying to reach people across all the static that separates them.
By 1979, both railroad lines were failing, killed by highways and the American love affair with individual transportation. The diamond crossing still functioned, but barely, like an old man's heart—still beating but not with conviction.
The Dump operated on a democratic principle that would have made Jefferson proud if he could have held his nose. Every Saturday morning, families from all four quarters lined up in their cars—station wagons from the money quarter, pickup trucks from Lincoln, rusted sedans from the forest edges where professors lived on academic salaries, and the Cavicchios' truck that always smelled like pig and carried twice as much as anyone else's.
At the Dump, postal codes didn't matter. Everyone's garbage stank equally. Everyone's children learned the same lesson about American consumption: we create more waste than we can hide, but burial is always an option.
The kids would sit in the cars while their fathers—always their fathers, this was 1979—would back up to the edge of the pit where bulldozers waited like mechanical vultures. Glass in one area, paper in another, everything else into the pit that would be covered with six inches of dirt and forgotten until the EPA decided forgetting wasn't an option anymore.
I take a sip of bourbon, feeling the warmth that helps me remember details that might otherwise stay buried under forty-five years of accumulated experience.
Between the Dump and the pig farm ran a network of dirt roads that connected to nothing official but everything important. This was where the mini-bikes ruled—Honda 50s and Yamaha 60s, ridden by kids who understood that freedom required small engines and large territories. The trails they carved would outlast the bikes, outlast the kids, outlast everything except the earth's memory of where children once believed they could go anywhere.
These were the boundaries that mattered in March 1979, when two boys who lived less than a mile apart discovered that distance isn't measured in feet but in expectations, opportunities, and the particular American talent for maintaining divisions while preaching unity.
The town center sat like a colonial film set, complete with white churches, a green where sheep once grazed, and the stocks where colonial justice once embarrassed sin into submission. By 1979, the stocks were gone but the impulse remained—just more subtle, more efficient at keeping people in their proper places.
This was the geography that would shape a Battle of the Bands, that would turn a music competition into a class war, that would prove—once again—that in America, even harmony has to fight for its right to exist.
And somewhere in this divided landscape, three kids who'd once made music together were about to remember what they'd forgotten, or forget what they'd remembered, depending on which side of which line they'd learned to call home.
