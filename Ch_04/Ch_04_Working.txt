Chapter 4: The Geography of Division

Picture Sudbury from above in March 1979, and you'll see a town carved into quarters, each section believing it represents the real America while the others are somehow less authentic.
Hop Brook runs north to south through the center, named for the hops that once grew wild along its banks. The brook starts in Ashland, cuts through Sudbury, and empties into the Sudbury River near the Wayside Inn where Henry Ford tried to preserve American authenticity by buying it wholesale.
But Hop Brook was only half the story. Running east to west, at a right angle to the brook, was what everyone called "the Dump"—twenty-three acres of town landfill where, every Saturday, people learned what it meant to throw things away. Its official name was the Sudbury Sanitary Landfill, but nobody used that. Places that smelled that honest needed no formal title.
These two lines—water and waste—created four distinct Sudburys, each with its own ZIP code anxiety, its own relationship to the American dream, its own soundtrack.
**Northeast Quarter:** The working-class section bordered Lincoln—a town most Sudbury residents would never set foot in their entire lives, though they shared a regional high school. Lincoln was less than a mile away but known to Sudburyites only through the hyphen in "Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School." 

In the northeast, streets bore names like Maple, Oak, and Elm—their namesakes long since turned to firewood or mulch, sacrificed for row houses and shingled triple-deckers packed so tight you could hear your neighbor’s cough through the walls. Dads worked jobs that caked their knuckles in drywall dust and cracked their backs by Thursday, rolling in late and leaving earlier. Moms snipped coupons with kitchen scissors and eyed the station wagon like it was a wounded animal; always afraid this would be the year it failed inspection for good. The kids? They picked up battered pawnshop guitars because it was cheaper to strum away your worries than talk to someone with a degree hanging on the wall. No one was saving for tuition here—if you wanted out, you practiced barre chords and waited for your callus to build.

**Southeast Quarter:** Down past the landfill, the Cavicchio pig farm slouched across a hundred acres. Pigs ate garbage, garbage fattened pigs, the circle of life recited in a Somerville accent thick enough to spread on toast. When the wind shifted, the stench could strip paint off the houses as far as Route 20—a sour warning carried on a cold March breeze. 

Giuseppe Cavicchio stood in his muddy boots and stared Henry Ford dead in the eye in 1923, refusing to sell his water rights, damming up the future with a single, calloused handshake withheld. Ford called it "Italian stubbornness," the phrase landing on the air like a hammer on anvil, but Giuseppe just shook his head, mud on his cuffs, and said it was knowing the true weight of land—the way water runs through generations, how roots remember. The pig farm outlasted Ford’s plans. Pigs gorged on trash and rooted in black earth. On certain winds, the reek rolled across Route 20, a sour ghost of ten thousand meals come home to decay.

**Southwest Quarter:** Here the land rose gentle and slow, protected by the Sudbury Valley Trustees and a palisade of laws, zoning lines as invisible as spider silk, just as binding. The trees held their positions, oaks shouldering the sky, white pines knitting shade over colonial houses with floorboards worn satin-smooth by centuries of bare feet. Professors buried in books, mothers running the PTA like a tight-jawed platoon, all calm surface and inward coiled vigilance. Here, music leaked from open windows in the yellowed afternoons—children’s hands moving over piano keys, each note as inevitable as rain pattering the leaves—because in these rooms, literacy, even musical, was a birthright as much as breath.

**Northwest Quarter:** This was money ancient and slow, the kind that seeps into the soil and gathers beneath stone. Boundaries marked by walls crusted in lichen, older than any law, older maybe than the dreams of the men who laid them. Estates named for ghosts: for vanished tribes, for vanished fortunes, for families who outlasted wars and prairie fires and boredom. These houses had names spoken with a hush, as though the sound alone might summon some old grievance or blessing. Here, children didn’t just have music—they were instructed, coaxed, and corrected, scales and sonatas echoing down hallways paneled in oak. Talent wasn’t a gift; it was a possession, pried from the world and polished into currency.
Between these four quarters ran the transportation lines that proved connection wasn't the same as unity. The Massachusetts Central Railroad ran east to west, carrying freight that never stopped in Sudbury because Sudbury had nothing to export except tax revenue. The Framingham and Lowell Railroad ran north to south, parallel to Hop Brook, carrying commuters to Boston jobs that paid for Sudbury dreams.
Where these lines crossed at South Sudbury station, engineers called it a "diamond crossing"—four tracks meeting at perfect angles, each train required to stop and wait for the other to pass. Perfect metaphor for a town where paths crossed but never really connected.

By 1979, both railroad lines that once stitched Sudbury into the fabric of New England were unraveling. The rails—rusted, pitted, flaked with orange oxidation—ran through tangles of scrub and rising sumac, the wooden ties softened by moss and neglect. Freight cars still rumbled by now and then, but their approach was more rumor than thunder, their horns distant and apologetic. The highways—Route 20 with its steady stream of Fords, Chevrolets, and the occasional imported Datsun or VW Rabbit—had claimed the future. The diamond crossing at South Sudbury station, once a proud clatter where east met west and north met south, now shuddered only when necessary, rails groaning as if startled awake from sleep.

Down the road, the Dump—twenty-three acres of exposed earth, gulls swooping and trash fluttering in the wind—operated on a kind of filthy democracy that would have made Jefferson proud if he could have stood the stink. Every Saturday, from early morning, a patient parade of Sudbury’s citizens drove up the dirt lane that curved past low brush and piles of discarded appliances. Station wagons from the northwest quarter, third-row seats packed with newspapers and glass bottles rattling in cardboard boxes. Pickups from the northeast, their beds jittering with rusty bikes, broken lawnmowers, warped paneling, stacked beside faded coolers and leaky garbage bags. From the southwest, Volvo and Buick sedans, spotless on the outside and striped with coffee cups and PTA flyers on the inside, professors trying to pretend their trash was somehow tidier than anyone else’s. And always, there was the Cavicchios’ flatbed truck—green, battered, clouded with old pig stickers and always trailing the heavy, sour musk of swine. It looked loaded for a move; in reality, it was just another week’s worth of everything no one wanted, packed twice as high and tied with dusty ropes.

At the Dump, the usual rules fell away. No one cared about ZIP codes or whether you filled out the census; the only question was whether your trash bag would split before you made the drop. The stench was democratic—banana peels, diapers, last week’s spaghetti, paint cans, and brittle Christmas trees all roasting and moldering together, perfume of America’s excess. Kids of every stripe slumped in the family cars, windows cracked, trying to escape the reek by holding their breath or pressing junior high gym shirts over their faces. Their fathers—always their fathers, in work boots and yard jeans and Red Sox caps—would step out, backs popping, and take quiet pride in lining the bumpers up just right along the muddy cliff’s precarious edge. There, at the precipice, bulldozers crouched like patient metal beasts, caked in a sculpture of clotted dirt and lemon-lime soda cans.

It was a ritual. Glass in one cavernous pile that glittered like a dragon’s hoard with the sunlight slanting through emerald ginger ale bottles and brown Schlitz sleeves. Newspapers—rolled tight and bound with knotted twine—tossed into the paper heap that pulsed with the movement of skittering mice. Everything else—egg cartons, linoleum tiles, old Barbie heads, mildewed encyclopedias—hurled into the wide, gravel-sided pit soon to be buried under six inches of grayish loam by the operator’s final, careful sweep. Forgotten, not gone, sleeping underground until some future EPA investigator decided that memory was mandatory after all.

Between the Dump and the pig farm ran a network of dirt roads that connected to nothing official but everything important. Mini-bike territory—Honda 50s and Yamaha 60s, ridden by kids who understood that freedom required small engines and large territories. The trails they carved would outlast the bikes, outlast the kids, outlast everything except the earth itself.
These were the boundaries that mattered in March 1979. Two boys lived less than a mile apart. Nick Johnson. Jackie Marsh. Close enough to walk to each other's houses. Far enough apart that they might as well have lived in different countries.
The town center sat like a colonial film set, complete with white churches, a green where sheep once grazed, and the stocks where colonial justice once embarrassed sin into submission. By 1979, the stocks were gone but the impulse remained—just more subtle, more efficient at keeping people in their proper places.
This was the geography that would shape a Battle of the Bands, that would turn a music competition into a class war, that would prove—once again—that in America, even harmony has to fight for its right to exist.
Somewhere in this divided landscape, two boys who'd once made music together were about to learn what happens when you try to cross invisible lines—or when you discover that the hardest distance to cross isn't measured in feet, but in expectations.
