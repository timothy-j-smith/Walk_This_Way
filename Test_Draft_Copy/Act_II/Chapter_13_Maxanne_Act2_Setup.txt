CHAPTER 13: MAXANNE - WHEN FRIENDSHIP STARTED DYING
**First Person - Maxanne's Voice, Studio B, 2024**
**Word Count Target:** 1,500-2,000 words

---

Three hours left.

The demo tape sits on the console beside me, its label faded but still legible in the amber booth light: "Demo - Nick/Jackie - un-Authorized - March 1979." Forty-five years of sitting in a filing cabinet, waiting for someone to remember that two kids almost made it. The plastic case is warm from my hands—I keep picking it up, putting it down, picking it up again like a rosary bead worn smooth by decades of unanswered prayers.

The ON AIR sign blinks red. The kid in the engineering booth scrolls through his phone, probably texting his girlfriend that he's stuck babysitting some old lady's final show. He has no idea what failure sounds like when it's been fermenting for forty-five years.

I take a sip of bourbon—just one, because getting drunk would be too easy, and I've never done easy—and lean into the microphone.

"You know what nobody tells you about discovering talent?" I say. "They don't tell you that sometimes you find lightning in a bottle and you still can't make it strike twice. They don't tell you that you can have the ear, the instinct, the track record of breaking bands that go on to fill stadiums, and it still won't matter. Because some stories don't end with record deals and tour buses. Some stories end with two kids who had everything it takes, except the one thing you can't manufacture: the ability to survive America's machinery of division."

I spin my chair to face the wall of albums. Aerosmith's first pressing, before anyone knew who they were. The Cars' demo, before they had a label. Boston's early tracks, before Tom Scholz built his basement studio into something legendary. Every one of these bands had something in common—I played them when nobody else would, and they remembered. Steven Tyler still sends bourbon. Ric Ocasek sent a Christmas card every year until he died. These are the wins you hang your career on, the proof that you know what you're doing.

Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh should be on this wall.

They're not.

"November 14th, 1977," I say, and the date tastes like metal in my mouth, like blood from a bitten tongue. "Dan Johnson died on a construction site in Marlborough. Crane cable snapped, and forty-three years of building things with his hands ended in the time it takes to draw a breath. The Globe gave it three paragraphs on page six, wedged between a car accident on Route 9 and a house fire in Framingham. That's what a working man's death is worth in print—three paragraphs and a misspelled last name."

The engineering kid looks up from his phone. Maybe something in my voice cut through his comfortable distance.

"Dan's son Nick was seventeen. Same age as the century when the first World War started—old enough to fight, too young to understand what you're fighting for. Grace Johnson went back to work the next day because the mortgage doesn't care that your husband's gone. She came home from double shifts at Friendly's smelling like fryer oil and forced cheerfulness, and she never once complained that her son spent grocery money on guitar strings. Some people think poverty is just about not having money. It's about not having the luxury to grieve properly. It's about carrying your dead in silence because nobody's listening anyway."

I pick up the tape again. The weight of it feels wrong—too light for what it represents, like holding a bird's hollow bones and being told this thing used to fly.

"Nick stopped playing guitar after Dan died. Just stopped cold, the way you stop speaking a language when there's nobody left who understands it. Every chord reminded him of his father's calloused hands guiding his fingers, every song became a eulogy he wasn't ready to write. That garage on Dutton Road went silent except for the sound of tools nobody would ever use again gathering dust."

Outside the booth windows, Boston glitters in the October darkness. Luxury condos where dive bars used to breed the next generation of bands who'd change everything. Clean streets where dangerous music used to spill out of basement clubs at 2 AM, making everyone who heard it feel like they'd witnessed something that mattered. The city's been gentrified into obedience, and I've been watching it happen for fifty years like a slow-motion funeral.

"Jackie Marsh tried to help," I continue. "God knows he tried. He called, he stopped by, he offered to do homework together or just sit in that garage and not talk. But grief builds walls faster than friendship can climb them. Nick pushed him away with the kind of viciousness that comes from not knowing how to ask for help, from learning too young that the world doesn't stop breaking your heart just because you ask it nicely."

The words come easier now, like lancing an old wound.

"'I don't need your pity,' Nick told him once. I know because Jackie told me years later, and the way he said it—like he was confessing a sin—made me understand that some sentences never stop echoing. 'I don't need anything from you.' Four words that built a wall higher than Hop Brook ever could."

I can see it playing out in my mind like a movie I've watched too many times: Jackie standing in that garage doorway, his college-prep clothes clean and perfect, his face trying to hide how much it hurt to be rejected by someone he'd once harmonized with so perfectly it made teachers cry. Nick surrounded by his dead father's tools, wearing that rejection like armor because armor was the only thing standing between him and complete collapse.

"Jackie didn't understand—how could he? His father was alive and writing papers about the working class without ever having to be working class. His family was intact, his future mapped out in ways that included safety nets and second chances. He wanted to help, but wanting isn't the same as knowing how. And Nick couldn't explain that accepting help from Jackie felt like admitting the gap between them was real, was permanent, was the kind of chasm that swallows friendships whole."

The silence in the booth feels heavy. I let it breathe, the way I learned decades ago that sometimes dead air says more than any song could.

"By the spring of 1978, they'd stopped even pretending. They'd see each other in the hallways at Lincoln-Sudbury, exchange the kind of nod you give strangers, then disappear back into their separate worlds. The cafeteria became a map of everything wrong with America—kids divided by property values and zip codes, by whose parents had to work double shifts and whose parents had summer houses. Nick sat with the working-class kids who knew what it meant to be one paycheck from disaster. Jackie sat with the college-bound crowd who treated SAT scores like they were measuring your worth as a human being."

I look at the clock. Three hours and twelve minutes left. Not enough time to undo what happened, but enough time to bear witness.

"The music that once connected them became the language of their separation. Nick threw himself into Aerosmith—raw, gritty, working-class rock that felt like survival. Jackie doubled down on Queen—theatrical, technically perfect, the sound of someone trying to prove that excellence could transcend origin. They were both right. They were both wrong. Music was supposed to be the bridge, but they'd turned it into ammunition."

The engineering kid is watching me now, phone forgotten. Maybe he's starting to understand that this isn't nostalgia—it's autopsy.

"I've spent forty-five years thinking about Nick and Jackie," I say, and my voice cracks just slightly on Jackie's name. "Thinking about what I could have done differently, what I should have seen coming, whether there was a moment I could have intervened and changed the trajectory. I discovered Aerosmith in 1973. I broke The Cars when they were still playing dive bars in Cambridge. I gave Boston their first airtime when everyone else thought they were too polished, too perfect, too different. I had the touch, the instinct, the reputation for picking winners."

I set the tape down carefully, precisely, like it might shatter.

"But I couldn't save Nick and Jackie. I couldn't make them see what I saw—that together, they had something rarer than talent, rarer than technical skill, rarer than the kind of raw authenticity that music industry people are always chasing. They had balance. They had contrast that created harmony instead of discord. They had the yin-yang chemistry that makes legends, the kind of creative tension that produces art instead of just music."

The booth feels smaller now, like the walls are closing in with the weight of everything I'm about to say.

"And here's what I've learned in the forty-five years since I met them: Division always wins. Always. It doesn't matter how talented you are, how much potential you have, how perfectly your voices blend when you sing together. America's machinery of separation is stronger than any individual connection. We build our suburbs to keep the classes apart. We design our school systems to ensure kids stay in their proper lanes. We create a thousand invisible barriers and then act surprised when people can't cross them."

Outside, a siren wails past on Boylston Street. Emergency on its way to someone's crisis, the sound swallowed by the city's indifferent hum.

"This station closes in three hours," I say. "WBCN, The Rock of Boston, fifty-six years of discovering bands and breaking artists and believing that music could matter more than marketing budgets. We're being shut down so this frequency can be repurposed for automated Christian contemporary, because apparently Jesus sells better than rock and roll. That's the same machinery that pulled Nick and Jackie apart—the force that says connection doesn't matter as much as profit, that harmony loses to division every single time, that the things worth preserving are exactly the things that get erased first."

I pick up the tape one more time. Hold it up to the booth light like I'm examining an X-ray, looking for the fracture point, the moment where everything went wrong.

"But before this station goes dark forever, before I walk out of this booth for the last time and let the corporate suits flip the switch on fifty-six years of music history, I'm going to tell you what happened to Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh. I'm going to tell you about the Battle of the Bands that became a war. I'm going to play you this tape—the only recording that exists of them playing together, the unauthorized demo they cut five days after everything fell apart, the proof that harmony was still possible even after America convinced them they were enemies."

The ON AIR sign pulses like a heartbeat that's running out of time.

"But first, you need to understand how completely their friendship died. You need to know about a wooden bridge over Hop Brook in the fall of 1978, and the day two boys who once made music that could break your heart learned that some divisions can't be bridged. You need to hear about the moment when childhood ended and America's class war claimed two more casualties."

I lean back in my chair. The leather creaks like old bones settling.

"Because the thing about losing something beautiful is this: You have to remember exactly what it looked like before it was gone. Otherwise, you'll forget it was ever possible in the first place. And forgetting—that's the real tragedy. That's what lets division win, over and over, generation after generation. We forget that harmony existed. We forget that different voices once blended into something bigger than themselves. We forget, and then we're doomed to repeat the same damn story until nobody remembers there was ever another way to live."

Three hours and seven minutes left. Just enough time to remember everything.

"The bridge over Hop Brook," I say, and the words taste like goodbye. "That's where we're going next. That's where childhood ended for Nick Johnson and Jackie Marsh, and where I learned that sometimes the most talented people in the room still lose. Not because they weren't good enough—but because America's machinery of division is stronger than any two kids with guitars could ever be."

The clock ticks. The city glitters. The station dies in slow motion.

And all I can do is tell the truth before the lights go out.
