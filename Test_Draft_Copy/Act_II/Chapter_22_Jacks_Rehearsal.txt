CHAPTER 22: JACKIE'S REHEARSAL
**Third Person - May 9, 1979 (Day Before the Battle)**
**Word Count Target:** 1,200-1,500 words

---

The Marsh basement had been converted into a practice space years ago—foam panels on the walls, good microphones on stands, a drum kit in the corner. Professional-grade equipment but not quite studio quality. Jackie's band, Crown Jewels, rehearsed there most afternoons.

Jackie stood at the upright piano against the far wall, running through "Bohemian Rhapsody" alone. His fingers moved across the keys with the precision of someone who'd been practicing since before memory. But tonight, the day before the Battle, every note felt heavier than it should.

He'd heard about Grace Johnson's speech at Friendly's. Everyone in Sudbury had heard about it.

The knowledge sat in his chest like a stone.

---

Classical was what Jackie was supposed to love. What his father had invested in since Jackie was five—lessons, recitals, competitions, the Steinway upstairs that cost more than most people's cars.

But rock and roll was what he breathed.

When he played Chopin, his father nodded. When he played Queen, his father checked his watch.

Jackie moved into the operatic section of "Bohemian Rhapsody," letting Mercury's complexity fill the basement. This was where he disappeared. Where the weight lifted. Where he wasn't performing for anyone's scorecard.

The side door opened. Dr. Richard Marsh descended the stairs, carrying a leather notebook he used for everything—patient notes, lecture prep, observations about his children's progress.

"Don't stop," his father said, settling into the chair near the drum kit.

Jackie kept playing. Felt his father's eyes on his hands, counting, measuring, grading.

---

Elizabeth arrived at six-thirty, violin case in hand. Thirteen years old and already better than adults. It came easy to her—the kind of natural talent that made teachers shake their heads in wonder. She practiced, sure, but she didn't have to suffer for it.

Arthur "Ace" Callahan came next, all athletic precision and disciplined rhythm. Then Max Wesslen with his bass, tall and quiet, handling his instrument with the confidence that came from owning gear other musicians envied.

Crown Jewels. Jackie's band. His brothers in excellence and expectation.

They ran through their set. The Chopin étude Jackie had arranged for full band—classical training on display. "Bohemian Rhapsody." "We Will Rock You." Every harmony locked in. Every chord progression mathematically precise. The kind of technical perfection that made other bands sound amateur.

Dr. Marsh watched from his chair, making occasional notes in his leather book.

When they finished, he stood. Smiled. "The Chopin was excellent, Jackie. Elizabeth, your intonation in the third movement—perfect."

He walked to the piano, placed his hand on Jackie's shoulder. "And the Queen pieces. You know, when you play like that—controlled, precise, every note where it should be—you remind me of Glenn Gould. That kind of technical mastery." He paused, looked at his son with what Jackie knew was meant to be pride. "Freddie Mercury is talented, but he's undisciplined. You've taken his chaos and made it... civilized."

Dr. Marsh squeezed Jackie's shoulder once, then headed for the stairs. "Keep this up, and Friday will take care of itself."

The door closed.

Jackie sat at the piano, his father's words settling over him like a lead blanket.

*Civilized.*

---

Ace and Max packed up their gear, said their goodbyes, headed home. Elizabeth stayed. Set her violin in its case, came over to the piano.

"You okay?" she asked.

"Fine."

"Jack."

He looked at his little sister. Thirteen years old and already free in ways he'd never been. She'd checked the box so effortlessly that their father had stopped needing her to keep proving it. She practiced because she wanted to, not because she had to. 

"You know he doesn't ask me to practice anymore, right?" Elizabeth said quietly. "I already did my job." She sat on the piano bench beside him. "But you? You keep trying to prove something that was already proven."

Jackie's hands rested on the keys. "What if it wasn't proven? What if I'm just... good enough?"

"You were never 'just good enough.' That's not why you're still trying." Elizabeth looked at him with the clarity only little sisters have. "You're trying to make him hear the music. But he doesn't hear music, Jack. He counts notes like credits on a transcript. Doesn't matter what you learned, just what's on paper."

She stood, picked up her violin case. "I'm going upstairs. You should probably sleep. Tomorrow's going to be long."

After she left, Jackie sat alone in the perfect basement studio. Surrounded by equipment his father had bought, piece by piece, year by year—each purchase a down payment on Jackie's future.

He played "Bohemian Rhapsody" again. This time without thinking about precision or control or making chaos civilized. He played it the way Mercury had written it—messy, operatic, raw, impossible, beautiful.

And for three minutes, he could breathe. He smiled. Don't hold back, he thought. Just this once, don't hold back.

---

But then the song ended, and the weight came back.

Tomorrow night wasn't about music. It was about being the plus on his father's internal scorecard, not the minus.

Jackie looked at the Chopin sheet music on the piano's music stand. Classical earned his father's approval. Rock earned his breath.

He couldn't have both.

Nick Johnson was playing in a garage his dead father had built, with a $25 guitar and borrowed equipment. Nick was playing for his mother, for his memory, for the last thread connecting him to what he'd lost.

Jackie was playing to be enough. And realizing he never would be.

The Battle was eighteen hours away.

Jackie closed the piano lid and turned off the basement lights. Upstairs, his father would be in his study, reviewing tomorrow's lecture notes, moving to the next item on his list of things to make right. His mother would be reading in the living room, present but distant. Elizabeth would be in her room, already asleep, already free.

And Jackie would lie awake, wondering what it felt like to play music the way he heard it—messy, raw, alive.

The only person who'd ever heard it that way was Nick. They'd been good together. Great together. Perfect harmony before anyone told them harmony wasn't enough.

Tomorrow night, Jackie had a choice to make.

He could play it safe. Execute with technical mastery. Make chaos civilized. Win the way his father expected him to win—proof that proper preparation produced superior results. That would earn approval. That would make him the plus.

Or he could do what his father didn't want. Lay it on the line. Play rock the way it was meant to be played—dangerous, raw, uncontrolled. The way he played it when no one was watching. Risk everything for three minutes of breathing.

One path led to his father's world. The other led... where? He didn't know. That was the terrifying part.

He wished he could turn back the clock. Middle school, when everyone was on the same level. Before pretension and cliques and quadrants. Before Rampers, Rats, Regs, and Rollers. Before he had to choose between breathing and approval.

He wasn't really a Ramper. He knew that. His father was climbing toward something they'd never quite reach. But Jackie had been drafted into the climb anyway.

The bridge was burned. The friendship was dead. But tomorrow night, Jackie Marsh would decide who he was going to be.

And somewhere in a garage across town, Nick was probably lying awake too, already knowing his answer.

The Battle was eighteen hours away.
