CHAPTER 24: THE WITNESS

---

May 9th, 1979. St. Michael's Church, Concord.

Patty Lally sat alone in the empty sanctuary, a cassette tape in her hands. Camp Winaukee, Summer 1975, written in her fourteen-year-old handwriting on the label. Four years old. Might as well be four decades.

She'd been carrying this tape in her backpack for three weeks, ever since she'd heard about the Battle of the Bands. Lincoln-Sudbury Regional, Friday night, WBCN sponsorship, the whole town choosing sides. Working-class Aerosmith versus upper-class Queen. Nick Johnson versus Jackie Marsh.

The boys who used to be brothers.

She pressed play. The tape hissed, then: three voices harmonizing on "Your Song," Elton John filtered through something that couldn't be taught. Nick's guitar, raw and honest. Jackie's piano, precise and pure. Her voice, finding the melody that connected them.

They'd recorded this on the last night of camp, Mr. Freedman's boom box balanced on a picnic table by the lake. They'd made promises that night. Stay in touch. Form a band. Change the world.

She'd kept her half. Wrote Nick in September. Wrote Jackie in October. Wrote them both every month through freshman year.

Neither one ever wrote back.

Patty stopped the tape. Four years of silence, and now they were going to war. On stage. In front of the whole town. Making the division permanent.

She wasn't going to save them. You can't save people who've already chosen.

But she could make them see the cost.

---

Dutton Road, Sudbury. Northeast quadrant. The address was still in her camp notebook.

The garage door was open. Nick Johnson stood alone with his guitar, playing "Dream On" to an audience of Dan Johnson's tools. The solo his father taught him, note-perfect, heartbreaking.

Patty watched from the driveway. He'd gotten taller, shoulders broader from construction work he'd taken on after his father died. But his hands on the guitar—those were the same hands from camp. Before they'd learned to grip so tight.

"You're playing like you're trying to prove something," she said.

Nick's fingers stopped. He turned. Stared.

"Patty?"

"At camp, you played like you had nothing to prove." She walked into the garage, stopped ten feet away. "That's the difference between real and desperate."

Nick's face went through several expressions—surprise, confusion, something that might have been shame. "What are you doing here?"

"I came to see what you've become."

"I'm fine. Busy. Senior year, you know—"

"I wrote you letters." Flat. No emotion. Just fact. "Seventeen letters over two years. You never wrote back."

Nick set the guitar down. "Patty, it's not—look, things got complicated. Jackie's dad, the class stuff at school, Dan dying—"

"I know about your father. I'm sorry." She meant it. "But that's not why you stopped writing. You stopped because writing to me meant admitting you were still the person from camp. And you couldn't be that person anymore. Not in Sudbury."

"You don't understand what it's like here—"

"No. I understand exactly." She pulled out the cassette tape. "I understand that you and Jackie were brothers. I understand that you made something real. And I understand that you let them turn you into symbols. Working-class hero versus privileged perfectionist. Aerosmith versus Queen. The garage versus the mansion."

She held up the tape. "This is what you were. Want to hear it?"

Nick looked at the tape like it was evidence at a crime scene. "I don't—"

"Tomorrow night, I'm entering the Battle." She put the tape back in her pocket. "Not to fix you. Not to make you friends again. To show everyone what you threw away. What brotherhood looked like before Sudbury divided it into territories."

"Patty—"

"I'm not asking for anything, Nick. I'm telling you. Tomorrow night, when you're on that stage proving you're more authentic than Jackie, more real than his classical training, more working-class than his privilege—I want you to remember this tape. Remember that you used to play music because it made you feel something. Not because it proved something."

She turned to leave. Stopped at the garage door.

"Your father was right. Music doesn't care where you come from. But you forgot the second half of that lesson. Music also doesn't care what you're trying to prove. It just cares if you're telling the truth."

She walked away. Didn't look back. Behind her, the garage stayed silent.

The Marsh house looked like something from a catalog. Colonial architecture, circular driveway, lawn maintained by people who got paid to care about grass ((Scalpel we used thissimilar line earlier in the novel when we introduced the MArsh family, chapter 02)).

Patty rang the doorbell.

A girl answered. Thirteen, maybe fourteen. Dark hair, violin case leaning against the wall behind her.

"I'm looking for Jackie," Patty said.

The girl studied her. "You're Patty Lally."

"How do you—"

"Jackie talked about you. After camp. For months." The girl stepped aside. "I'm Elizabeth. His sister. He's in the basement."

Patty followed her inside. The house smelled like furniture polish and expectations.

"He doesn't talk about you anymore," Elizabeth said, leading her down carpeted stairs. "He doesn't talk about Nick either. It's like that summer never happened."

"I know."

"But it did happen, right? They really were friends?"

"Yes."

Elizabeth stopped at the basement door. "Good. I wanted to make sure I wasn't imagining it. That he used to be different."

She opened the door. Left Patty alone at the top of the stairs.

The basement had been converted into a practice studio. Soundproofing, professional equipment, a Steinway baby grand that cost more than Grace Johnson's car. Jackie sat at the piano, playing "Bohemian Rhapsody" with technical perfection and zero breath.

Patty descended. Her footsteps silent on the carpeted stairs.

Jackie's hands stopped when he saw her reflection in the piano's polished surface.

"Hello, Jackie."

He turned slowly. Pale. "Patty? What—how did you get in?"

"Elizabeth let me in. She's worried about you."

"I'm fine—"

"You're not playing music. You're executing it." She crossed to the piano, pulled out the cassette tape. "Remember this?"

Jackie stared at the tape. Something crossed his face—recognition, loss, fear.

"Camp Winaukee," Patty said. "Final night. The three of us by the lake. You played piano like you were breathing. Not performing. Not proving. Just breathing."

"That was a long time ago—"

"Four years. I wrote you letters. You never wrote back."

Jackie stood. "Patty, I'm sorry. I was—things got busy. My father wanted me to focus on college prep, classical competitions—"

"Your father counts notes like credits on a transcript." She held his eyes. "You used to make music. Now you're just collecting approval. Which one of you am I talking to—you or him ((we used simillar line earlier about the Dad counting like ... Dont need it"

"You're right. I don't know what it's like in your house." She gestured at the studio. "But I know what it's like to make music that matters. We did it together. Remember?"

She set the tape on the piano. "Tomorrow night, I'm entering the Battle. I'll play this for everyone. Original songs, the way we said we would. No covers.
