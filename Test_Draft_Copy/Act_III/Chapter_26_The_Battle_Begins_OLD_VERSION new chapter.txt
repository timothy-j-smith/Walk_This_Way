# CHAPTER 26: THE BATTLE BEGINS

Grace Johnson ironed her work uniform in the bathroom at Friendly's, the steam from the iron filling the small space like confession. She'd taken off early from her shift to see her son play tonight, and she wanted to look nice. The uniform was the same one she'd worn to serve burgers and fries for the past two years, since Dan died. Same uniform, different occasion. Same dress she saved for church and funerals and events where Nick was performing.

The iron made a hissing sound against the fabric. Grace pressed it harder, watching the wrinkles disappear. In twenty minutes, she'd be in the Lincoln-Sudbury auditorium, sitting in the third row, watching her seventeen-year-old son try to prove something she wasn't sure he needed to prove.

The guitar had become an obsession after Dan died. Nick practiced in the garage until two in the morning, until Grace came out in her bathrobe and begged him to stop. The neighbors complained. She was tired. Grief was supposed to get easier, not harder. But Nick kept playing because it was the only thing that made the silence bearable, the only thing that made him feel connected to his father in a way that words couldn't touch.

Grace folded the ironed uniform and put it in her purse. She'd change in the car. She couldn't afford to go home first, not with the gas prices these days and the mortgage payment due next week.

Outside, the evening was already settling into itself. May 15th, 1979. Tuesday night. The Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School auditorium was filling up with kids who'd been told this mattered. The Battle of the Bands. WBCN sponsorship. Prize money and a recording session for the winners.

Nick's band, Walking the Line, would play fifth. Four bands before them. Grace drove the ten minutes from Friendly's to the high school, her work uniform pressed and folded in her purse. She'd change in the car, check her reflection in the rearview mirror, smooth down the fabric like it mattered how she looked.

At the high school, the parking lot was already full. Grace found a spot in the back and changed in her car, the windows steamed up. She smoothed down the uniform—her best dress, the one she saved for church and funerals and events where Nick was performing.

Inside the auditorium, eight hundred seventeen-year-olds filled the seats. The Rampers sat in front because that's where they always sat. The Rats spread along the back and aisles because that's where they always sat. The Regs filled the middle because that's where they always sat. The Rollers were scattered, like they'd forgotten there was supposed to be a system.

Grace found her seat in the third row, on the left. She'd be able to see Nick clearly from there. Her purse sat in her lap, her hands gripping it like she was holding onto something. She was. She was holding onto the hope that tonight would prove Dan's death meant something, that Nick's obsession wasn't just grief turned inward, that maybe music could be the bridge instead of the wall.

Behind the stage curtain, Nick Johnson stood in the narrow space, watching the crowd through the gap where the velvet didn't quite meet the floor. His guitar hung from a strap his father had bought him three years earlier. The strap was worn smooth where Nick's thumb rested when he wasn't playing. A piece of leather gets soft and dark after years of being touched.

"Dream On" came out of the PA speakers between acts. Aerosmith. Nick closed his eyes and heard his father's voice saying *Don't rush the notes, let them breathe*. That was eighteen months ago, before the cable snapped and four steel I-beams came down on a construction trailer in Back Bay. Before Nick stopped being a kid who played guitar for fun and started being a kid who played guitar because it was the only thing that made the silence bearable.

The song ended. Nick opened his eyes. Two more bands before Walking the Line went on.

Through the curtain gap, he saw his mother in the third row. Grace looked small and tired and proud all at once, like she'd been carrying something heavy for a long time and tonight was the first night she might get to put it down.

Dr. Richard Marsh sat three rows behind her. Jackie's father. He taught psychology at Boston University, which meant he wrote papers about why people did what they did. He'd written papers about adolescent development, about the psychology of achievement, about the relationship between parental expectations and student performance.

He'd never written a paper about his own son, but Jackie suspected he'd thought about it.

Across the backstage area, separated by extension cords and amplifiers, Jackie Marsh stood at the school's Steinway piano, running his fingers across the keys without making any sound. The Crown Jewels would go on sixth, after Walking the Line. Jackie had time to run through the parts that still felt uncertain. Not the notes themselves—those were perfect. He'd played "Bohemian Rhapsody" so many times he could do it with his eyes closed. The uncertainty was in the space between the notes, the places where he was supposed to feel something and instead felt nothing at all.

"Jackie."

Dr. Marsh stood in the doorway. "Everything all right?"

"Fine," Jackie said. "Just nervous."

"Good. Nerves mean you're taking it seriously." Dr. Marsh adjusted his tie. "Remember, this isn't just about music. It's about showing people who you are."

Jackie nodded. That was the problem. He wasn't sure who he was when he wasn't trying to be who everyone else needed him to be.

The guitar had been Nick's thing. The piano was supposed to be Jackie's thing. But somewhere along the way, the piano had stopped being about what Jackie wanted and started being about what Jackie thought everyone else wanted. Classical earned his father's approval. Rock earned his breath. He couldn't have both.

"You'll do fine," Dr. Marsh said. "You always do."

Jackie watched his father walk back toward the auditorium. The Crown Jewels would play Queen songs, and everyone would see exactly who Jackie Marsh was supposed to be—the kid with perfect technique, the kid who made difficult things look easy, the kid whose father could afford to care about things beyond making rent.

But the person he was supposed to be wasn't the person he was, and that was the problem. That had always been the problem.

Backstage, Nick heard the crowd react to whatever band was playing now. Two more acts before Walking the Line. In a few minutes, he'd be standing on that stage with Dan's solo running through his head and eighteen months of grief trying to find its way out through his fingers.

"Walking the Line!" someone called from the stage. "You're up!"

Nick looked at Stephen, at Mark, at David. They were just kids. But they were his kids, his band, and for the next fifteen minutes, they were all he had.

He took a deep breath and walked toward the curtain, toward the stage, toward whatever happened next.

Behind him, Jackie watched Nick disappear through the curtain. The Crown Jewels would go on after Walking the Line. Jackie would play perfectly, because that's what he did. He'd make his father proud and his mother smile and everyone in the crowd would see exactly who Jackie Marsh was supposed to be.

But the person he was supposed to be wasn't the person he was, and that was the problem. That had always been the problem.

The crowd roared as Walking the Line took the stage. Jackie pressed his fingers against the piano keys and tried to remember what music had felt like when it was just about the music, when it wasn't about expectations or fathers or proving anything to anyone.

But that had been a long time ago. Before high school. Before everything got complicated.

Before he'd needed Nick for all the wrong reasons.
